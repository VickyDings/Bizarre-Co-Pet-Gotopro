The one-sentence version

2 What Exactly Is a Bearded Dragon?

An Australian desert lizard that happens to be remarkably tolerant of people.

The pet trade's bearded dragon is almost always Pogona vitticeps, the central or inland bearded dragon, native to the arid woodland and scrub of inland Australia. Australia banned the export of native wildlife decades ago, so every bearded dragon you can buy descends from animals that left before that — they are among the most thoroughly captive-bred reptiles in the hobby.

What makes them the default beginner lizard is temperament. They are diurnal, so they are awake when you are. They are large enough to handle without feeling fragile. And they are genuinely unbothered by people in a way most reptiles are not. What makes them commonly mis-kept is that they come from an environment with brutally strong sun, and reproducing that indoors costs real money and needs equipment most starter kits don't include.

Reading their body language

Bearded dragons communicate visibly, which is half the fun of keeping one.

  • Beard darkening / puffing. The spiny throat pouch flares and turns black. It means stress, threat display, territorial warning, or sometimes just discomfort. An occasional dark beard is normal; a persistently black beard means something is wrong.
  • Head bobbing. Fast, assertive bobbing is dominance or breeding display, usually from males. Slow bobbing can be an acknowledgement.
  • Arm waving. A slow circular wave of one front leg. It is a submission or "I see you" signal, most common in juveniles and females. Completely normal and rather charming.
  • Gaping. Sitting with the mouth open while basking is normal thermoregulation — it is how they shed excess heat. Gaping away from the basking spot, or with laboured breathing, is not.
  • Glass surfing. Scrabbling at the walls. Usually means the enclosure is too small, too bare, too bright, or the dragon can see its reflection.
  • Digging. Normal, especially in gravid females looking to lay. Provide a dig box.
  • Brumation. A hibernation-like slowdown in autumn and winter — sleeping for days, refusing food, hiding in the cool end. Normal in adults, covered in §8.

3 Legality & Where to Buy

Legal almost everywhere in the US — with one absolute exception.

Bearded dragons are banned in Hawaii, with no permit available

Hawaii prohibits bearded dragons outright to protect native species that have no defence against introduced large lizards. Unlike some restricted animals there is no permit route — not for pets, not for breeders. Penalties reach $200,000 and up to three years in prison. The state does run an amnesty programme allowing illegal animals to be surrendered without prosecution. Checked August 2026.

Everywhere else in the United States, bearded dragons are legal with no permit. The UK, most of Europe and Canada also allow them freely. Australia is the interesting one: they are native there, so keeping one requires a state wildlife licence and animals must come from licensed Australian breeders — you cannot import.

City and county rules occasionally add restrictions on reptile keeping, and rented accommodation frequently does. Check both before you buy.

Buying from a good source

Bearded dragons are bred in enormous numbers, and quality varies more than price does. A reputable breeder is usually cheaper than a chain store and will have raised the animal under proper UVB from day one — which matters, because early MBD is often irreversible.

Green flags

  • Alert, bright-eyed, and grips firmly when held
  • Rounded tail base and full limbs — no visible hip or rib bones
  • Straight legs, straight spine, firm jaw with no swelling
  • Breeder can tell you the hatch date, the UVB brand and the feeding schedule
  • Housed individually, not piled in together
  • All toes and a complete tail tip
  • Will happily accept an insect while you watch

Red flags

  • Sold with a compact coil UVB bulb or no UVB at all
  • Kept on calci-sand or crushed walnut shell
  • Sunken eyes, wrinkled skin, or a thin, pointed tail base
  • Rubbery or swollen lower jaw, bowed legs, tremors
  • Sticky or bubbling mouth, or audible breathing
  • Sold as a "starter kit" in a 20-gallon tank
  • Silkbacks, or any animal under 6 weeks old
Build the enclosure first and run it for a week

Unlike an aquarium there is no cycling to wait for, but you do need to prove your temperatures. Set everything up, run the full day cycle for several days, and check the basking surface with an infrared temp gun until it sits reliably in range. Adjusting a thermostat with a stressed new dragon already in the enclosure is a bad time to learn your bulb is wrong.

4 Enclosure & Setup

Floor space, a proper thermal gradient, and a surface they can't swallow.

1

Basking lamp on a dimming thermostat

A halogen flood in a ceramic dome, positioned over the raised basking platform at the hot end. The thermostat holds the surface at 100–105°F instead of letting it drift with room temperature.

2

Linear T5 HO UVB tube, mounted inside

Spanning roughly two thirds of the enclosure length, starting at the hot end. Mounted inside — a mesh screen absorbs a third or more of the output.

3

Raised basking platform

Rock, slate or sturdy branch that puts the dragon's back at the right distance from both lamps. Wide enough for the whole animal, not just the front half.

4

Cool-end hide

Somewhere dark to retreat to at the far end. A dragon with no shade cannot escape the heat and will not thermoregulate properly.

5

Solid, swallowable-proof substrate

Slate tile, sealed timber or a properly built bioactive soil mix. Not calci-sand, not crushed walnut, not reptile carpet.

6

Shallow food and water dishes

A salad dish that stays in permanently, and a shallow water dish. They rarely drink from standing water, but it costs nothing to offer.

7

Thermostat probe at basking height

Secured next to the basking surface, not on the cool floor. A probe in the wrong place is how an enclosure ends up 15 degrees off.

Enclosure size

The floor for an adult is 4 × 2 × 2 ft (120 × 60 × 60 cm) — about 120 gallons. That is a minimum, not a target: 5 or 6 feet of length is meaningfully better, because a longer enclosure produces a better thermal gradient, and a good gradient is the whole point.

You can raise a hatchling in the adult enclosure from day one, which saves buying twice. Clutter it heavily with hides, branches and foliage so a small dragon can cross it feeling covered, and hand-feed at first so it doesn't have to hunt across four feet of open ground.

The starter kit problem

Bearded dragon "starter kits" almost universally include a 20–40 gallon tank, a coil UVB bulb and a heat mat. All three are wrong: the tank is a quarter of the space needed, coil bulbs produce far too little usable UVB, and belly heat does nothing for a lizard that basks with its back. Budget for the real setup or wait until you can.

Furnishing it

  • Two hides minimum — one at the warm end, one at the cool end, so they never have to choose between temperature and security.
  • Climbing branches. Bearded dragons are semi-arboreal and will use height. Wedge branches securely; a falling dragon can break bones.
  • A basking platform that puts them at the correct distance from the lamps, and is stable under a 500-gram animal.
  • Artificial or safe live plants for cover and to break up sightlines — less glass surfing.
  • A dig box of slightly damp play sand and soil, especially for females.
  • No heat rocks. They cause contact burns and there is no safe version.

5 Heat, Light & UVB

This is the section that decides whether your dragon stays healthy. Read it twice.

Bearded dragons come from a place with ferocious sun. They digest food using external heat, and they build bone using UVB radiation to synthesise vitamin D3. Get either wrong and you get the same two outcomes: food that sits undigested, and metabolic bone disease. Neither is subtle once it arrives, and MBD is often permanent.

Stick-on dial thermometers are useless

The analogue dials sold with starter kits are frequently off by 10–20 degrees and only read the air next to the glass anyway. You need an infrared temp gun for the basking surface and digital probes for ambient air at each end. Together they cost about $25 and they are the difference between guessing and knowing.

Heating, the right way

  • A white halogen flood bulb in a ceramic dome is the best basking source. Halogen produces the broad-spectrum, high-intensity heat that mimics sunlight, and it lights the enclosure at the same time.
  • Run it through a dimming thermostat with the probe at basking height. Without one, your basking temperature changes every time the room does.
  • Raise or lower the basking platform to fine-tune, rather than constantly swapping bulb wattages.
  • No coloured night bulbs. Red and blue "night" lamps disturb sleep. Bearded dragons need full darkness, and a drop to room temperature is good for them.
  • Only add night heat if your room genuinely drops below about 60°F, and then use a ceramic heat emitter or a deep heat projector on a thermostat — something that emits no light.
  • Never a heat mat as the primary source. Bearded dragons absorb heat through their backs and do not reliably sense belly heat, which is how mat burns happen.

UVB — the part most people get wrong

UVB drives vitamin D3 synthesis, which drives calcium absorption. Without it, a dragon can eat all the calcium in the world and still develop metabolic bone disease. There is no supplement that fully replaces good UVB.

A UVB tube that is 14 months old still lights up and still looks identical, but may be producing a fraction of its original UVB. Take a marker, write the install date on the end of the tube, and set a calendar reminder for 12 months out. It is the single cheapest thing you can do to prevent MBD.

6 Diet & Feeding

The ratio flips as they grow. A dragon fed like a baby for life gets fat and sick.

Bearded dragons are omnivores, and the single most-missed fact in the hobby is that their diet inverts with age. Hatchlings and juveniles are growing fast and eat mostly insects. Adults are not growing at all, and an adult still eating a juvenile's insect load becomes obese and develops fatty liver disease. By 18 months the salad should be the main meal.

The eye-spacing rule

Never feed an insect wider than the space between your dragon's eyes. Anything bigger is a choking and impaction risk, and in young dragons oversized prey has been linked to hind-leg paralysis from pressure on the spine.

Greens and vegetables

The salad should be roughly 80–90% leafy greens, with the rest other vegetables and a very little fruit. Chop everything small and offer it fresh each morning.

  • Dandelion greens

  • Watercress
  • Butternut and acorn squash

Supplements

Unlike axolotls, bearded dragons genuinely need dusting. Insects are naturally low in calcium and high in phosphorus, which is exactly backwards from what a lizard needs.

More is not better with D3 and vitamin A

Both are fat-soluble and accumulate in the body. Over-supplementing causes real harm — and with good UVB, dragons make their own D3. Follow a schedule rather than dusting everything with everything, and if your UVB is genuinely dialled in with a measured UVI of 4–6, you can use the lower end of the D3 range.

Water

Provide a shallow dish, but don't expect much use of it — bearded dragons get most of their moisture from food and often don't recognise still water. Better hydration comes from a fresh daily salad, the occasional hornworm, and a lukewarm 10–15 minute bath once a week or so. Misting the salad works too. Signs of dehydration are wrinkled skin, sunken eyes, and skin that stays tented when gently pinched.

7 Health: Signs, Problems & First Aid

Most bearded dragon illness is husbandry failure wearing a medical name.

A healthy dragon

  • Alert, head up, tracking movement across the room
  • Straight limbs and spine, firm jaw line
  • Grips firmly and holds its own weight when walking
  • Rounded tail base and full, muscular thighs
  • Clear eyes, clean nostrils, clean vent
  • Eats eagerly and passes formed stool regularly
  • Baskets each morning, then explores

Warning signs

  • Swollen, soft or rubbery lower jaw
  • Bowed legs, tremors, or dragging the back legs
  • Persistently black beard away from any threat
  • Sunken eyes and wrinkled, tenting skin
  • Sticky saliva, bubbling at the mouth, open-mouth breathing away from the basking spot
  • Not passing stool for over a week
  • Sudden refusal to eat outside brumation season
  • Yellow or grey crusty patches on the skin

Handling

This is the part bearded dragons are famous for, and they genuinely do tolerate it well. A few rules make it better for both of you.

  • Support the whole body. Scoop from underneath with a flat palm so all four feet are supported. Never grab from above — that is what a predator does.
  • Never pick one up by the tail or legs. Bearded dragons do not drop their tails, and it will not grow back.
  • Start short. Five to ten minutes at a time for a new dragon, building up as it settles. Most adults will happily sit with you far longer.
  • Let a new arrival settle first. Give it a week or two of just food and water before handling sessions start.
  • Don't handle right after a big meal — they need to be on the basking spot digesting.
  • Wash your hands before and after, every time. Reptiles commonly carry Salmonella without showing any sign of it. Keep dragons out of kitchens, and away from food-preparation surfaces. This matters most in households with young children, elderly or immunocompromised people.

Brumation

Somewhere between autumn and late winter, most adult bearded dragons slow right down: sleeping for days at a time, hiding at the cool end, ignoring food, and generally looking worryingly unwell. This is brumation, a reptile version of hibernation, and it is normal.

  • It typically starts after about 10–12 months of age and can last anywhere from a few weeks to several months.
  • Weigh weekly. Steady weight is fine. Continued loss is not brumation, it's illness.
  • Keep offering water and a small salad; keep the UVB and heat on a shortened cycle so they can warm up if they choose.
  • Rule out illness first — particularly parasites and impaction — before assuming a dragon that stops eating is brumating. A faecal test is the cheap way to be sure.
  • Don't force-feed or repeatedly wake a genuinely brumating dragon.

One dragon per enclosure

Bearded dragons are solitary and territorial. Housed together, the dominant animal takes the basking spot and the subordinate slowly develops MBD from lack of UVB exposure — often with no visible fighting at all. Two males will fight outright, and a male and female will breed relentlessly. There is no benefit to the animals in cohabiting them.

11 Beginner Mistakes to Avoid

Every one of these is common, and every one is preventable.

Using a coil UVB bulb

The single most common cause of MBD in pet bearded dragons. A linear T5 HO tube is the only acceptable answer.

Never replacing the UVB tube

It still glows at 24 months and produces almost nothing. Write the date on it and change it yearly.

Mounting UVB above a mesh screen

Mesh absorbs roughly a third of the output. Mount the fixture inside the enclosure.

Trusting a stick-on dial thermometer

They're commonly 10–20 degrees out. Use an infrared gun for surfaces and digital probes for air.

A basking spot that's too cool

Below about 95°F, adults can't digest properly. Undigested food in the gut is how impaction starts.

Loose sand substrate

Calci-sand and walnut shell are swallowed with food and clump in the gut. Tile or bioactive only.

Feeding an adult like a juvenile

Insects every day past 18 months produces an obese dragon with a fatty liver. Salad becomes the main meal.

Prey that's too big

Nothing wider than the space between the eyes — oversized insects have been linked to hind-leg paralysis in young dragons.

Housing two dragons together

The dominant one takes the basking spot and the other quietly develops MBD, often with no visible fighting.

Assuming a dragon that stops eating is brumating

Rule out parasites, impaction and cold first. Weigh weekly — steady weight is brumation, falling weight is illness.

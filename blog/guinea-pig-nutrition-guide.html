The one-sentence version

Unlimited grass hay, a cup of fresh vegetables built around bell pepper, a small measured scoop of plain timothy pellets, a daily source of vitamin C, and clean water. Everything that goes wrong nutritionally — scurvy, bladder stones, dental disease, obesity, gut stasis — traces back to one of those five being wrong.

2 Why Guinea Pigs Are Different

Three pieces of biology explain the entire feeding plan.

1. They cannot make vitamin C

Almost every mammal manufactures its own vitamin C in the liver. Guinea pigs, humans and a handful of other species lost the enzyme that does it — so like us, they must get it from food, every single day. A guinea pig deprived of vitamin C can show signs of scurvy in as little as two weeks. This is the single most important fact in guinea pig nutrition and the one most commonly missed.

2. Their teeth never stop growing

All twenty of a guinea pig's teeth are open-rooted — they grow continuously throughout life. The only thing that keeps them at a workable length is the long, sideways grinding action of chewing coarse fibre. Pellets and soft vegetables don't do it; only hay does. A guinea pig that runs out of hay develops overgrown molars, sharp spurs that cut the tongue and cheek, and eventually can't eat at all. Dental correction under anaesthetic is one of the most common and expensive guinea pig vet bills, and it is very largely preventable with a full hay rack.

3. They are hindgut fermenters that eat their own droppings

Guinea pigs digest fibre by fermenting it in the caecum, which means the useful nutrients come out at the wrong end. They solve this by producing special soft droppings called caecotrophs and eating them directly — recovering B vitamins, protein and other nutrients on a second pass. It looks alarming and it is completely normal and essential. A pig that stops doing it, or one you find caecotrophs squashed around the cage from, needs a vet: it usually means obesity, arthritis or dental pain is stopping it reaching.

Hindgut fermentation also explains why the gut must never stop. A guinea pig that goes 12–24 hours without eating is a genuine emergency, not a wait-and-see.

And one more: they are herd animals

Not strictly nutrition, but it affects feeding. Guinea pigs are intensely social and should never be kept alone — Switzerland makes solo keeping illegal for that reason. In a pair or group, feed at multiple stations and watch that the bolder pig isn't monopolising the pellets and vegetables.

3 Hay: 80% of the Diet

If you get nothing else right, get this right.

Unlimited grass hay, available every hour of every day, is the foundation of guinea pig feeding. It wears the teeth down, it provides the long fibre the gut needs to keep moving, it occupies a grazing animal that would otherwise be bored, and it is low enough in calcium to be safe in unlimited quantity. A guinea pig should be eating roughly its own body size in hay every day.

Buying and storing it

  • Green, not yellow. Fresh hay is green and smells sweet. Yellow, dusty or musty hay has lost nutrients or gone mouldy — mouldy hay is dangerous.
  • Buy big. Small pet-shop bags are extremely poor value. A box or bale from a hay specialist typically costs a fraction per pound and is fresher.
  • Store it dry and breathing. A cardboard box or fabric bag in a cool dry place. Sealed plastic traps moisture and grows mould.
  • Refill more often than you think. Guinea pigs pick through hay and reject what they've soiled. A rack that looks half-full may hold nothing they'll eat.
  • Offer it in several places — a rack, a pile, a stuffed tunnel. More stations means more chewing and less squabbling.
"They don't like hay" almost always means something else

A guinea pig refusing hay is usually telling you the hay is stale, the rack is soiled, or its teeth already hurt. Try a fresh bag of a different grass first. If it still won't eat hay but will eat soft food, get the teeth checked — molar spurs are painful and invisible without a proper oral exam.

4 Vitamin C: The One They Can't Make

Every day, without exception. Two weeks of deficiency is enough to cause disease.

Daily vitamin C requirement
AnimalRequirementFor a 1 kg pig
Healthy adult10–30 mg per kg body weight~10–30 mg/day
Growing, pregnant or nursing30–50 mg per kg~30–50 mg/day
Sick or recovering30–50 mg per kg (vet may advise more)~30–50 mg/day

Vitamin C is water-soluble, so a modest excess is passed in urine rather than stored — which is why a daily supply matters far more than a big dose now and then. Sources, in order of reliability:

1

Fresh vegetables, every day

The best and most natural source. Half a bell pepper alone covers an adult's daily requirement comfortably.

2

A quality fortified pellet

Helpful, but treat it as a bonus rather than the plan — see the warning below about how fast the vitamin C in pellets degrades.

3

A daily tablet, if needed

Plain chewable vitamin C tablets are cheap insurance, especially for a fussy eater, a sick pig, or a household that sometimes runs out of fresh veg.

Never put vitamin C drops in the water bottle

It is the most common well-meaning mistake in guinea pig care and it fails twice. Vitamin C in water degrades within hours, especially in light, so the dose is unreliable. Worse, it changes the taste, and a guinea pig that drinks less water is at higher risk of bladder stones and dehydration. Give vitamin C in food or as a tablet.

Recognising scurvy

Vitamin C deficiency stops the body making collagen, which shows up across the whole animal. Watch for a rough, staring coat, reluctance to move, a hunched posture, swollen or painful joints, bleeding gums, loose stools, poor wound healing, and weight loss. It responds well to prompt treatment — and a vet should confirm it, because several of those signs overlap with other illnesses.

5 Pellets: A Small, Boring Scoop

A supplement to hay, not a meal. And the type matters enormously.

Pellets fill nutritional gaps that hay and vegetables might miss, and provide a reliable vitamin C top-up. That's their whole job. Fed in excess they displace hay, cause obesity, and contribute to bladder stones. An eighth of a cup — around 30 grams — per pig per day is the standard measure, and adults do fine at the lower end.

Buy pellets that are

  • Timothy or grass-hay based, listed as the first ingredient
  • Plain and uniform — every piece identical
  • Fortified with stabilised vitamin C
  • Well within their best-before date
  • Alfalfa-based only for pigs under 6 months or nursing sows
  • Sold in a quantity you'll use within about three months

Leave on the shelf

  • Muesli mixes with seeds, flakes and coloured bits
  • Anything containing nuts, corn or dried peas
  • Pellets with added sugar, honey or molasses
  • Rabbit pellets — no vitamin C
  • Yoghurt drops and seed sticks
  • Bulk sacks you can't finish in a few months
Why muesli mixes are genuinely harmful

Given a bowl of mixed pieces, a guinea pig will pick out the sweet, fatty, tasty bits and leave the fibrous pellets behind. This is called selective feeding, and the result is an animal eating an unbalanced high-sugar diet while the healthy portion sits uneaten. It drives obesity, dental disease and gut problems. A plain uniform pellet removes the choice, which is exactly the point.

Vitamin C in pellets decays fast

Even stabilised vitamin C degrades over time, and much of it can be gone within roughly 90 days of the manufacture date — faster in heat and light. That's why pellets are a backup rather than your vitamin C plan. Buy small bags, check the date on the packet rather than the shelf, and store them sealed, cool and dark.

6 Fresh Vegetables & Fruit

About one cup per pig per day, built around bell pepper.

Fresh vegetables are where most of the vitamin C comes from, and they're the part of the day a guinea pig gets genuinely excited about. Aim for roughly one cup per pig per day, split across a couple of servings, and built from three or four different items rather than a mountain of one thing. Introduce anything new in a small amount and wait a day.

Rotate, don't repeat

A sensible daily plate: half a bell pepper, a couple of leaves of romaine, a slice of cucumber, and one rotating extra — a sprig of cilantro one day, a small piece of courgette the next. Rotating the high-calcium greens rather than feeding them daily is what keeps the calcium load safe while still getting the nutrition.

7 Calcium, Bladder Stones & Water

The other big nutritional risk, and the one most owners don't see coming.

Guinea pigs absorb dietary calcium efficiently and excrete the excess through their urine — which is why healthy guinea pig urine is often cloudy and chalky. Push that too far and the calcium precipitates into bladder stones, which are painful, common, and usually need surgery. Most guinea pig uroliths are calcium-based.

The target is balance rather than avoidance. Growing bones need calcium; the aim is a calcium-to-phosphorus ratio of roughly 1.2–2:1. Grass hays sit around 1.5:1, which is why an unlimited-grass-hay diet is naturally safe. Alfalfa sits above 4:1, which is why it isn't.

Managing calcium
DoWhy
Grass hay (timothy, orchard, meadow) as the stapleNaturally sits in the right Ca:P range
Alfalfa only under 6 months, or pregnant and nursingToo calcium-rich for adult maintenance
Rotate kale, parsley, spinach and dandelion rather than dailyGreat nutrition, high calcium or oxalate load
Keep pellets to the measured 1/8 cupPellets concentrate minerals; more is not better

Watch for blood in the urine or squeaking while urinating

Straining, squeaking in pain while passing urine, blood-tinged urine, or a hunched posture point at bladder stones or a urinary infection. Both need a vet promptly — stones don't pass on their own and cause real suffering. Note that plain cloudy or chalky urine on its own is normal for the species.

Water

  • Fresh, clean water every single day — roughly 100 ml per kg of body weight, though a pig eating lots of wet vegetables will drink less.
  • Bottle or bowl, or both. Bottles stay clean; bowls are more natural and many pigs drink more from them. Offering both is a good way to make sure they're drinking.
  • Glass bottles beat plastic — they don't cloud, they're easier to scrub, and guinea pigs can't chew them.
  • Check the ball valve daily. A stuck sipper is a classic silent killer: the bottle looks full and nothing is coming out.
  • Scrub weekly with a bottle brush. Biofilm builds up fast and green algae faster in the light.
  • Nothing added. No vitamin drops, no flavourings, no medication unless a vet directs it.

A workable daily rhythm

1

Morning

Top up hay to overflowing. Half the day's vegetables. Fresh water, and check the sipper actually flows.

2

Evening

The rest of the vegetables and the measured pellet scoop. Remove anything uneaten from the morning before it spoils.

3

Every day, while you're there

Watch them eat for a minute. Appetite is the earliest warning sign you get, and it's free.

4

Weekly

Weigh each pig and write it down. Scrub the bottle. A loss of 50–100 g is a red flag long before anything looks wrong.

A kitchen scale is the best diagnostic tool you own

Guinea pigs are prey animals and hide illness expertly. Weight doesn't hide. A weekly weigh-in on a digital kitchen scale, written in a notebook, catches dental disease, scurvy and gut problems weeks before you'd otherwise notice. Consistent loss over two weeks, or a sudden drop of 50 g or more, means a vet visit.

Not eating is always an emergency

A guinea pig's gut has to keep moving. If yours has not eaten or passed droppings for 12–24 hours, that is an emergency vet visit, not a wait-and-see. Find an exotics vet who sees guinea pigs before you need one, and save the out-of-hours number.

Never: iceberg lettuce, potato, onion, garlic, leek, avocado, rhubarb, tomato leaves and stems, chocolate, dairy, meat, bread, seeds, nuts, and lawn mower clippings. Guinea pigs are strict herbivores with a fibre-dependent gut, and several of those are outright toxic. Fruit isn't forbidden but should be a thumbnail-sized treat once or twice a week.

Usually normal. Guinea pigs excrete excess dietary calcium through their urine, which makes it cloudy or chalky. What isn't normal is straining, squeaking in pain while urinating, blood-tinged urine, or a hunched posture — those suggest bladder stones or an infection and need a vet promptly. If the chalkiness is heavy, review calcium: switch to grass hay, rotate the high-calcium greens, measure the pellets and make sure they're drinking.

Only pigs under about six months old, and pregnant or nursing sows. Alfalfa is a legume rather than a grass, with far more calcium and protein than an adult needs — a calcium-to-phosphorus ratio above 4:1 against the 1.2–2:1 you're aiming for. In adults it's a significant bladder stone risk. Grass hays sit around 1.5:1, which is why they're safe in unlimited quantity.

What's not normal is finding squashed caecotrophs around the cage — that usually means obesity, arthritis or dental pain is stopping them reaching, and it needs a vet.

Common causes are dental pain, gut stasis, bloat and scurvy. Keeping a critical care recovery formula in the cupboard means you can support them while you arrange the appointment — but it's a stopgap, not a substitute for the vet.

Weekly, on the same day, on a digital kitchen scale that reads in grams. Guinea pigs hide illness extremely well but weight doesn't lie, so this is the single best early-warning system you have. Adults typically sit between 700 and 1,200 g. A sudden drop of 50 g or more, or a steady decline over two weeks, warrants a vet visit even if everything else looks fine.

Key takeaways

  • Unlimited grass hay is 80% of the diet — it's what keeps their teeth and gut working.
  • Vitamin C every single day. 10–30 mg/kg for adults; never in the water bottle.
  • One cup of fresh veg per pig, built around bell pepper.
  • 1/8 cup of plain timothy pellets. No muesli mixes, ever.
  • Calcium causes bladder stones — rotate kale and parsley, skip alfalfa in adults.
  • Fresh water daily, and check the sipper actually flows.
  • Weigh weekly. It catches illness weeks before you'd see it.
  • Not eating for 12–24 hours is an emergency. Not a wait-and-see.

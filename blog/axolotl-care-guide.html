The one-sentence version

A cycled 20-gallon-long tank, water under 68°F, fine sand or bare glass, gentle filtration, two hides, and a diet of earthworms will get you a healthy axolotl for a decade. Almost every axolotl that dies young died of warm water, an uncycled tank, or swallowed gravel.

2 What Exactly Is an Axolotl?

Not a fish, not quite a frog, and permanently a teenager.

The axolotl is a salamander that never grows up. Nearly every other amphibian hatches as an aquatic larva, then metamorphoses — loses its gills, grows lungs, and walks onto land. Axolotls skip that step. They keep their larval body for their entire adult life: feathery external gills, a tail fin, lidless eyes, and a fully aquatic lifestyle. Biologists call this neoteny, and it is the single most important fact about keeping them.

Practically, neoteny means an axolotl is an aquatic animal that breathes mostly through gills and skin. There is no land section in their tank. There is no basking spot. Their skin is permeable, which is why water quality matters far more to them than it does to a fish, and why so many common aquarium medications are lethal to them.

Ten facts worth knowing before you buy

  1. They are critically endangered in the wild. Axolotls are native to exactly one place on Earth — the canal remnants of Lake Xochimilco in Mexico City. Wild numbers are estimated in the dozens per square kilometre. Every pet axolotl comes from a captive lineage established over a century ago, so keeping one does not harm the wild population.
  2. They regenerate. An axolotl can regrow a lost limb, tail, gill, jaw, and parts of its heart, spinal cord and brain — scar-free. This is why they are one of the most-studied animals in regenerative medicine. It is not a licence to be careless: regeneration costs energy and a fresh wound can go septic.
  3. Their skeleton is mostly cartilage. Adults never fully ossify. Squeezing an axolotl in your hand can injure it.
  4. They have no eyelids and poor eyesight. They hunt by smell and by sensing water movement, and they dislike bright light. Never point a strong aquarium light at one.
  5. They eat by suction. An axolotl lunges and snaps its jaws open, vacuuming in whatever is nearby — including gravel, small stones, and other axolotls' toes. That reflex is why substrate choice is a life-or-death decision.
  6. They are solitary. There is no social benefit to keeping two. In the wild they meet mainly to breed.
  7. They can gulp air. Occasional trips to the surface for a mouthful of air are normal — they do have small, poorly developed lungs. Constant surfacing is not normal and usually means poor water quality or heat.
  8. They come in many colours. The morph does not change care requirements at all.
  9. They can live 10–15 years. This is a decade-long commitment, closer to a dog than a goldfish.
  10. Warmth is their number one killer. Sustained temperatures above 74°F (23°C) cause stress, refusal to eat, and fungal or bacterial infection. Above about 77°F (25°C) it becomes an emergency.

3 Legality & Where to Buy

Check this before you check anything else — axolotls are illegal to keep in several places.

Axolotls are restricted in parts of the US

As of 2026, keeping axolotls as pets is prohibited in California, Maine, New Jersey, Hawaii and Washington D.C., and New Mexico requires a permit. Some cities and counties add their own rules. Wildlife regulations change, so confirm with your state fish & wildlife agency (and your city) before you buy. Outside the US, most of Europe, the UK, Australia (with state variation) and Canada allow captive-bred axolotls — but again, verify locally.

Buying from a good source

Axolotls are one of the easiest exotics to breed, which means the market is full of both excellent hobbyist breeders and careless volume sellers. A dedicated breeder is almost always cheaper and better than a chain pet store, because you can ask questions and see the parents.

Green flags

  • Animals are kept at 60–68°F and the seller can tell you the exact temperature
  • They ask you whether your tank is cycled
  • Juveniles offered at 3–4 inches or larger, not hatchlings
  • They tell you the hatch date, morph, and current diet
  • Clear photos of the actual animal, gills full and forward
  • A live-arrival guarantee and overnight shipping with a cold pack

Red flags

  • Housed in a warm, brightly lit display tank with gravel
  • Sold with a "starter kit" containing a heater
  • Sold under 2 inches, or several crowded together
  • Curled or stubby gills, curled tail tip, fuzzy white patches
  • Missing toes or bite marks from tankmates
  • Seller cannot say what the animal eats
  • Any claim of "wild caught" — illegal and unethical
Set the tank up first — weeks first

A fishless cycle takes 4–8 weeks. Buy the animal after your tank reads 0 ammonia, 0 nitrite and holds temperature. Bringing an axolotl home to an uncycled tank is the most common beginner mistake, and it usually shows up as chemical burns on the gills within two weeks.

4 Habitat & Tank Setup

Cool, calm, dim and roomy. Build for floor space, not height.

1

Ceramic or terracotta hides

At least two per axolotl, both wide enough for the animal to turn around in. Terracotta pots on their side are cheap and perfect. A stressed axolotl with nowhere to hide stops eating.

2

Gentle filtration

A sponge filter or a canister with the return baffled. Axolotls are ambush predators from still canal water — constant current exhausts them and curls their gills forward.

3

Spray bar or baffled outflow

Point the return along the back glass or at the surface, never down at the animal. A DIY baffle from filter sponge over an HOB outlet works fine.

4

Fine sand or bare bottom

Grains under about 1 mm pass safely through the gut. Anything bigger — pea gravel, decorative pebbles, glass beads — can cause fatal impaction.

5

A thermometer you actually read

Temperature is the parameter most likely to kill your animal and the one most likely to drift. Check it daily in summer.

6

Low-light plants

Anubias, Java fern, Java moss, Marimo balls and Elodea all thrive in cool, dim water. Floating plants add shade and soak up nitrate.

7

A secure mesh lid

Axolotls occasionally launch themselves at the surface. A lid also slows evaporation and keeps cats out.

Tank size & shape

A single adult needs a 20-gallon long as an absolute minimum; a 29-gallon or 40-gallon breeder is meaningfully better and not much more expensive. The critical dimension is floor area, because axolotls spend almost all their time walking on the bottom. A tall, narrow 20-gallon "high" is a worse home than a shallow 20-long with the same volume.

Water depth is flexible. Anything from about 8 inches up is fine, and deeper water is actually easier to keep cool and stable — as long as flow stays low. Fill it and forget the old myth that axolotls need shallow water.

Temperature: the other one

Axolotls come from cool highland water. Their comfort band is 60–68°F (16–20°C), with about 64°F being ideal. They will tolerate brief excursions, but sustained warmth suppresses the immune system and appetite, and fungal infections follow within days.

What temperature actually does
TemperatureStatusWhat you'll see
54–60°F / 12–16°CCoolSafe. Slower metabolism, reduced appetite. Used deliberately when treating illness.
60–68°F / 16–20°CIdealGood appetite, full forward gills, active evenings.

Above 76°F / 24°CEmergencyFungal and bacterial infection, organ stress, death within days. Act immediately.

How to cool a tank, cheapest first

  • Put the tank in the coolest room — a basement or north-facing room away from windows and radiators. Free, and often enough on its own.
  • Lift the lid and run a clip-on fan across the surface. Evaporative cooling reliably buys you 2–4°F. Top up with dechlorinated water as it evaporates.
  • Air-condition the room. Cooling the air is far more efficient than cooling the water.
  • Fit an aquarium chiller. The only method that holds an exact temperature through a heatwave. Expensive, but if you live somewhere hot it is not optional.
  • Frozen water bottles are a last-resort emergency measure only. They cause swings, and swings are their own stressor.
Never put a heater in an axolotl tank

If a shop sells you a "tropical starter kit", remove the heater. Room temperature is already at or above the top of their range in most homes.

Tankmates: the short answer is none

Fish nip at gills and carry parasites. Small fish and snails get inhaled and can cause impaction. Shrimp become snacks. Other axolotls bite each other's limbs and gills, especially when one is bigger or hungry — juveniles are outright cannibalistic.

If you really want two, house them only as similar-sized adults, in a 40-gallon breeder or larger, with multiple hides and separate hand-feeding — and be ready with a second tank. A solo axolotl is not lonely; it is safe.

5 Water Quality & Cycling

Their skin is permeable. Water chemistry is their environment, not just what's around them.

The nitrogen cycle in sixty seconds

Your axolotl produces ammonia through waste and respiration. Ammonia is toxic. In a mature tank, two groups of beneficial bacteria live in the filter and substrate: the first converts ammonia into nitrite (also toxic), and the second converts nitrite into nitrate (much less toxic). You then export nitrate with weekly water changes. Growing those bacteria colonies before the animal arrives is called cycling the tank, and it is non-negotiable.

1

Set up everything and fill with dechlorinated water

Filter running, substrate in, hides placed. No animal.

2

Dose pure ammonia to 2–4 ppm

Unscented household ammonia with no surfactants, or a bottled aquarium ammonia product. A bottled bacteria starter will speed things up considerably.

3

Test every 2–3 days and re-dose

Ammonia falls, nitrite spikes then falls, nitrate climbs. Keep topping ammonia back up to 2 ppm whenever it hits zero.

4

Wait for the 24-hour test

The tank is cycled when a 2 ppm ammonia dose reads 0 ammonia and 0 nitrite 24 hours later. Typically 4–8 weeks.

5

Big water change, then add your axolotl

Drop nitrate below 20 ppm, match the temperature, and acclimate the animal slowly by floating and drip.

Things that are safe for fish and lethal for axolotls
  • Copper-based medications (many ich and parasite treatments) — highly toxic to amphibians.
  • Tea-tree / melaleuca products (Melafix-type "fix" remedies) — widely reported to harm amphibian skin. Avoid entirely.
  • Untreated tap water — chloramine does not gas off overnight the way chlorine does.
  • Aquarium heaters — see above.
  • Salt at fish-tank doses left in permanently — salt is a short bath treatment, not a tank additive.

When in doubt, treat in a separate container under the guidance of an exotics vet rather than dosing the display tank.

Use a liquid test kit, not paper strips

Strips are notoriously inaccurate for ammonia and nitrite — the two readings that actually matter. A liquid master kit costs about the same as two strip packs and lasts hundreds of tests.

6 Diet & Feeding

Axolotls are obligate carnivores with one near-perfect staple food: the humble earthworm.

In the wild axolotls eat worms, insect larvae, small crustaceans, molluscs and the occasional small fish. In captivity you can meet all of that nutrition with earthworms as the staple and a quality sinking pellet as backup. Everything else is variety or a treat.

Portion size: feed until the animal loses interest and stops striking — usually a volume roughly equal to the size of its own head. A well-conditioned axolotl's body is about as wide as its head. Noticeably wider means you're overfeeding; noticeably narrower, or a visible spine ridge, means underfeeding.

Feeding technique

  • Use tongs. Dangle a worm just in front of the animal's snout and let it strike. Feeding by hand teaches them to bite fingers, and fingers taste like whatever you touched last.
  • Feed over a dish or bare tile if you keep sand. A shallow ceramic dish catches pellets and dramatically cuts the amount of sand swallowed.
  • Feed in the evening. Axolotls are most active at dawn and dusk and will be far more responsive with the lights off.
  • Remove leftovers within 20–30 minutes. Uneaten protein is the fastest way to spike ammonia.
  • Use a turkey baster for blackworms and bloodworms so you can place food precisely instead of clouding the tank.
Do axolotls need vitamin supplements?

Generally no — and this surprises people coming from reptiles. Axolotls need no UVB lighting and no calcium or D3 dusting. A varied diet built on whole earthworms supplies what they need. The supplements that genuinely matter for an axolotl are water supplements: a dechlorinator, a mineral/GH booster if your tap water is very soft, beneficial bacteria while cycling, and non-iodised salt kept on hand for treatment baths. Those are covered in the product section below.

A refusal to eat is a symptom, not a preference

A healthy axolotl at the right temperature eats reliably. If yours skips more than two or three offered meals in a row, test your water and check your thermometer before you try a different food. Warm water and ammonia are the two usual answers.

7 Health: Signs, Problems & First Aid

Axolotls tell you how they feel with their gills and their tail. Learn to read both.

A healthy axolotl

  • Long, full, feathery gill filaments held out or gently back
  • Straight tail with a clean, intact fin
  • Smooth skin, no fuzz, no redness, no excess slime
  • Body roughly as wide as the head
  • Strikes at food eagerly and reliably
  • Rests calmly on the bottom, active at dusk
  • All four limbs with complete toes

Warning signs

  • Gills curled sharply forward over the head
  • Gill filaments shrinking or going bald
  • Tail tip curled to one side
  • Floating and unable to sink
  • Refusing food for several days
  • White cottony tufts on gills or skin
  • Red or inflamed skin, belly or gill stalks
  • Constant surfacing for air, or lying on its side

Three treatments worth knowing before you need them

1. Tubbing — your universal first response

Move the axolotl to a clean, lidded plastic tub with enough dechlorinated, temperature-matched water to cover it comfortably. Change 100% of the water every day. Tubbing gives you perfect water quality, lets you watch appetite and waste closely, and removes them from whatever in the display tank caused the problem. It is the right answer to an uncycled tank, a spike, an injury, or "something looks off and I'm not sure why".

2. Salt baths — for fungus

Dissolve 2–3 teaspoons (roughly 10–15 g) of non-iodised aquarium or rock salt per litre of dechlorinated, tank-temperature water. Bathe the axolotl for 10–15 minutes, once or twice a day, for up to three days, then return it to clean water each time. Stay and watch the whole time: if the animal thrashes or goes rigid, end the bath early. Never salt the main tank.

3. Fridging — for impaction and stubborn illness

Slowing an axolotl's metabolism right down can buy time and help pass a blockage. Put the tub of dechlorinated water in a fridge held at 41–46°F (5–8°C), cool it gradually over several hours rather than in one step, change 100% of the water daily, and do not feed. Two weeks is a normal maximum, and warming back up must be just as gradual. Fridging is a real medical intervention — do it with an exotics vet's input, not as a first guess.

Find your exotics vet on day one

Very few general practices treat amphibians. Search for an exotic-animal or aquatic vet in your area, call ahead, and confirm they see axolotls — before you have a sick animal and a weekend to get through. Save the number on your phone next to the tank readings.

Quarantine every new arrival

Keep a new axolotl in a separate tub or tank for 30 days before it goes anywhere near an existing animal, and feed and maintain it last so nothing travels on your hands or nets. The same applies to live plants and any second-hand decor.

Never rinse filter media under the tap

Chlorinated tap water kills the beneficial bacteria living in your sponge, and you will crash the cycle you spent two months building. Squeeze the media out in a bucket of water you just siphoned from the tank, then put it straight back.

Handling: look, don't touch

Axolotls have delicate, permeable skin, a largely cartilaginous skeleton, and no reason to enjoy being held. Handling should be limited to genuine necessity — moving the animal for treatment or a deep clean.

  • Best method: submerge a clean plastic container, guide the axolotl in, and lift it out with water around it. No contact at all.
  • Acceptable: a soft, fine-mesh net — coarse mesh catches gill filaments and toes.
  • If you must use hands: wet them first with tank water, support the whole body, keep it over the tank, and keep it to seconds. Never squeeze.
  • Always wash your hands before and after — soap residue, lotion and hand sanitiser all pass straight through amphibian skin. Rinse thoroughly, and rinse again.

Behaviour, enrichment and what "normal" looks like

Expect a lot of sitting. Axolotls are ambush predators: long still periods punctuated by a slow walk, a sudden strike, or a lap of the tank at dusk. Some individuals hover mid-water; some perch on top of their hide. All of it is normal.

Enrichment is simple — more hides, a few live plants, occasional rearranging, and dim light. They do not need toys, companions, or a light cycle beyond ambient room light. What they do need is to be left alone, which is the easiest care requirement in the hobby.

A note on breeding

Axolotls breed easily — that is the problem. A single clutch runs from a few hundred to over a thousand eggs, hatchlings are cannibalistic and must be reared in separate containers, and they need live food twice a day for weeks. Don't pair animals casually, don't breed under 18 months old, and have homes lined up first.

11 Beginner Mistakes to Avoid

Every one of these is common, and every one of them is preventable.

Buying the axolotl before the tank is cycled

The fix is boring and it works: set up the tank, cycle it for 4–8 weeks, then buy the animal. If you already have one in an uncycled tank, tub it with daily 100% water changes until the tank finishes.

Gravel in the tank

Axolotls feed by suction and will inhale it. Fine sand or bare glass, no exceptions.

Ignoring temperature until summer

Have your cooling plan in place in spring, not during the first heatwave. A fan and an open lid take five minutes to set up and buy you several degrees.

Too much flow

A powerful filter blasting into open water will curl their gills forward and exhaust them. Baffle it, spray-bar it, or aim it at the glass.

Adding tankmates

Fish nip gills, small fish get eaten, snails get inhaled, and other axolotls bite limbs. One axolotl per tank is the safe default.

Overfeeding adults

Adults eat every 2–3 days, not daily. Daily feeding causes obesity, bloating and a constant ammonia load.

Rinsing filter media under the tap

Chlorine kills your bacteria colony and crashes the cycle. Squeeze media in a bucket of old tank water instead.

Treating the display tank with fish medication

Copper and tea-tree products are dangerous to amphibians. Treat in a separate container, ideally with a vet's input.

Handling for fun

Delicate skin, cartilage skeleton, no benefit to the animal. Move them in a container of water when you genuinely have to.

Trusting test strips

They routinely under-read ammonia — the exact reading you need to be right about. Use a liquid kit.

Key takeaways

  • Cool water is everything. 60–68°F, never a heater, and a cooling plan ready before summer.
  • Cycle the tank before you buy the animal. 0 ammonia, 0 nitrite, nitrate under 20 ppm.
  • Fine sand or bare bottom. Gravel is the most common avoidable killer.
  • Gentle flow, dim light, two hides. Still-water ambush predator, not a display fish.
  • Earthworms are the staple. Sinking pellets as backup; no feeder fish, no mealworms.
  • Keep them alone. No tankmates, no exceptions worth the risk.
  • Gills and tail are your dashboard. Curled forward gills or a hooked tail tip mean check temperature and water today.
  • Have a tub, salt and a vet's number ready before you need any of them.

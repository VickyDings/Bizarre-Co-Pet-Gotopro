You bought ten cherry shrimp, acclimated them carefully, and watched them spend a happy first week grazing across the glass. Then one morning there were eight. A few days later, six. Nothing in the tank looks wrong — the water is clear, the fish are fine, the plants are growing — and yet the colony is quietly disappearing, one shrimp at a time, with no obvious cause and no warning.

That slow fade is the single most common experience in shrimp keeping, and it is almost never caused by the thing people go looking for. Ammonia is usually zero. Disease is rare. What is actually killing them is nearly always one of four things: copper, a swing in water chemistry that went unnoticed, a failed molt, or a tank that was simply too new to feed them. This guide covers all four, along with everything needed to take a colony from ten animals to a self-sustaining population you give away in bagfuls.

That last line is worth sitting with. Neocaridina are not delicate animals — they are one of the hardiest invertebrates in the hobby, and a settled colony in a stable tank is close to unkillable. The reputation for fragility comes almost entirely from the first six weeks, when people are still making the four mistakes above. Get through those and you will spend the following year giving shrimp away.

🎉 Fun Fact #1

Shrimp blood is blue, not red. Fish and mammals carry oxygen on hemoglobin, which is built around iron and turns red when it binds oxygen. Shrimp use hemocyanin instead, which is built around copper and turns faint blue. This is not a piece of trivia — it is the whole reason copper is so lethal to them. An animal whose oxygen transport runs on copper has no way to handle a sudden excess of it, and the dose that treats ich in a fish is many times the dose that shuts a shrimp down.

💧 The Water Parameters That Actually Matter

Most beginners obsess over pH and ignore GH, which is exactly backwards. Shrimp build their shells out of minerals dissolved in the water, so general hardness is the number that decides whether they can molt successfully. pH matters far less, and within any sane range it matters hardly at all.

Here is the reference, for both the beginner species and the advanced one, so you can see at a glance why one is recommended and one is not.

GH (general hardness)6 – 10 dGH4 – 6 dGH

The single most useful line in that table is the water source. It is the real fork in the road, and it decides everything else. If you plan to fill the tank from the faucet, you are building a Neocaridina tank, and you should buy Neocaridina. If you are willing to buy reverse-osmosis water and mix minerals back into it by hand every week, Caridina become possible. Trying to keep Caridina in tap water is the most expensive mistake in this hobby, and people make it constantly because the shrimp are prettier.

Note also what the table does not say. There is no "ideal" pH to chase. Anywhere from 6.5 to 8.0 is fine for Neocaridina, and a stable 7.8 beats a 7.0 that you are constantly adjusting. Chasing a number with pH-down products is a reliable way to kill shrimp, because what actually harms them is the swing, not the value.

Neocaridina or Caridina: pick before you buy anything

They are not interchangeable, and the choice changes what equipment you need.

Neocaridina — start here

Solid, saturated colors: red cherry, blue dream, blue velvet, yellow golden back, orange pumpkin, green jade, black rose. All one species, Neocaridina davidi, selectively bred into color lines the way goldfish were.

They tolerate a genuinely wide range of water, breed in it without any intervention from you, and cost $2 to $5 each. A colony started with a dozen will be forty animals within four months and will keep going.

The one catch: different color lines interbreed, and the offspring revert toward wild brown within a couple of generations. Keep one color per tank.

Caridina — later, deliberately

Intricate patterning rather than flat color: crystal red and black, the bee shrimp, blue bolts, king kong, wine reds. Genuinely stunning animals and priced accordingly, from $8 to well over $100 each.

They need soft, acidic, low-KH water that almost no tap supply provides. In practice that means buying or producing RO water, remineralizing it to an exact GH with a dedicated salt, and using an active soil substrate that actively pulls the KH down.

Do not start here. Keep Neocaridina alive and breeding for six months first. The skills transfer completely, and the mistakes cost $3 instead of $80.

⚠️ The Thing That Kills Them

If shrimp die suddenly — several at once, within hours, in a tank that has been running fine — the cause is copper until proven otherwise. Not ammonia, not disease, not old age. Copper.

⚠️ Copper: the invisible wipeout


Copper kills shrimp at 0.01 to 0.03 ppm — a concentration you cannot see, smell, or detect with a standard test kit. Most fish medications are dosed at 0.25 to 0.50 ppm. A single course of copper-based ich treatment in a shrimp tank kills every invertebrate in it, usually within a day.
Where it comes from, in rough order of how often it catches people:
• Fish medication. Ich treatments, general cures and any product listing copper sulfate or chelated copper. Read every label. If it treats parasites, assume copper until the bottle says otherwise.
• Plant fertilizers. Many all-in-one liquid ferts contain copper as a trace micronutrient. At label dose most are survivable, but double-dosing is not. Look for a shrimp-safe formulation.
• Snail killers. Every one of them works by being toxic to invertebrates. Shrimp are invertebrates.
• Old copper plumbing. Water that has sat overnight in copper pipe picks up measurable copper. Run the faucet for thirty seconds before filling a bucket.
• Untreated driftwood, decor and secondhand equipment. A tank that previously ran copper medication can leach it back out of the silicone for months.
There is no antidote and no treatment. If copper has gone into the tank, the shrimp are lost. The only response is prevention: never medicate a shrimp tank, and move shrimp to a separate container before treating fish.

The second-largest cause is less dramatic and much more common: a chemistry swing during a water change. Shrimp handle a wide range of parameters but they do not handle rapid movement between them. A 50% change with water that is 10°F colder and 100 ppm different in TDS is a genuine shock, and the animal that dies from it will often die two or three days later, during its next molt, which is why the connection gets missed.

Change 10 to 20% weekly, match the temperature, and match the TDS. That is the whole discipline.

🎉 Fun Fact #2

A shrimp's shell does not grow. It is rigid, so the only way to get bigger is to build a soft new one underneath, crack the old one open behind the head, and climb out — a process that takes a few seconds and leaves the animal defenseless for hours afterward. A healthy Neocaridina does this roughly every three to four weeks, more often when young. The discarded shells are perfectly clear and hold their shape, which is why new keepers regularly panic at what looks like a dead shrimp and is actually a ghost.

🧪 Failed Molts and the White Ring of Death

When a molt goes wrong it usually goes wrong in the same way, and it has a name. The white ring of death is a clean white or clear line running right around the shrimp's body at the join between head and abdomen — exactly where the shell is supposed to split.

In a normal molt that seam opens at the top only, like a hinge, and the shrimp bends and springs out through the gap. When the shell separates all the way around instead, the animal is left inside two disconnected halves with nothing to push against. Most get stuck and die within a couple of days. A few work free. There is nothing you can do for a shrimp that already has the ring.

What causes it is almost always mineral related, and specifically one of three things:

  1. GH too low. The shrimp cannot build a shell with enough structure to split cleanly. This is the most common cause in soft-water areas and the reason a GH test kit matters more than a pH one.
  2. A large, fast water change. A sudden drop in TDS triggers an unscheduled molt at the worst possible moment. This is why the deaths cluster two or three days after a big change.
  3. Not enough calcium and protein in the diet. A colony fed only on algae wafers slowly runs down. Shell building needs minerals from the water and raw material from food.

All three are prevented the same way: keep GH between 6 and 10, change water little and often rather than rarely and heavily, and feed a food formulated for shrimp rather than a generic bottom-feeder pellet.

🔧 Setting the Tank Up, Start to Stocked

The whole process takes about six weeks, and five and a half of those are waiting. That waiting is not padding — it is the difference between a colony that establishes and one that fades. Shrimp do not eat what you feed them nearly as much as they eat what grows on the surfaces, and a new tank has nothing growing on it.

  1. Week 0 — hardscape and plant. Inert gravel or sand for Neocaridina; active soil only if you are going Caridina. Add wood and rock, then plant heavily. Java moss, anubias and subwulawesi ferns are the classics because shrimp graze them and they need no fertilizer worth worrying about. Density matters more than variety.
  2. Week 0 — fill and start the filter. Dechlorinate, fit the sponge filter, and set the heater only if the room drops below 68°F. Most homes do not need one.
  3. Weeks 1–4 — cycle it. Dose ammonia to 2 ppm and hold it there until the tank processes 2 ppm to zero ammonia and zero nitrite within 24 hours. Bottled bacteria speeds this up; seeded media from an established tank speeds it up enormously. Do not shortcut this.
  4. Weeks 4–6 — grow the food. This is the step everyone skips. Keep the light on 6–8 hours a day and let the glass and wood go slightly grubby. That faint brown film is diatoms and biofilm, and it is what your shrimp will actually live on. A tank that looks pristine on stocking day is a tank with an empty pantry.
  5. Day 1 of week 6 — test everything. Ammonia 0, nitrite 0, nitrate under 20, GH 6–10, KH 2–8, TDS 180–300. Adjust GH now, before the animals arrive, not after.
  6. Stocking day — drip acclimate. Float the bag 20 minutes to match temperature, then empty shrimp and bag water into a small container and run an airline drip from the tank at 2–3 drips per second. Take 2–3 hours to roughly quadruple the volume. Net the shrimp into the tank and discard the bag water — never pour it in.
  7. First 48 hours — leave them alone. No feeding, no lights for the first day, no rearranging. They will hide. This is normal and it is not a problem.
  8. Week 7 onward — feed lightly. A piece of prepared food the size of a grain of rice per 10 shrimp, every second day, removed if it is not gone within three hours. Underfeeding a shrimp tank is very difficult; overfeeding it is the easiest thing in the world.

💚 The one-sentence version

Build the tank, cycle it, then wait a further two weeks doing nothing at all before you buy a single shrimp. Almost every failed colony in this hobby was stocked into a tank that was technically cycled but biologically empty.

🍽️ What They Actually Eat

A shrimp's diet is roughly 80% things you did not put in the tank. Biofilm — the microbial layer that coats every submerged surface — is their staple, and they graze it continuously from the moment the lights come on. Prepared food is a supplement, not a meal plan.

That said, the supplement matters, because biofilm alone is thin on protein and calcium and both are needed for shell building. A reasonable week looks like this:

FoodHow oftenWhy it is in the rotation
Biofilm and algaeConstantly, unmanagedThe staple. Your job is to grow it, not to serve it

Blanched vegetablesOnce or twice a monthZucchini, spinach, cucumber. Blanch 30–60 seconds, cool, remove after 4 hours
Indian almond leavesOne leaf per monthSlowly rots into biofilm, releases mild tannins, doubles as shelter

Cholla woodPermanent fixtureHoneycombed surface, enormous grazing area for its size

Two specific warnings. First, detritus worms are a feedback signal, not a pest: a sudden bloom of thin white worms in the substrate means there is more food going in than the tank is consuming. Cut feeding rather than treating the worms. Second, almost every commercial "algae wafer" is formulated for plecos and is far too large and too rich for a nano shrimp tank. A wafer intended for a six-inch catfish will foul a five-gallon tank.

🎉 Fun Fact #3

Amano shrimp cannot breed in your tank, and it is not your fault. Caridina multidentata is the best algae eater in the hobby and is bought by the thousand, but its larvae hatch at about 1 mm and require brackish to full marine salinity to develop. In the wild the larvae are swept downstream into estuaries, spend weeks in salt water, and then walk back up the river as juveniles. Drop them in fresh water and they die within hours. Every amano shrimp you have ever bought was either wild-caught or raised in a facility running two separate salinities.

👀 A Healthy Shrimp and a Shrimp in Trouble

Shrimp are small and mostly transparent, which makes them harder to read than a fish. Fortunately the signals are consistent once you know where to look, and almost all of them are behavioral rather than physical.

What you are looking for at feeding time

Thirty seconds of watching tells you more than any test kit.

✅ A colony that is fine

  • Constant grazing. Front legs picking at a surface, all day, in the open
  • Deep, even color that holds rather than flickering
  • Fast, deliberate movement. A startled shrimp shoots backwards and then carries on
  • Berried females fanning eggs under the tail — the clearest possible sign of a healthy tank
  • Clear shed shells appearing regularly and disappearing again as they are eaten
  • Visible babies — miniature adults, not larvae, hiding in the moss

🚩 Stop and test the water

  • Sitting still in the open, not grazing, for long periods
  • Swimming frantically along the glass — a classic response to a chemistry swing
  • Color draining toward translucent across the colony
  • A white ring at the head-body join — a failed molt in progress
  • A solid opaque white body, which is a dead shrimp, not a shed shell
  • Every female dropping eggs at once, which is stress, not bad luck
  • Deaths clustering two or three days after a water change

The distinction between a shed shell and a dead shrimp catches everyone once. A molt is clear and hollow, holds its shrimp shape, and often has the split visible behind the head. A dead shrimp is opaque white or pink, like cooked seafood, because the proteins in the flesh have denatured. Hold it up to the light; if you can see through it, nothing has died.

🐣 Breeding, Which Happens Whether You Plan It or Not

There is no technique to breeding Neocaridina. If the water is stable, the tank is mature and there are males and females present, they breed. The only real question is whether the babies survive, and that comes down to two things: whether the filter can eat them, and whether anything else in the tank can.

The sequence is worth recognizing so you know what you are seeing:

  1. The saddle. A yellow-green patch on the female's back behind the head, visible through the shell. These are eggs developing in the ovaries — she is not yet carrying them.
  2. The molt and the signal. A saddled female molts and simultaneously releases a pheromone. Males become visibly agitated and swim the tank in search of her. This is the "shrimp disco" people report in the evening.
  3. Berried. The eggs move from the saddle to under the tail, held in the swimmerets. She will fan them constantly with her pleopods to keep them oxygenated. A berried female is the single best indicator that your tank is right.
  4. 28 to 35 days. Gestation, faster at the warm end of the range. You can watch the eggs develop — they darken, and eventually you can see eyes in them.
  5. Hatching. They emerge as fully formed miniature shrimp, about 2 mm long, with no larval stage at all. They eat exactly what the adults eat and need nothing special from you.
  6. Repeat, quickly. A female can be carrying a new clutch within days of the last one hatching. A colony of 12 becomes 40-plus in four months.
🎉 Fun Fact #4

That "no larval stage" detail is genuinely unusual and it is exactly why Neocaridina are easy and amano shrimp are not. Most shrimp species — including almost every marine one — hatch as planktonic zoea larvae that drift, need microscopic live food, and pass through several body plans before becoming recognizable shrimp. Neocaridina skipped all of it: the eggs are larger, fewer, and the young hatch as working shrimp. It is the aquarium equivalent of an animal being born able to walk.

If you actively want more babies, the highest-leverage changes are a sponge filter or pre-filter sponge so nothing gets eaten by the intake, a thick clump of java moss for them to hide in for the first two weeks of their lives, and keeping the tank shrimp-only. Almost any fish that fits a baby shrimp in its mouth will eat baby shrimp, regardless of how peaceful it is otherwise.

A shrimp colony is not really a pet you keep — it is a small ecosystem you set the conditions for and then mostly leave alone. The keepers who succeed are the ones who get bored of intervening. — The Pet-GoToPro Editorial Team

🎉 Four More Things Worth Knowing

🎉 Fun Fact #5

Cherry shrimp change color with their mood and their background, and it happens fast. Chromatophores in the shell expand and contract in response to stress, light and substrate, which is why a shrimp moved into a bag goes pale within minutes and why colonies kept on black substrate consistently look richer than the same genetics on white gravel. If your reds look washed out, try the substrate before you blame the breeding.

🎉 Fun Fact #6

Every color line in the hobby — red cherry, blue dream, yellow golden back, orange pumpkin, green jade, black rose, chocolate — is the same species, Neocaridina davidi, whose wild form is a drab translucent brown. The entire rainbow was produced in under three decades of selective breeding starting in Taiwan in the 1990s. It is the fastest domestication event in the aquarium hobby, and it is why mixing two lines gives you brown shrimp back within a few generations: you are simply undoing the selection.

🎉 Fun Fact #7

Shrimp eat their own shed shells, and this is not scavenging — it is recovery. The shell is mostly chitin bound with calcium carbonate, and rebuilding one is metabolically expensive, so reabsorbing the old one recovers a large share of the mineral cost of the next molt. Scooping out molts to keep the tank tidy quietly makes your shrimp work harder for every shell they build.

🎉 Fun Fact #8

A berried female fans her eggs continuously for the entire month she carries them, using specialized swimming legs called pleopods. It is not affection, it is plumbing: a static egg mass develops low-oxygen dead zones and fungal infections within days. If you watch a berried shrimp closely under a light you can see the clutch rippling as she works — and a female who stops fanning has almost always already lost the clutch.

🛍️ Buying Shrimp Without Getting Burned

Where you buy matters more than what you pay. A $3 shrimp from a healthy colony is a far better purchase than an $8 one that has spent a week in a copper-dosed chain-store tank, and the difference does not show for two weeks.

Buy from a hobbyist breeder if you possibly can. Online shrimp specialists and local aquarium club members sell animals bred in water they will tell you the parameters of, from colonies that have been stable for generations. They ship well, because shrimp travel better than almost any fish. Ask two questions before you order: what GH and TDS the parent colony is kept at, and whether the line has been outcrossed recently.

The first question lets you match your tank to theirs, which removes most of the acclimation risk. The second matters because heavily inbred color lines get progressively weaker — a breeder who cannot tell you when they last brought in fresh stock is often selling animals that will struggle regardless of your water.

What to look at in the seller's tank, in person or on a video call:

  1. Are they grazing? A tank of shrimp sitting motionless in the open is a tank with a problem. Healthy animals pick constantly at surfaces.
  2. Are there berried females? The single best indicator you can get. Shrimp do not breed in water that stresses them, so a tank with visible berried females is a tank that works.
  3. Are there babies at several sizes? Staggered sizes mean continuous successful breeding rather than one lucky clutch.
  4. Is the color even across the colony? A few pale individuals among strong ones is normal genetic variation. A uniformly washed-out tank is stress.
  5. Any fish in with them? Not disqualifying, but shrimp raised shrimp-only tend to be bolder and easier to observe once they settle.

Buy more than you think you need. Ten is the practical minimum and fifteen is better. This is not about stocking density — it is about genetics and about the arithmetic of early losses. Expect to lose one or two in the first month regardless of how carefully you acclimate; starting with six means a single bad week can leave you without a viable breeding group.

Grade and price are mostly about color saturation and how far it extends down the legs and rostrum. A "painted fire red" costs several times a standard cherry because the red is opaque and covers the whole animal rather than being patchy and translucent. It is worth paying for if you want the look, but it has no bearing on hardiness — the care is identical, and a low-grade cherry will breed just as readily and often more vigorously.

One last piece of practical advice: do not stock the tank the day the shrimp arrive if the water does not match. If the seller keeps their colony at 250 TDS and yours reads 160, spend a week walking your tank up with mineral salt before the order goes in, not three panicked hours on the doorstep. Shrimp ship fine and will sit in the bag for another day if they must.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

My shrimp keep dying one at a time and everything tests fine. What is going on?

Test GH specifically — it is not in the standard master kit and it is the most common blind spot. Slow attrition with zero ammonia is usually low GH causing failed molts, or a TDS swing at water changes. Check the deaths against your water change schedule: if they cluster two to three days after, the change is the cause. Switch to 10–20% weekly with temperature and TDS matched, and raise GH to 6–10 with a mineral salt if it tests low.

How many shrimp can I keep in a 5 or 10 gallon tank?

Start with 10–15 in either, then let them settle. Shrimp have a tiny bioload, so the practical ceiling is much higher than for fish — an established, planted 10-gallon comfortably holds 50 to 100. The limit in practice is food, not filtration: a colony will grow until the available biofilm stops supporting more, then stabilize on its own.

Do I need a heater?

Usually not. Neocaridina are comfortable from 68 to 78°F, which covers most heated homes year-round. If your room drops below 68°F in winter, a small adjustable heater set to 72°F is worth it — but note that breeding is faster at the warmer end, so a heater is more about consistency than survival.

Why is my shrimp white and opaque? Is it sick?

If it is lying on the substrate and completely opaque white or pink, it has died — the proteins in the flesh turn white the way cooked shrimp do. If it is clear and hollow and holds its shape, it is a shed shell and everything is fine. Hold it up to a light: you can see straight through a molt. A living shrimp with a white ring at the head-body join is a third case, a molt going wrong, and unfortunately there is nothing to be done for it.

Can I keep shrimp with a betta?

Sometimes, and it depends entirely on the individual fish. Some bettas ignore shrimp completely for years; some clear a colony inside a week. If you try it, stack the odds: add the shrimp first and let them establish, plant the tank so densely the betta cannot patrol it, use adult shrimp rather than juveniles, and have a second tank ready. Expect to lose the babies regardless.

How long until they start breeding?

Usually six to twelve weeks after stocking, assuming the tank is mature and stable. The first berried female is the signal that your parameters are genuinely right, since shrimp will not breed in water that stresses them. After that a female can carry a new clutch within days of the last hatching, so the colony compounds quickly.

Can I mix red cherry and blue dream shrimp?

You can, and they will interbreed happily, but the offspring revert toward the wild-type brown within two or three generations. If you like the look of a mixed tank and do not care about keeping lines true, it is harmless. If you want to keep selling or trading colored shrimp, keep one line per tank.

Is tap water safe?

For Neocaridina, usually yes — dechlorinate it and test GH. Moderately hard tap water is close to ideal. Very soft tap water needs a GH mineral salt added; very hard water above about 400 TDS is worth cutting with RO. Run the faucet for thirty seconds before filling if you have copper plumbing, since water that has sat in the pipe overnight picks up copper.

What is the brown film on my glass and should I remove it?

Diatoms, and no — that is food. A new tank goes through a diatom phase at roughly four to eight weeks and shrimp graze it enthusiastically. It fades on its own as the tank matures. Scrubbing the glass every few days in a young shrimp tank is actively counterproductive; clean the front pane for viewing and leave the other three alone.

How long do they live?

One to two years for Neocaridina, with females generally outliving males. This is short enough that losing an occasional adult in an established colony is normal rather than alarming — if you started with ten animals a year ago and you still have a working colony, individual deaths are just the population turning over.

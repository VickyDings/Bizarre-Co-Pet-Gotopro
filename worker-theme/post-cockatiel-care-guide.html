A cockatiel will outlive your car. Fifteen to twenty-five years is normal, and the record sits past thirty — so the bird in the pet shop window is not a starter pet, it is a commitment roughly the length of a mortgage. Most people find that out after they have already paid for the cage.

The other thing nobody mentions at the till is the dust. Cockatiels are one of only three parrot groups that produce powder down, a fine white powder that coats every surface within about six feet of the cage and never entirely stops. None of that is a reason not to keep one. They are gentle, funny, genuinely affectionate birds that will learn your whistle and shout it back at you from another room. But the cage they are sold in is too small, the seed mix they are sold with will shorten their life, and the pan on your stove can kill one in under half an hour. This guide covers all three, plus everything that follows.

Cockatiels are nomads from inland Australia, and the second most popular pet bird in the world after the budgerigar. In the wild they travel in flocks, following the rain.

The cage: one number that is not negotiable

Almost every cage sold with a cockatiel on the label is too small. The bird is 12 to 14 inches long, over half of which is tail, and it needs room to turn around, stretch both wings fully, and move sideways along a perch without its tail dragging through the water bowl.

Avian veterinarians put the floor at 24 inches wide by 24 deep by 30 high for one adult bird. The Association of Avian Veterinarians asks for a minimum of two square feet of floor space per bird. Those are minimums — the number you buy to if money is tight, not the number you aim for. A cockatiel given 36 inches of width behaves visibly differently from one given 24, because at 36 inches it can actually fly a short hop rather than only climb.

Width beats height every time. A cockatiel flies horizontally, not vertically. A tall narrow cage is a column of air the bird cannot use, and the designs that look most impressive in a shop — the round ones, the pagoda-topped ones — are usually the worst of all, because a round cage gives a bird no corner to retreat into and nothing to orient itself by.

Bar spacing is a safety number, not a preference. Anything wider than 5/8 of an inch and a cockatiel can push its head through, panic, and break its own neck trying to pull back. Measure it before you buy, in the shop, with your fingers if you have nothing else — the gap should be narrower than your little finger is wide. Horizontal bars on at least two sides are worth seeking out, because cockatiels climb, and vertical bars give them nothing to grip.

Horizontal bars give a climbing bird something to hold. Note the orange cheek patch — the single most reliable field mark on a normal gray cockatiel.

Perches: throw away the ones in the box

Every cage ships with two or three identical smooth dowels, usually the same diameter, usually plastic or sanded birch. A bird standing on the same diameter all day grips with the same muscles in the same position for fifteen years, and the result is arthritis, pressure sores on the sole of the foot, and a condition called bumblefoot that is miserable to treat.

Replace them. You want at least four perches of genuinely different diameters, between about half an inch and an inch and a quarter, and at least two of them natural branch with the bark left on. Manzanita, java wood, apple, pear, willow and birch are all safe. The irregular surface works different parts of the foot through the day, and the bark gives the beak something to strip, which cockatiels enjoy enormously.

Perches: what to use and what to bin

Worth having

  • Natural hardwood branch — manzanita, java, apple, willow. Irregular diameter, bark intact.
  • Rope perch — soft, flexible, good for sleeping. Check weekly for frayed threads.
  • One pedicure perch — a single textured perch sited away from the sleeping spot keeps nails blunt.
  • A flat platform — older birds and heavy hens appreciate somewhere to stand flat-footed.

Take it out

  • Smooth plastic dowels — one diameter, no grip, nothing to chew.
  • Sandpaper sleeves — they abrade the sole of the foot, not the nails. Genuinely harmful.
  • A perch above a food or water bowl — everything that lands in them is obvious.
  • Anything treated, painted or from an unknown tree — cherry, apricot, oak and yew are all a problem.
Fun fact: A cockatiel's crest is a live readout of its mood, and it is worth learning. Straight up and tight means alarm. Angled forward and relaxed means curious and interested. Flattened hard against the skull means fear or aggression — back off. Half-raised and loose is a contented bird, which is where you want it most of the day.

Diet: the seed myth is what kills most pet cockatiels

This is the single biggest avoidable cause of early death in companion cockatiels, and the pet trade is largely responsible for it. A seed-only diet looks like what a bird eats. It is not. A wild cockatiel eats dozens of grass seeds at different ripeness, plus greens, plus whatever insects it picks up by accident, and it flies miles a day to find them. A bowl of dried sunflower and millet is the nutritional equivalent of leaving a child alone with a bag of chips.

Birds fed seed-only diets routinely develop fatty liver disease, vitamin A deficiency, obesity and calcium deficiency. They do not look ill for years, and then they look ill very suddenly.

Converting a seed addict takes weeks, not days, and a bird can starve itself. Never remove seed and replace it with pellets overnight. A cockatiel will not recognize pellets as food and may simply not eat. Mix a small amount of pellet into the seed, increase the proportion slowly over three to six weeks, and weigh the bird on a kitchen scale twice a week throughout. A drop of more than ten percent of body weight means stop and call a vet.

The thing that kills them

Non-stick cookware. PTFE — the coating sold as Teflon and under a dozen other names — begins to break down at around 400°F, well below the temperature of an empty pan left on a burner, and releases a gas that destroys bird lungs. A cockatiel in the next room can die inside half an hour. There is no antidote and no treatment; in most documented cases the bird is dead before anyone notices anything is wrong.

Birds breathe through a one-way flow-through system with air sacs reaching into their bones, which makes them several times more efficient at extracting oxygen than we are — and several times more efficient at extracting poison.

It is not only frying pans. PTFE is in drip pans, waffle irons, air fryers, slow cooker inserts, clothing irons, ironing board covers, self-cleaning oven liners, space heaters, hair dryers and some heat lamps. If you keep a cockatiel, the kitchen goes stainless steel or cast iron, and anything else coated gets checked.

A normal gray — the wild-type coloring every other mutation descends from. The solid gray body, white wing bar and orange cheek are the original pattern.

The dust nobody warns you about

That faint bloom on the feathers is powder down. It is constantly produced, constantly shed, and it is how a cockatiel waterproofs and conditions its own plumage.

Cockatiels, cockatoos and African greys share something no other common pet parrot has: specialized feathers that disintegrate at the tip into a fine white powder. The bird works it through its plumage while preening, and it functions as a dry shampoo and a waterproofing agent in one. It is a sign of health. A cockatiel that stops producing powder is a cockatiel that is unwell.

It also goes absolutely everywhere. Within a few weeks of bringing one home you will find a pale film on the top of the television, on the picture frames, on the inside of lampshades, and in a ring on the floor around the cage. It settles on everything within six feet and drifts a good deal further. No amount of cleaning stops it, because the bird makes more every day for fifteen years.

This matters for two reasons. The first is housekeeping, and it is solvable: an air purifier with a true HEPA filter running near the cage removes most of the airborne fraction, a weekly wipe-down handles the rest, and a damp cloth works far better than a dry one because dry dusting just puts it back in the air.

The second reason is health, and it is worth knowing before you commit. People with asthma, chronic bronchitis or existing respiratory sensitivity can react badly to powder down. There is a documented occupational condition in bird keepers, and while it is uncommon and usually associated with large cockatoo collections in poorly ventilated rooms, it is real. If anyone in the house has significant lung disease, spend an hour in a room with a cockatiel before you buy one.

Fun fact: Only three groups of parrots make powder down — cockatiels, cockatoos and African greys. If you have ever stroked a cockatiel and then noticed your dark shirt had a faint gray handprint on it, that is not dirt. That is the bird's own waterproofing.

Night frights: the injury that happens at 3am

This is the one that catches new owners completely off guard. A cockatiel in total darkness can be startled by a car headlight sweeping a wall, a creaking pipe, or nothing identifiable at all, and the response is not to freeze but to explode — thrashing around the cage at full speed in the dark, hitting bars, crashing into toys, and breaking blood feathers or worse. Owners typically wake to a sound like someone shaking a box of cutlery.

Why cockatiels specifically is not fully settled, but the leading explanation is their wild history. They are a flock bird of open inland Australia, where a night predator means the whole flock bursts upward at once and sorts itself out in the air. In a two-foot box there is nowhere to burst to.

The fix is almost embarrassingly simple: a night light. A small, dim light near the cage — not bright enough to disturb sleep, just enough to see by — gives the bird a visual reference the instant it wakes, and the panic generally does not start. Most owners who fit one never have another episode.

Two other things help. Site the cage away from a window where headlights sweep, and leave the cage uncovered or only partly covered — a fully covered cage is darker, not safer, and it removes every landmark the bird could use.

After a night fright

Check for this, in daylight

  • Blood feathers — a growing feather has a live blood supply. A snapped one bleeds freely and will not stop on its own.
  • Scuffed cere or beak — from hitting bars face-first. Usually superficial.
  • A wing held low or reluctance to perch. Both mean a vet, today.
  • Blood on the cage bars or paper — follow it to its source rather than assuming it is minor.

Stop it happening again

  • A dim night light, left on all night. The single most effective change.
  • Move the cage away from windows and away from a door that opens late.
  • Uncover, or half-cover. Total darkness is the problem, not the solution.
  • No mirrors or swinging toys at the sleeping end, which are what a panicking bird hits first.

Is it a male or a female?

A bright yellow face with a clean orange cheek is the classic adult male normal gray. A hen's face stays mostly gray and her cheek patch is duller.

On a normal gray cockatiel, sexing is straightforward once the bird is adult. After the first molt at around six to nine months, the male's face turns bright yellow or white and his cheek patch becomes a vivid orange. The hen keeps a mostly gray face with a muted cheek, and she retains barring — fine yellow stripes — on the underside of her tail feathers, which the male loses.

Behavior gives it away too, and often earlier. Males whistle, chatter, and perform: a repeated two-note call, wolf-whistles picked up from the household, a strutting walk with the crest half-raised and the wings held out in a heart shape. Hens are far quieter, and mostly produce a single plaintive contact call. A bird that learns to whistle the first bars of a tune is almost certainly male.

Color mutations break all of it. Lutino, albino, pied and whiteface birds may show none of the usual markers, and a pied bird can have a patchy face that reads as neither. For these, behavior is the better guide — and if it genuinely matters, a DNA test from a single plucked feather or a drop of blood costs around twenty dollars and settles it in a week.

Fun fact: A cockatiel that wolf-whistles almost certainly learned it from a person, and almost certainly is a male. Hens can learn sounds but very rarely bother. If your "Charlotte" has started whistling the theme from a television advert, it may be time to reconsider the name.

Taming a cockatiel, in the order that works

Cockatiels tame more readily than almost any other parrot, but the process is one of removing fear rather than adding trust, and it cannot be hurried. A hand-reared baby may step up within days. A parent-reared or shop-raised adult may take two months. Rushing either produces a bird that bites, and a cockatiel bite from a frightened adult draws blood.

  1. Leave it completely alone for three days. Food, water, clean paper, quiet conversation from across the room. Nothing else. A newly moved bird is dealing with more change than it has ever met, and hands at this stage only confirm that the big animal is dangerous.
  2. Sit beside the cage and do something boring. Read aloud, work, talk to it. Twenty minutes, twice a day, with your body turned slightly away. You are teaching it that your presence predicts nothing bad.
  3. Rest a hand against the outside of the cage and leave it there. Do not move it. When the bird stops watching it and goes back to preening or eating, that session has succeeded.
  4. Offer millet through the bars. Spray millet is the universal currency — almost no cockatiel refuses it. Hold the end and let the bird come to the far end. Do not move toward it.
  5. Open the door and offer millet on a flat palm resting on the cage floor. Still no reaching, still no grabbing. Let the bird make every approach.
  6. Introduce the step-up. Press a finger gently against the lower chest, just above the legs, and say "step up". The pressure tips the bird's balance so that stepping on is easier than staying put. Never pull a bird off a perch by the feet.
  7. Practice step-up inside the cage until it is automatic, then move to a perch outside it. A bird that will step up reliably is a bird you can get down from a curtain rail at midnight, which is the whole point.

Why millet works when nothing else does

Spray millet is the one food almost no cockatiel can ignore, and it has a practical advantage over seeds in a bowl: it is long. The bird can take a grain from one end while your hand is still six inches away at the other, which means the first hundred positive interactions happen at a distance the bird is comfortable with — and that comfort is the thing you are actually building.

Keep it as a training reward rather than a cage fixture. A spray left in the cage all day is a seed binge, and it stops being a reward the moment it becomes free.

Hens and the egg problem

A pet cockatiel hen with no mate will often lay eggs anyway. One clutch is normal and harmless. The problem is that cockatiels are opportunistic breeders — in the wild they breed whenever conditions say food is plentiful — and a centrally heated house with twelve hours of light and a full bowl says exactly that, all year round. So a hen can lay clutch after clutch, and that is a serious welfare issue.

Each egg costs her an enormous amount of calcium. Lay enough of them without a break and the calcium comes out of her bones, which leads to weak shells, then to an egg that cannot be passed. Egg binding is a genuine emergency: a hen straining, fluffed, sitting on the cage floor and unable to pass an egg will die within a day or two without veterinary help.

Egg binding — go now, not tomorrow

A hen who is sitting on the floor of the cage, fluffed up, tail pumping, straining, possibly with a swollen abdomen or a dropped wing, is an emergency. Calcium deficiency makes the muscle contractions too weak to move the egg, and a retained egg presses on the kidneys and the single functional ureter.

Do not attempt to remove it. Keep her warm — around 85°F — and humid, and get to an avian vet the same day. Treated early, the outcome is usually good. Left overnight, often not.

Stopping chronic laying is about removing the signals, not the eggs. Taking eggs away makes it worse, because the hen simply replaces them. Instead:

  • Cut daylight to ten hours or less. Cover the cage or move her to a dark quiet room for fourteen hours. This is the single most effective lever, because day length is the primary breeding trigger.
  • Remove anything nest-shaped. Huts, tents, hammocks, cardboard boxes, the dark gap behind the sofa. A happy hut is a nest site as far as she is concerned.
  • Stop stroking her back and tail. That is courtship behavior from her point of view, not affection. Head and neck only.
  • Rearrange the cage. Moving perches and toys makes the territory feel unsettled, which discourages nesting.
  • Leave the eggs she has laid until she loses interest, usually two to three weeks. Replacing them with dummy eggs works too.
  • Cuttlebone always available. Not optional for a laying hen.

If none of that stops her, an avian vet can use a hormone implant. That is a real and effective option, not a last resort, and it is far better than letting a hen lay herself into osteoporosis.

Fun fact: Wild cockatiels are nomads rather than migrants — they do not follow a route, they follow rain. A flock will travel hundreds of miles across inland Australia to wherever the grass has seeded, and breed there. That is why day length and food availability flip the breeding switch so easily in a centrally heated house.

Molting: the scruffy six weeks

Twice a year, roughly, a cockatiel replaces its feathers. For a few weeks it will look moth-eaten, be short-tempered, sleep more, and be covered in what look like little white drinking straws — pin feathers, each one a new feather growing inside a keratin sheath.

Pin feathers on the head and neck are the only ones the bird cannot open itself, which is why a molting cockatiel will suddenly become very keen on having its head scratched. Gently rolling a sheathed pin between finger and thumb to crack it is a genuine favor. Never touch one that is still fully blue or dark at the base — that is blood supply, and breaking it bleeds.

Molting is metabolically expensive. Protein demand roughly doubles, so this is when a seed-only diet shows up as a bird that molts for three months instead of six weeks, or produces feathers with visible stress bars across them. Egg food, a little cooked egg, or a good pellet carries them through it.

Bird-proofing the room

A cockatiel needs three to four hours out of the cage a day. That is not a luxury item on the list — a bird confined for fifteen years develops behavioral problems, and the plucking and screaming that people describe as "my bird went strange" nearly always traces back to a cage it never leaves.

But a loose cockatiel in an ordinary room is surrounded by things that kill birds, and most of them are not obvious.

Before the bird comes out

The fast killers

  • Windows and mirrors — a bird at speed does not see glass. Close the blinds.
  • Open water — a toilet, a washing-up bowl, a full sink, a mug of tea. A wet cockatiel cannot climb out.
  • The stove — hot rings, open pans, and the PTFE problem above. The kitchen is simply off limits.
  • Ceiling fans, obviously, and leave them off for an hour beforehand so the blades have stopped.
  • Other pets — cat saliva carries bacteria that will kill a bird from a scratch that barely breaks the skin.

The slow ones

  • Scented candles, plug-ins, aerosols — the same lungs that make PTFE lethal make air fresheners a bad idea.
  • Houseplants — lily, philodendron, dieffenbachia, ivy and avocado are all a problem. Spider plant and areca palm are fine.
  • Avocado, chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, onion, apple seeds — all genuinely toxic to birds, not merely unsuitable.
  • Zinc and lead — cheap galvanized hardware, older paint, curtain weights, some costume jewelry.
  • Cigarette smoke, including the residue on hands. Wash before handling.

Out-of-cage time is where a cockatiel's personality appears. A bird that only ever sees the inside of a cage stays a decoration rather than becoming company.

Do

  • Buy the widest cage you can fit, and check bar spacing before anything else
  • Fit a dim night light from the first night
  • Convert to pellets slowly, weighing the bird twice a week
  • Keep cuttlebone available permanently, especially for a hen
  • Give three to four hours out of the cage, every day
  • Replace the dowel perches with natural branch of several diameters
  • Find an avian vet before you need one — a general small-animal practice often will not see birds
  • Weigh the bird weekly on a kitchen scale and write it down

Don't

  • Use non-stick cookware anywhere in the house
  • Give a happy hut, tent or anything else nest-shaped to a hen
  • Stroke a hen along the back or tail
  • Cover the cage completely and leave it in total darkness
  • Switch from seed to pellets overnight
  • Clip the wings of a bird that has not yet learned to fly and land
  • Keep a single cockatiel with no company and no out-of-cage time

One bird or two?

Cockatiels and budgies can share a room, but a budgie is faster, bolder and more inclined to pick a fight. If they are housed together it needs to be a large flight cage with two of everything.

A cockatiel is a flock animal and does not do well alone with no company at all. But "company" can be you. A single cockatiel in a household where someone is home most of the day, with several hours out of the cage, is a happy bird — and it will bond to people far more strongly than a bird that has another cockatiel to talk to.

That is the real trade-off, and it is worth being honest about before you buy a second one. Two cockatiels keep each other entertained, chatter to each other, and are much easier on your conscience if the house is empty all day. They are also noticeably less interested in you. If what you want is a bird that sits on your shoulder while you work, one bird is the answer.

If you do get a second, quarantine it for thirty days in a separate room, ideally with separate air. Psittacosis and avian bornavirus are both real, both can be carried without symptoms, and both are miserable. Thirty days and a vet check first is the standard, and skipping it is how people lose the bird they already had.

Budgies and cockatiels are often kept together and it can work, but it is not a default. A budgie is smaller, faster and much more willing to start something, and a cockatiel's response to being harassed is to sit there and take it. If you mix them, it needs to be a large flight cage, two of every food and water station, and a real willingness to separate them if the budgie turns into a bully.

A cockatiel does not show illness until it has no choice. In a wild flock the sick bird is the one the hawk takes, so hiding weakness is hard-wired — and by the time a pet bird looks unwell, it has usually been ill for a week or more. Weighing it every Sunday on a kitchen scale will tell you before anything else does.

Normal, and not normal

A well cockatiel

  • Bright, fully open eyes with no discharge
  • Smooth feathers held close to the body most of the day
  • Standing on one leg while resting, with the other tucked up
  • Grinding its beak quietly as it falls asleep — contentment, not pain
  • Active, vocal, and interested in what you are doing
  • Droppings in three parts: dark solid, white urates, a little clear liquid
  • A steady weight, week to week, between 80 and 120 g

Call an avian vet today

  • Sitting fluffed on the cage floor — the single most serious sign in any pet bird
  • Tail bobbing with each breath, or any open-mouth breathing or clicking
  • Weight down more than 10%, even if the bird seems fine
  • Droppings gone green, black, bloody or entirely liquid for more than a day
  • Discharge from nostrils or eyes, or matted feathers above the beak
  • Vomiting, or food stuck to the head feathers
  • A hen straining, or any sudden lameness
  • Any bleeding at all — a bird has about 8 ml of blood per 100 g
Fun fact: Beak grinding is the cockatiel equivalent of a cat purring. A bird settling down for the night will often grind its upper and lower mandible together in a soft crunching sound for several minutes. It means the bird feels safe enough to fall asleep in front of you, which from a prey animal is a genuine compliment.

Talking, whistling and the three-note problem

Cockatiels can learn words, but they are not good at it — the voice comes out thin and whispery, and most birds manage a handful of phrases at best. What they are extraordinarily good at is whistling. A male cockatiel will pick up a tune from the house and reproduce it with genuine accuracy, and will then repeat it several hundred times a day for the next decade.

That last part is the bit to think about carefully. Whatever you teach a young male, you are going to live with. A smoke alarm chirp, a microwave beep, or the ringtone of a phone you no longer own are all things cockatiels learn enthusiastically and never unlearn. Choose deliberately, and teach the whistle you actually want to hear.

Teaching is repetition and nothing else. Pick a short two or three note phrase, whistle it at the same times each day — during cage cleaning, when you come home — and wait. Most males start trying within a few weeks, usually badly, and refine it over months. Hens may never bother, and that is normal rather than a failure.

Questions people actually ask

Are cockatiels loud?

Moderate. They whistle, chirp and chatter rather than scream, and a cockatiel is considerably quieter than a conure, a cockatoo or even a pair of budgies in full flow. A male's contact call carries through a house but will not trouble neighbors in an apartment with normal walls.

There are two predictable noisy periods: first thing in the morning and around sunset, which is when a wild flock calls to regroup. Twenty minutes at each end of the day is normal and cannot be trained out. Persistent screaming outside those windows is usually boredom, loneliness or a bird that has learned screaming brings you into the room.

How long do cockatiels live?

Fifteen to twenty-five years is the realistic range for a well-kept bird, and the record is thirty-two. The average pet cockatiel manages about fifteen, and the difference is nearly all diet — seed-only birds die of fatty liver disease and vitamin A deficiency years before they should.

Plan for the whole span. A cockatiel bought for a ten-year-old will still be alive when that child finishes university, and rescues are full of birds whose owners did not think that far ahead.

Can a cockatiel live alone?

Yes, provided you are the flock. A single cockatiel in a house where someone is around most of the day, with three to four hours out of the cage, does very well and bonds far more closely to people than a bird with a cockatiel companion would.

A single cockatiel left alone in an empty house all day is a different proposition, and that is where plucking and screaming start. If the house is empty nine hours a day, get two.

Do cockatiels talk?

A little, badly, and mostly males. The voice is thin and whispery and most birds learn only a few phrases. Whistling is where they excel — a male will reproduce a tune with real accuracy and then repeat it for years, so choose what you teach with some care.

What cage size does a cockatiel need?

Minimum 24 inches wide by 24 deep by 30 high for one bird, with bar spacing no wider than 5/8 of an inch. Aim for 36 inches of width if you can, because width is what the bird actually uses — cockatiels fly horizontally, and a tall narrow cage is mostly unusable air.

A bonded pair needs 48 inches of width. Round cages are a poor choice at any size: no corners to retreat into and nothing for the bird to orient by.

Why does my cockatiel thrash around the cage at night?

Night frights, and they are specific to this species. Something startles the bird in complete darkness and it panics, crashing around the cage and sometimes breaking blood feathers. A dim night light near the cage fixes it in most cases, because the bird has a visual reference the moment it wakes.

Check the bird in daylight afterward for bleeding, a dropped wing or scuffing on the cere. Leaving the cage uncovered helps; covering it fully makes it worse.

My cockatiel is laying eggs and there is no male. Is that normal?

It is common and usually harmless once. Repeated clutches are not — each egg is a large calcium cost, and chronic laying leads to weak bones and eventually egg binding, which is fatal if untreated.

Reduce daylight to ten hours, remove anything nest-shaped including happy huts, stop stroking her back and tail, rearrange the cage, and keep cuttlebone available. Leave the eggs she has already laid until she loses interest rather than removing them. If it continues, an avian vet can fit a hormone implant.

Is my cockatiel male or female?

On a normal gray, after the first molt at six to nine months: males develop a bright yellow face and vivid orange cheeks and lose the barring under the tail; hens keep a gray face, duller cheeks and retain the barring. Behavior helps too — whistling, chattering and strutting are nearly always male.

Color mutations such as lutino, pied and whiteface hide all of those markers. For those, a DNA test from a plucked feather costs around twenty dollars and is the only reliable answer.

Is the dust really that bad?

Yes, and it never stops. Cockatiels produce powder down, a fine white powder that coats surfaces within about six feet of the cage permanently. A HEPA air purifier and a damp cloth manage it; nothing eliminates it.

It is a health question too. Anyone in the house with asthma or significant lung disease should spend time around a cockatiel before committing to one.

Can I keep a cockatiel with budgies?

Sometimes, but it is not a default. Budgies are smaller, faster and much more willing to start trouble, and a cockatiel's usual response to being harassed is to sit and take it. It needs a large flight cage, two of every food and water station, and a genuine readiness to separate them.

Quarantine any new bird for thirty days in a separate room first, whatever species it is.

What foods are toxic to cockatiels?

Avocado, chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, onion, garlic and apple seeds are all genuinely toxic, not merely unsuitable. Salt is a problem in small quantities because of the bird's size. Avoid anything from your own plate as a default.

Safe and useful: dark leafy greens, carrot, bell pepper, broccoli, sweet potato, squash, peas, cooked egg in small amounts, and fruit as an occasional treat.

Should I clip my cockatiel's wings?

Generally no, and certainly not before the bird has learned to fly and land properly as a fledgling. A bird clipped too young often never develops the coordination to land safely and injures itself for years afterward.

There are situations where a light clip is the lesser risk — an unavoidably hazardous home, or a bird that repeatedly flies into windows. It is a conversation for an avian vet, not a routine procedure, and a flighted bird in a properly prepared room is almost always the better outcome.

How much does a cockatiel cost to keep?

Expect $400 to $900 in the first year, with the cage being most of it, and $250 to $500 a year after that for food, toys, perches and a routine vet visit. Budget separately for the possibility of an emergency: avian medicine is specialized and an out-of-hours visit for egg binding or a broken blood feather is not cheap.

Find an avian vet before you need one. Many general small-animal practices will not see birds at all, and discovering that at nine on a Sunday night is the wrong time.

        Show the math

        Why the number on the bag is usually wrong

        Feeding charts on pet food packaging are built for one hypothetical dog: intact, moderately active, at the weight printed in the left-hand column. Neutering alone drops a dog's energy requirement by roughly 20 to 30 percent, and most pet dogs in the United States are neutered. Add a dog who gets one walk instead of three, and the gap between the chart and reality becomes a few hundred calories a day — which is how a Labrador quietly gains fifteen pounds over two years while their owner follows the instructions exactly.

        The veterinary approach is different. It calculates what the individual dog burns, then works backwards to a portion.

        The two-step formula this calculator uses

        Step one — resting energy. RER = 70 × (ideal body weight in kg)0.75. That exponent is the important part. Energy needs don't scale in a straight line with body size, because a small dog loses heat far faster relative to its mass. A 5 kg dog burns roughly 37 calories per kilogram at rest; a 40 kg dog burns about 22. Double a dog's weight and you don't double its calories, you add about 68 percent.

        Step two — the multiplier. RER only covers breathing, circulation and staying warm. Multiply it by a factor reflecting life stage, activity and neuter status to reach the daily energy requirement.

        Life stageLow activityModerate (~1 hr)High / working
        Puppy, under 4 months2.8 × RER3.0 × RER3.0 × RER
        Puppy, 4 months to adult1.8 × RER2.0 × RER2.2 × RER
        Adult1.4 × RER1.6 × RER2.0 × RER
        Senior1.2 × RER1.4 × RER1.6 × RER
        Weight loss (adult)1.0 – 1.2 × RER, calculated on ideal weight

        Intact adults add roughly 0.2 to their factor. Giant breeds run slightly below the formula's prediction and toy breeds slightly above, so the calculator applies a small breed-size correction on top.

        Vet tip

        Feed the dog you want, not the dog you have. If your dog is overweight, the calculation must run on their target weight — otherwise you are precisely maintaining the excess. This is the single most common mistake in home feeding, and it's why the body condition question sits above everything else in the tool.

        Where protein, fat and carbohydrate actually come in

        Macronutrient percentages don't set the portion size — calories do. But they matter in two ways the portion alone can't tell you.

        They let you estimate calories when the bag doesn't say. Using the modified Atwater factors — 3.5 kcal per gram of protein, 8.5 for fat, 3.5 for carbohydrate — you can reconstruct metabolizable energy from the guaranteed analysis. Carbohydrate isn't printed, so it's derived: 100 minus protein, fat, moisture, ash and fiber. That's exactly what the estimate mode above does.

        They tell you whether the food suits the dog. Percentages printed on a can and on a bag aren't comparable until you strip the water out, because a canned food at 78 percent moisture has almost four times as much dry food behind every printed percentage point. Convert both to a dry-matter basis and the comparison becomes honest:

        AAFCO minimum, dry-matter basisProteinFat
        Adult maintenance18%5.5%
        Growth and reproduction22.5%8.5%

        The calculator runs this conversion automatically whenever you use estimate mode, and flags anything that falls short for your dog's life stage.

        Format changes the portion, not the calories

        A dog's calorie requirement doesn't care what the food looks like. What changes dramatically is how many grams deliver those calories.

        Wet / canned~78%80–130 kcal/100 gRoughly 3–4× the weight for the same calories

        Air-dried~12%380–480 kcal/100 gSimilar to kibble, slightly richer

        This is why switching a dog from kibble to canned food using the same-sized scoop is one of the fastest ways to cause unintended weight loss — and why going the other way causes rapid gain.

        Why we push hydration

        Wet formats aren't just lower in calories per gram — they carry water into a dog who may not drink enough on their own. Combining a portion of wet or rehydrated food with a filtered water source is one of the cheapest ways to support kidney and urinary health across a lifetime, particularly in dogs who ignore a still bowl but will drink from a moving one.

        The bag price is not the food price

        Two bags on the same shelf, one at $45 and one at $65. Most shoppers see a $20 difference. What they can't see is that the $65 bag is more energy-dense — a smaller daily portion — and lasts eleven days longer, making it the cheaper food per day. Sticker price tells you almost nothing until you divide it by how long the bag actually lasts your particular dog.

        That's why the calculator asks for bag size and price. It converts the sticker into three numbers that are actually comparable:

        • Cost per day — the price divided by how many days the bag lasts at your dog's portion, not an average dog's.
        • Cost per 1,000 kcal — the only fair way to compare across formats. A can that is 78 percent water can be cheaper per pound than kibble and still cost three times more per calorie.
        • Days per bag — so you can reorder before it runs out instead of switching foods overnight and dealing with the stomach upset that follows.

        Run the tool once with your current food and once with the food you're considering. The comparison that matters takes about a minute.

        Weigh it, don't scoop it

        Measuring cups are the weakest link in the whole chain. The same cup of the same kibble varies by 20 to 30 percent depending on kibble shape, density and how firmly it settles. For a 60 lb dog that's a rounding error you might get away with. For a 10 lb dog it can be a fifth of the day's calories, every single day.

        A digital kitchen scale removes the problem entirely and takes about four seconds a meal. It is the highest-return purchase in dog nutrition, and it costs less than one bag of treats.

        Large and giant-breed puppies

        Growing large-breed puppies must not be fed to appetite. Rapid growth and excess calcium are established risk factors for hip dysplasia and other developmental orthopaedic disease, and the damage is permanent. Feed a food formulated for large-breed growth, keep the puppy lean enough that you can feel ribs easily, and treat any calculator — including this one — as a ceiling rather than a target. Discuss the growth curve with your veterinarian.

        Adjust, then adjust again

        No formula predicts an individual dog's metabolism perfectly; published equations carry an error range of roughly 20 percent in both directions. The calculation is the starting point. The dog is the data.

        • Weigh your dog every two weeks, at the same time of day.
        • Score body condition by hand at the same time — ribs should feel like the back of your knuckles, not like the back of your hand.
        • If the trend is going the wrong way, change the portion by 10 percent and hold it for another two weeks.
        • Cap deliberate weight loss at 1 to 2 percent of body weight per week. Faster than that costs muscle, not fat.
        • Recalculate after neutering, after any change in exercise routine, and when your dog crosses into their senior years.

        Frequently asked questions

        How many calories does my dog need per day?

        Start with 70 × (ideal weight in kg)0.75 to get resting energy, then multiply by a life-stage and activity factor between 1.2 and 3.0. A moderately active, neutered 45 lb adult lands near 1,000 kcal a day. The calculator above does the arithmetic and applies the breed-size and body-condition corrections.

        Should I measure in cups or grams?

        Grams, every time. Cup measurements vary by 20 to 30 percent depending on kibble density and packing. The tool gives cups as a secondary figure only when you supply a kcal-per-cup value, because that is the only situation where the number is trustworthy.

        Do I use current weight or ideal weight?

        Ideal. Feeding an overweight dog for the weight they carry today maintains that weight indefinitely. The calculator estimates ideal weight from the body condition you select, using the standard rule that each point above ideal on the nine-point scale represents about ten percent of excess body weight.

        How much of the diet can be treats?

        Ten percent of daily calories at most. Beyond that, the complete-and-balanced portion of the diet is diluted enough to distort the vitamin and mineral profile. Treats are the most common reason a carefully measured meal still produces weight gain — dental chews in particular are far more calorific than most owners assume.

        How many meals a day?

        Puppies under four months do best on four small meals, dropping to three until they finish growing. Most adults do well on two. Single large meals are associated with a higher risk of gastric dilatation-volvulus in deep-chested breeds, so if you have a Great Dane, Standard Poodle, Weimaraner or similar, split the ration and avoid vigorous exercise around mealtimes.

        How do I compare the cost of two dog foods fairly?

        Never by price per pound — moisture makes that meaningless across formats. Compare cost per day (bag price ÷ days the bag lasts at your dog's portion) or cost per 1,000 kcal (bag price ÷ total calories in the bag). Premium foods are often denser, so the daily portion shrinks and the real gap is far smaller than the shelf price suggests — sometimes it reverses entirely.

        When should I skip the calculator and call my vet?

        Pregnancy and nursing, any prescription or therapeutic diet, kidney or liver disease, diabetes, pancreatitis, and unexplained weight change all require an individualised plan. Sudden weight loss in a dog eating normally is a medical sign, not a feeding problem.

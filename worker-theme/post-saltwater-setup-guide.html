Saltwater has a reputation for being difficult, expensive and unforgiving, and about a third of that is true. It is more expensive than freshwater — meaningfully so, and mostly at the start. It is not, however, harder in any interesting way. The chemistry is the same nitrogen cycle you already know, with three extra numbers on top, and almost every failed first marine tank fails for one of four reasons that have nothing to do with difficulty and everything to do with impatience.

Those four are: filling it with tap water, measuring salinity with the wrong instrument, adding fish before the tank has cycled, and adding too many of them too quickly afterward. Avoid those and a modest reef tank is genuinely achievable as a first aquarium. This guide is the whole path — what to buy, in what order, what the numbers mean, and how long each stage actually takes.

That cost line is the one people flinch at, and it is worth being honest about. A saltwater tank is not a slightly pricier freshwater tank — the lighting alone, if you intend to keep coral, will cost more than an entire planted freshwater setup. But the running cost is modest, most of the spending is one-time, and buying the right thing once is dramatically cheaper than buying the cheap version twice, which is the actual expensive path.

🎉 Fun Fact #1

Sea water is not simply salty water. It contains roughly seventy elements in ratios that have been almost constant for millions of years, and reef organisms depend on a surprising number of them. This is why marine salt mix costs what it does and why table salt is not an alternative in any sense — you are not reproducing a flavor, you are reproducing a chemical environment. It is also why the standard advice for topping off evaporated water is to add fresh water, not salt water: the water evaporates and the salt does not.

💧 Water: The Mistake That Costs the Most

Start here, because it determines whether your first year is pleasant or a running battle with algae.

Tap water is not suitable for a marine tank, and the reasons stack up. Municipal supplies routinely contain phosphate, often deliberately added to inhibit pipe corrosion. They contain nitrate, silicate and dissolved metals including copper. Many are treated with chloramine, which standard dechlorinators break into ammonia. Individually these are minor; together, in a closed system where nothing leaves except through your water changes, they accumulate into persistent nuisance algae that no amount of scrubbing resolves.

What is in tap waterWhat it does in a marine tank
PhosphateThe single biggest driver of nuisance algae. Also inhibits coral calcification
NitrateStarts you partway to a problem before a single fish goes in
SilicateFeeds diatom blooms — the brown dust that coats everything
Copper and heavy metalsLethal to invertebrates at concentrations too low to taste
ChloramineDechlorinator splits it and leaves ammonia behind
Carbonate hardnessThrows off alkalinity before you have added any salt

The fix is RO/DI water — reverse osmosis followed by deionization, which strips essentially everything out and leaves you a blank slate to mix salt into. You have two options: buy a unit for $150 to $300 and make your own, or buy it by the jug from a local fish store for roughly 50 cents to a dollar a gallon. For a 30-gallon tank doing 15% weekly changes, buying works out at about $5 a month, so a unit pays for itself in two to four years. Start by buying, and get a unit when the trips become annoying.

And measure salinity with a refractometer, not a swing-arm hydrometer. This is a $25 decision that causes an outsized share of beginner losses. Swing-arm hydrometers are affected by temperature, by bubbles clinging to the arm, and by salt creep on the pivot, and they commonly read 0.002 or more off — enough that a tank you believe is at 1.025 is actually at 1.021, and everything in it is slowly stressed. Calibrate the refractometer with a proper calibration fluid, not tap water.

Reef tank or fish-only? Decide before you buy lights

It is the one choice that changes the budget substantially.

FOWLR — fish only with live rock

No coral, so no expensive lighting and no need to hold calcium, alkalinity and magnesium in a narrow band. A basic light that lets you see the fish is enough.

Far more forgiving of a wandering parameter, cheaper by several hundred dollars, and it opens up fish that a reef tank cannot keep — many angels and butterflies eat coral.

Good choice if you want marine fish, a lower budget, and less testing.

Reef — coral as well as fish

Needs reef-grade LED lighting, stronger and more varied flow, and stable alkalinity, calcium and magnesium. That means more testing and, eventually, dosing.

Start with soft corals and LPS — zoanthids, mushrooms, euphyllia, leathers. They tolerate the swings a new tank will have. SPS corals are a second-year project, not a first-month one.

Good choice if coral is the reason you want the tank at all. Most people find it is.

⚠️ The Thing That Kills Them

⚠️ Rushing, in its four usual disguises


Nothing in this hobby fails as reliably as impatience, and it shows up in four specific forms.
1. Adding fish before the cycle is finished. Ammonia and nitrite are just as lethal in salt water, and a marine fish costs three to ten times what a freshwater one does. Wait for 0 ammonia and 0 nitrite with nitrate present, confirmed by a test kit, not by how long it has been.
2. Stocking too fast afterward. The bacterial colony has sized itself to the bioload it has been fed. Add four fish in one afternoon and you get a second, smaller ammonia spike in an established tank. Add one fish every two to three weeks.
3. Chasing numbers. A beginner reads that alkalinity should be 9 dKH, measures 7.6, and doses hard to correct it. The swing does far more damage than the original reading ever would. Stability beats target, every time. Move any parameter slowly, and never adjust two at once.
4. Skipping quarantine. Marine ich is common, arrives on new fish looking perfectly healthy, and cannot be treated with copper in a tank containing invertebrates — the treatment kills every snail, crab, shrimp and coral you own. A $40 quarantine tub run for four weeks is the cheapest insurance in the hobby, and the alternative is often a full tank wipeout.

There is a fifth that belongs with these but deserves its own note, because it is invisible: topping off evaporation with salt water. Water evaporates; the salt it contained stays behind. Replacing that loss with more salt water raises salinity a little every day, and over a few weeks a tank quietly drifts to a level that stresses everything in it. Top off with fresh RO/DI water. Use salt water only when you are replacing water you actually removed.

🎉 Fun Fact #2

Reef-building corals are animals farming plants inside their own cells. Each polyp hosts microscopic algae called zooxanthellae, which photosynthesize and hand over as much as ninety percent of the sugars they produce. The coral supplies shelter and nitrogen; the algae supply food and nearly all of the color. It is why coral needs strong light despite being an animal, and why a stressed coral "bleaches" — it has expelled its algae, and what you are seeing is the bare white skeleton through transparent tissue.

Marine keeping adds three parameters to the freshwater set, and they are all connected. If you are running fish-only you can ignore the bottom three rows entirely.

The relationship between those last three is the piece that takes people longest to internalize. Magnesium is the referee. Calcium and carbonate ions in sea water want to combine and drop out of solution as chalk; magnesium interferes with that reaction and keeps both available. If magnesium is low, calcium and alkalinity become impossible to hold steady no matter how carefully you dose them — so when both are misbehaving, test magnesium before you touch either.

🔧 Week by Week, Start to Stocked

The single most useful thing to understand is that the timeline is not negotiable. The first fish goes in somewhere around week five, the first coral around month four, and no amount of equipment shortens either. Knowing that in advance is what makes the waiting tolerable.

  1. Week 0 — the dry build. Tank on a stand that can carry it — salt water weighs about 8.5 lb per gallon, so a 40-gallon setup is over 400 lb with rock and sand. Heater, powerhead and return pump fitted. Do not fill anything yet.
  2. Week 0 — rock and sand. One to one and a half pounds of rock per gallon, and an inch or two of aragonite sand. Dry rock is cheaper and pest-free but seeds slowly; live rock cycles the tank far faster and may bring hitchhikers, good and bad. Build the rockwork directly on the glass, with the sand poured around it, so nothing can be undermined and collapse later.
  3. Week 1 — mix and fill. Mix salt into RO/DI water in a separate container with a powerhead running, let it circulate 24 hours, and check it reads 1.025–1.026 before it goes anywhere near the tank. Never mix salt in the display.
  4. Weeks 1–5 — cycle it. Dose ammonia to 2 ppm, or drop in a raw shrimp and let it rot. Test every day or two. You are waiting for ammonia and nitrite to both read zero within 24 hours of dosing, with nitrate present. The principle is identical to fresh water, and our nitrogen cycle guide covers the mechanism in full.
  5. Week 5 — the ugly phase, and the clean-up crew. Expect a brown diatom film over everything. This is normal and it passes. Add a modest clean-up crew — a few snails and a hermit or two — and resist the urge to scrub.
  6. Week 6 — the first fish. One, hardy, after four weeks of quarantine. A clownfish or a small goby. Watch it for two weeks before you even think about the second.
  7. Weeks 8–16 — stock slowly. One fish every two to three weeks, each quarantined first. Alongside this, get into the weekly rhythm: test, 10–15% water change, top off with fresh water daily.
  8. Month 4 onward — the first coral. Start with soft corals and LPS: zoanthids, mushrooms, leathers, euphyllia. They tolerate the parameter swings a young tank still has. Give the tank a full year before attempting SPS.

💚 The one-sentence version

Everything in a marine tank goes better slowly, and the tank itself will tell you when it is ready — through a test kit, not a calendar. The hobbyists who succeed are almost always the ones who found the first two months boring and did nothing about it.

Two points that save beginners real money. And the anemone can wait. Clownfish sold with anemones will host perfectly happily in a coral, a powerhead or nothing at all, and anemones need light, flow and stability that a tank under a year old simply cannot deliver.

🎉 Fun Fact #3

Every clownfish is born male, and the largest fish in a group becomes female. They live in a strict size-ordered hierarchy: one breeding female at the top, one breeding male below her, and the rest non-breeding juveniles held small by social suppression. If the female dies, the breeding male changes sex and takes her place, and the largest juvenile matures into the new breeding male. This is why two clownfish in a tank get along and three usually do not — the third has nowhere to sit in the hierarchy.

The clean-up crew is the unglamorous part of a reef tank that does most of the day-to-day work. It is also routinely oversold: many stores will happily sell you eighty animals for a forty-gallon tank, which is far more than there is food for, and most will starve within a few months.

👀 A Tank That Is Settling In, and One That Is Not

The first six months, read correctly

Several alarming-looking things are completely normal. A few genuinely are not.

✅ Normal, leave it alone

  • Brown diatom film over sand and glass around weeks 4–8
  • A green algae phase after the diatoms, lasting weeks
  • Cloudy water for a day or two after a sand stir
  • Coralline algae appearing as purple-pink patches — this is good
  • Coral closing up at night or after a water change
  • Tiny white specks on the glass — copepods, a sign of health
  • A new fish hiding for its first few days

🚩 Act on these

  • Any ammonia or nitrite in a stocked tank
  • White spots on fish that look like grains of salt — marine ich
  • Rapid gilling or gasping at the surface
  • Coral bleaching — going white while tissue remains
  • Salinity that has drifted above 1.027 or below 1.023
  • Alkalinity swinging more than 1 dKH in a day
  • Cyanobacteria — slimy red-purple sheets that peel off in mats

The diatom and algae phases are the ones that cause people to quit, and they should not. A new tank has a surplus of nutrients and nothing established to consume them, so algae takes the opportunity. It is a stage, not a failure, and it resolves as the rock and sand mature. Using RO/DI water from the start shortens it considerably; scrubbing daily and doing huge water changes does not.

🎉 Four More Things Worth Knowing

🎉 Fun Fact #4

The purple-pink crust that spreads over rock and glass in a maturing tank is coralline algae, and it is a sign the system is settling rather than a problem. It is a calcifying red alga that lays down calcium carbonate exactly as coral does, which means it competes for the same alkalinity and calcium — and it is also one of the things that cements a natural reef together. Most reefkeepers spend their first year willing it to appear and every year afterwards scraping it off the front glass.

🎉 Fun Fact #5

Live rock is not rock that is alive. It is old coral skeleton, colonized — a porous calcium carbonate structure whose surfaces and interior hold bacteria, sponges, worms and microfauna. The porosity is the point: a pound of it carries an enormous internal surface area, and it functions as the tank's main biological filter. This is why a marine tank can run with no conventional filter at all, and why rinsing rock in tap water is as destructive as rinsing filter media in it.

🎉 Fun Fact #6

A pistol shrimp snaps its claw fast enough to boil water. The claw closes at around 60 mph and the pressure drop creates a cavitation bubble that collapses with a crack loud enough to hear through the glass, momentarily reaching thousands of degrees and emitting a faint flash of light. Colonies of them are among the loudest things in the ocean. In a home aquarium one will pair with a watchman goby: the shrimp digs and maintains the burrow, and the goby stands guard, keeping one fin on the near-blind shrimp as a tripwire.

🎉 Fun Fact #7

Marine ich is not the same organism as freshwater ich, and the difference matters. Cryptocaryon irritans has a life cycle that spends only part of its time on the fish — the rest is spent as a cyst on the substrate and then as free-swimming juveniles hunting for a host. This is why the spots seem to vanish and then return a week later, and why the only reliable treatment is one held for the full cycle in a separate tank. A display tank cannot be treated with copper without killing every invertebrate in it.

🗓️ The Routine, Once It Is Running

The day-to-day of a settled marine tank is far less work than its reputation suggests — about twenty to thirty minutes a week once the routine is habit. What matters is that it is a routine, because consistency is the entire mechanism by which a reef stays stable.

WhenWhatWhy
Daily, 2 minutesTop off evaporation with fresh RO/DI. Feed. Look at everythingSalinity drifts fastest through evaporation. The look is your early warning
Weekly, 20 min10–15% water change. Test salinity, nitrate, phosphate, alkalinityExports waste and replenishes trace elements in one action
WeeklyClean the front glass. Empty the skimmer cup if you run oneLeave the back and side glass — coralline is welcome there
Every 2 weeksTest calcium and magnesium. Rinse mechanical filter mediaThese move more slowly than alkalinity
MonthlyCalibrate the refractometer. Check heater against a second thermometerInstruments drift, and a stuck heater can empty a tank overnight
Every 6 monthsReplace test reagents. Inspect pumps for calcium buildupExpired reagents give confidently wrong readings

Water changes do two jobs at once, which is why they remain the backbone of marine keeping despite every piece of equipment invented to reduce them. They remove accumulated nitrate, phosphate and organics, and they replenish the trace elements that corals and coralline algae consume from the water. A skimmer only does the first. Dosing only does the second. A regular 10 to 15% change does both without you having to understand which element ran short.

Mix the replacement water at least 24 hours ahead in a dedicated container with a powerhead running, bring it to tank temperature, and confirm the salinity with the refractometer before it goes in. Water that is colder, saltier or fresher than the tank turns a maintenance task into a stress event.

On feeding: less than you think, more often than you think. Most marine fish do better on two small feedings a day than one large one, and the measure of "small" is that everything is eaten within about a minute with nothing reaching the sand. Frozen food — mysis, brine, a marine blend — rinsed before it goes in, since the pack liquid is concentrated phosphate. A tank with persistent nitrate and phosphate problems is nearly always an overfed tank rather than an underfiltered one.

🎉 Fun Fact #8

Corals fight each other, slowly and chemically. Many species extend sweeper tentacles several inches at night to sting neighbors, and others release compounds into the water to inhibit growth nearby — a phenomenon called allelopathy. Two corals placed six inches apart may look fine for months and then one begins visibly receding. It is why reefkeepers plan placement with mature size in mind, why running activated carbon is standard practice in a mixed reef, and why an unexplained decline in one coral is often about its neighbor rather than your water.

One last habit worth building early: write the numbers down. A simple log of the weekly tests turns a set of isolated readings into a trend, and a trend tells you things a single reading never will — that alkalinity has been falling half a point a week as the corals grew, or that nitrate has been climbing since you added the last fish. Nearly every problem in a reef tank is visible in the log weeks before it is visible in the tank.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Is saltwater really much harder than freshwater?

Not harder, but less forgiving of impatience and more expensive to get wrong. The biology is the same nitrogen cycle with three extra parameters on top. What genuinely differs is that the animals cost more, the margin for a chemistry mistake is smaller, and everything takes longer. If you can already keep a stable planted freshwater tank, you have most of the skills.

What is the smallest tank I should start with?

Twenty gallons. Smaller nano tanks look appealing and are genuinely harder, because a small water volume means every change — evaporation, a dead snail, a missed water change — moves the parameters further and faster. Counterintuitively, a 40-gallon tank is easier to keep stable than a 10, and forgiving of a beginner's mistakes in a way a nano is not.

Can I really not use tap water?

You can fill a fish-only tank with heavily conditioned tap water and some people do. For a reef it is a persistent handicap: the phosphate, silicate and nitrate it carries feed algae continuously, and heavy metals accumulate. Buying RO/DI by the jug costs a few dollars a month at nano scale, which is a small price for removing the most common cause of a first-year algae battle.

How long before I can add the first fish?

Three to six weeks, and the test kit decides, not the calendar. You are waiting for the tank to process 2 ppm of ammonia to zero ammonia and zero nitrite within 24 hours, with nitrate present. Live rock and bottled bacteria speed it up; dry rock is slower. Then one fish, quarantined for four weeks first, and another only after two or three more weeks.

Do I need a protein skimmer?

Under about 40 gallons, not if you are consistent with water changes. A skimmer removes dissolved organic compounds before bacteria turn them into nitrate, which matters more as stocking and feeding increase. Treat it as an upgrade you add when the tank is full rather than a day-one purchase.

Why is my brand new tank covered in brown dust?

Diatoms, and every new marine tank goes through it, usually around weeks four to eight. They are feeding on silicate and excess nutrients that nothing has established to consume yet. It passes on its own as the rock and sand mature. Using RO/DI water from the start makes the phase noticeably shorter; scrubbing daily does not help and large panic water changes make it last longer.

My alkalinity reads 7.5 and it should be 9. What do I do?

Very little, and slowly. A stable 7.5 is better for your corals than a 9 you reached in an afternoon — swings cause far more harm than a reading slightly outside the ideal band. If you want to raise it, move no more than about 0.5 dKH per day, change one parameter at a time, and check magnesium first, because low magnesium makes both alkalinity and calcium impossible to hold steady.

Can I keep a clownfish without an anemone?

Yes, and for a first tank you should. Anemones need light, flow and stability that a tank under a year old cannot provide, and a wandering anemone can get into a powerhead and foul the whole system. Captive-bred clownfish have never seen an anemone and will host in a coral, a powerhead or nothing at all without any sign of distress.

What is the running cost once it is set up?

Roughly $25 to $60 a month for a 30-gallon: salt, RO/DI water, food, replacement test reagents and electricity. Lighting and pumps are the main power draw. The large spending is front-loaded, so the ongoing cost is much closer to a freshwater tank than the setup cost would suggest.

What should I do first, today?

Decide reef or fish-only, because it sets the budget, then buy the refractometer and the test kits before the tank. Getting your instruments before your animals is the habit that separates the tanks that work from the ones that do not — and if you have not cycled an aquarium before, read our nitrogen cycle guide first, since that process is identical in salt water and is where most of the waiting happens.

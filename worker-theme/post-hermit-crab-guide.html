Almost everything sold to you with a hermit crab is wrong. The wire cage cannot hold humidity, and a crab that cannot keep its gills moist suffocates over weeks. The inch of colored gravel in the bottom is not deep enough to molt in, and a crab that cannot molt dies. The painted shell it arrived in was forced onto it after its own shell was destroyed. The little sponge is a bacterial culture. The pamphlet says two years; the animal in your hand can live past thirty.

That is not an exaggeration for effect — it is the actual gap between how land hermit crabs are sold in the United States and what they need. They are marketed as a cheap starter pet and they are in fact a long-lived, social, burrowing tropical invertebrate with specific humidity, temperature and substrate requirements. The good news is that every one of those requirements is easy and cheap to meet once you know it. This guide covers the setup that works, the products that quietly kill them, and the single behavior — molting — that causes most first-year deaths.

Read that lifespan line again, because it reframes everything else. A hermit crab is not a six-month pet that happens to be cheap. It is a commitment closer to a parrot than a goldfish, and the reason so few reach even five years is almost entirely husbandry rather than anything inherent to the animal.

🎉 Fun Fact #1

Hermit crabs are not crabs. They belong to a separate group, the Anomura, and their closest well-known relatives are actually squat lobsters and porcelain crabs. The giveaway is the abdomen: a true crab has a short, hard, folded tail tucked under its body, while a hermit crab's is long, soft, asymmetric and spiraled clockwise — shaped, over millions of years, to fit the inside of a snail shell. It is not that they found shells convenient. Their bodies evolved around them.

🌡️ Humidity and Heat: The Two Numbers That Decide Everything

Land hermit crabs breathe through modified gills. Not lungs — gills, held in a chamber under the back of the shell, which must stay damp to exchange oxygen at all. This single fact explains most of what follows.

At 70–85% relative humidity the gills work. Below about 65% they begin to dry, and the crab slowly asphyxiates over a period of weeks while appearing, to an owner, to simply become less active and then stop moving. It is the most common cause of death in pet hermit crabs and it is almost never recognized, because nothing about it looks like suffocation.

Buy a digital hygrometer and thermometer before you buy anything else. The analog dial gauges packaged with starter kits are frequently 15 to 20 points out, which is the entire difference between a tank that works and one that is quietly killing its occupants. A digital combination unit costs about ten dollars and is the highest-value purchase in this entire guide.

Getting humidity up, and keeping it there

The order matters — most people try step three first and wonder why it does not hold.

What actually works

Seal the top. A glass lid, or a screen lid covered with plastic sheeting. Nothing else matters until the humidity has somewhere to stay. Leave a small gap for air exchange, not a whole open face.

Damp the substrate. Mixed to the consistency of sandcastle sand — it should hold a tunnel shape when squeezed, not drip. A six-inch bed of correctly damp substrate is itself the main humidity source.

Add surface water. Two open dishes plus a moss pit — a small dish of damp sphagnum moss — will carry you the last ten points.

What does not

Misting. A spray bottle raises humidity for about twenty minutes and then it falls back. Misting into an unsealed tank is bailing out a boat with a hole in it.

A water dish alone. In an open-topped tank a dish evaporates into the room, not into the enclosure.

Heat lamps. They dry the air aggressively and give hermit crabs nothing they need. These animals are nocturnal and do not bask. Use a side-mounted mat.

A bottom-mounted heat mat, which is worse than useless: a crab that buries to molt in warm sand cannot escape and dies underground.

⚠️ The Thing That Kills Them

⚠️ Never dig up a buried crab


A hermit crab that has vanished has almost certainly buried itself to molt, and it must be left completely alone. Molting can take anywhere from two weeks to six months underground for a large crab, and during it the animal sheds its entire exoskeleton, eats the old one to reclaim the calcium, and hardens a new one.
A molting crab is soft, blind and completely defenseless. Digging one up to check on it will usually kill it — either directly, through damage to the soft body, or by interrupting the process partway. This is the single most common way a well-meaning owner destroys a healthy animal.
What it looks like when they are about to molt: lethargy for days beforehand, digging and re-digging, an ashy or dull gray cast to the skin, cloudy eyes, a noticeably slack or drooping body, and sometimes a soft grayish lump under the abdomen — a water sac stored for the molt. Eating much more than usual is also common.
What to do: nothing. Mark the spot, keep the humidity and temperature stable, do not dig, do not move the tank, and do not remove the crab to an "isolation tank" once it has already started. If you keep several crabs together, a barrier pushed gently into the sand around the spot protects it from tankmates without disturbing it.
How to tell a molt from a death: a dead hermit crab smells, strongly and unmistakably, of rotting seafood within a day or two. A molting crab does not smell at all. If there is no smell, it is alive. Wait.

The corollary matters just as much: the substrate has to be deep enough and damp enough to dig in. This is why the gravel in a starter kit is not a cosmetic shortcoming but a fatal one. A crab that needs to molt and has nowhere to do it will attempt a surface molt, which in a tank with other crabs usually means being eaten, and even alone has a much lower survival rate.

🎉 Fun Fact #2

Hermit crabs organize queues to swap shells, and biologists have a name for it: a vacancy chain. When a crab finds a shell too large for itself, it waits beside it rather than moving on. Others gather, and they arrange themselves in order of size, largest to smallest. When a big enough crab finally arrives and moves in, every crab in the line steps into the shell vacated by the one ahead of it, in sequence, within seconds. It is one of the very few examples of a non-human animal forming an orderly queue, and it means a single new shell can rehouse an entire colony.

🐚 Shells: Why the Painted Ones Are Not a Style Choice

A hermit crab's shell is not decoration and not really a house. It is part of its body plan — the soft spiraled abdomen has no armor of its own, and the shell also traps the water that keeps the gills functioning. A crab without a properly fitting shell dehydrates and is defenseless.

A crab will not leave a shell voluntarily just because you would prefer it somewhere else. That is the whole problem with painted shells. To get a crab into one, the animal has to be forced out of the shell it chose, and the methods used commercially include heating the shell until the crab abandons it, crushing the shell in a vice, and poking the animal out with wire. A significant proportion die in the process. Those that survive are then sold in a shell coated in paint that flakes into the tank and is chewed by an animal that eats its own molted exoskeleton.

Buying one is not neutral, because the market is the reason the practice continues. If you already have a crab in a painted shell, do not try to force it out — simply offer several attractive natural shells of the right size and it will usually move on its own within weeks.

What to offer: natural, unpainted shells with a round rather than slot-shaped opening for purple pinchers, in a spread of sizes from slightly smaller to noticeably larger than the shell each crab currently wears. Turbo shells are the classic choice. Rule of thumb: at least three spare shells per crab. Rinse new shells in dechlorinated water and boil nothing — heat can crack them.

🔧 Building the Tank, Start to Stocked

Unlike an aquarium there is no cycling period to wait out, so a hermit crab enclosure can be ready in an afternoon. What it does need is a couple of days running empty so you can confirm the humidity and temperature actually hold before any animal depends on them.

  1. Start with the right box. A 10-gallon glass tank is the minimum for two small crabs; a 20-gallon long is far better and costs little more secondhand. Glass, not mesh, and with a solid lid. Height above the substrate matters less than floor area, but you need room for six inches of bedding plus climbing space.
  2. Seal the lid. A glass lid is ideal. A standard screen lid works if you cover most of it with plastic sheeting or acrylic, leaving a small gap. Until this is done, nothing else you do about humidity will hold.
  3. Mix the substrate. Five parts plain play sand to one part expanded coconut fiber. Add dechlorinated water gradually and mix until it holds a shape when squeezed — sandcastle consistency, damp but not dripping. Too wet goes anaerobic and smells; too dry collapses a burrow.
  4. Fill to depth. Six inches minimum, and at least three times the height of your largest crab measured in its shell. This is the single most expensive-feeling step and the one that has the largest effect on survival.
  5. Mount the heat on a side wall. Above the substrate line, on the outside of the glass, with a thermostat set to hold 78–82°F. Never underneath the tank.
  6. Put in both water dishes. Two of them, deep enough for the largest crab to submerge completely and climb out of unaided — a sloped edge or a pebble ramp. One dechlorinated fresh water, one marine salt water mixed to the packet instructions.
  7. Add shells, climbing and cover. Three-plus spare shells per crab, cholla wood or cork bark to climb, and at least two hides. A dish of damp sphagnum moss helps humidity and they will sit in it.
  8. Run it empty for 48 hours. Watch the gauges across a full day-and-night cycle. You are looking for 75–85°F and 70–85% humidity, holding steady without intervention. Adjust before the crabs arrive, not after.
  9. Introduce the crabs and leave them be. Expect them to bury almost immediately and stay down for days or even weeks — the stress of transport commonly triggers a molt. This is normal. Do not dig.

💚 The one-sentence version

Seal the lid, fill it six inches deep with damp sand, heat it from the side, and then leave the animals alone. Nearly every hermit crab death in captivity traces back to one of those four being wrong.

💧 Two Dishes, and Why Both Are Mandatory

This is the detail most starter guides get wrong, and it is not a refinement. Land hermit crabs need access to both fresh water and salt water at all times, and they use them for different things: fresh water for drinking and for the water they carry in the shell, salt water for the mineral balance their gills and molting depend on. Offer only fresh and you will see them lethargic and molting poorly over a period of months.

Two specifics worth being exact about. Dechlorinator matters because they breathe through gills — chlorine and chloramine burn gill tissue directly, and a conditioner that removes both is a couple of dollars. Avoid formulations with added slime coat, which is aimed at fish and does nothing useful here. And marine salt is not interchangeable with table salt: marine mixes supply roughly seventy trace elements, while table salt is sodium chloride plus an anti-caking agent.

🎉 Fun Fact #3

Hermit crabs carry a personal water supply inside the shell. Tucked behind the abdomen is a reservoir of water the crab tops up at the dishes and uses to keep its gill chamber wet between visits. It is why a crab needs a dish deep enough to fully submerge in rather than sip from, why a badly fitting shell is genuinely dangerous rather than merely uncomfortable, and why you will sometimes see a crab tip slightly forward and release a dribble of water when it changes shells — it is decanting.

🍽️ Diet: Far More Varied Than the Packet Suggests

Wild land hermit crabs are opportunistic scavengers eating fallen fruit, decaying wood and leaf litter, carrion, algae and washed-up material. In captivity the commercial pellet sold alongside them is usually the worst thing in the tank, and frequently contains preservatives — ethoxyquin and copper sulfate chief among them — that are poor choices for an invertebrate.

A good diet is mostly things already in your kitchen, offered in tiny quantities and rotated:

Two practical rules. Remove fresh food within 24 hours, because a warm 80% humidity tank turns fruit to mold fast. And keep calcium permanently available — a crab preparing to molt will eat a surprising amount of cuttlebone in the weeks beforehand, and the shell it builds afterwards depends on it.

👀 A Healthy Crab and a Crab in Trouble

What to look for after dark

They are nocturnal, so daytime tells you almost nothing.

✅ A crab that is fine

  • Active at night — climbing, digging, visiting both dishes
  • Firmly in its shell, carried up off the ground
  • Eating and drinking, with food visibly disappearing
  • Bright, extended eyestalks that track movement
  • Burying for weeks and re-emerging larger, with a shed exoskeleton
  • Shell swapping, sometimes repeatedly and noisily
  • Chirping — a genuine sound they make, and not a problem

🚩 Something is wrong

  • Hanging out of the shell or abandoning it entirely on the surface
  • Lethargic at night as well as during the day
  • Dropping legs or claws — a stress response to bad air, heat or chemicals
  • A strong rotten-seafood smell, which means a death, not a molt
  • Surface molting in the open with other crabs present
  • Sitting permanently in the water dish, often a humidity problem
  • Mites — tiny moving specks around the eyes and mouth

Dropped limbs deserve a specific mention, because they alarm people and the cause is usually fixable. A hermit crab sheds legs under stress — most often heat that is too high, humidity that is too low, chlorine in the water, or fumes from paint, cleaning products or air fresheners in the room. The limbs regrow over the next one or two molts. Treat a dropped leg as an instrument reading: something in the enclosure is wrong, and it is worth checking the gauges and thinking about what has been sprayed nearby recently.

🦀 Species, and Why You Cannot Breed Them

Most crabs sold in the United States are Coenobita clypeatus, the purple pincher or Caribbean hermit crab, named for the large purple-tinged left claw. The second most common is Coenobita compressus, the Ecuadorian or E-crab, which is smaller, faster and generally regarded as more active. Less commonly you will see the Indo (C. brevimanus), strawberry (C. perlatus), and violascens. All have broadly similar requirements, though Ecuadorians want slightly more salt access and Indos prefer deeper substrate still.

Essentially every hermit crab in the pet trade is wild-caught, and this is worth knowing before you buy rather than after. Captive breeding is not commercially viable because of their life cycle: females release larvae into the ocean, where the young drift as plankton through several stages over weeks before locating a tiny shell and coming ashore. Reproducing that at home requires marine larval tanks and a supply of microscopic live food, and while a handful of dedicated hobbyists have succeeded, it remains rare enough to be newsworthy.

🎉 Fun Fact #4

Hermit crabs chirp. The sound is real, is called stridulation, and is produced by rubbing ridges on the claw or leg against the shell — the same mechanism a cricket uses. They do it when handled, when competing over a shell, and sometimes for no reason a keeper can identify. New owners regularly assume the noise means distress; often it simply means two crabs are negotiating over real estate.

The practical consequence of wild capture is that a new crab has had a hard few weeks before reaching you: collected, shipped, held in a store tank that was probably too dry, and frequently forced into a painted shell. Expect a long settling-in period, expect an early molt, and judge nothing about the animal for the first month or two.

🕳️ The Molt Cycle, in Detail

Molting is the whole ballgame with hermit crabs. It is how they grow, how they regrow lost limbs, and it is when nearly every captive death happens. Understanding the sequence is what stops an owner from intervening at exactly the wrong moment.

  1. Pre-molt, one to three weeks. Eating noticeably more, especially calcium. The skin takes on a dull, ashy gray tone and the eyes look cloudy rather than glossy. Many crabs develop a soft grayish bulge on the left side of the abdomen — a stored water sac, and a reliable signal. Activity drops off.
  2. Digging in. The crab excavates, often making several attempts in different spots before committing. Once it has settled it will seal itself into a chamber. From this point on it is untouchable.
  3. The molt itself. The old exoskeleton splits and the crab withdraws from it entirely, including the lining of its gills and the covers of its eyes. It emerges pale, soft and completely helpless.
  4. Hardening, the long part. The new exoskeleton takes days to weeks to harden depending on size, and throughout this the crab eats its shed exoskeleton to recover the calcium. This is why the molt must be left in the tank.
  5. Resurfacing. The crab digs its way back up, visibly larger and much brighter in color, often with regrown limbs where it had dropped some. It will be hungry and will usually go straight to the water dishes.
  6. Total time underground. Two to four weeks for a small purple pincher, two to six months for a large one, and Indos can be down longer still. A crab that has been buried for three months is not a crisis.

The surface molt is the case that needs action. If a crab molts in the open rather than underground, it is either unable to dig — substrate too shallow, too dry or too compacted — or it was forced up by another crab. A surface-molting crab in a communal tank is in real danger, since tankmates will eat a soft one. Isolate it in place, with a plastic barrier pressed into the substrate around it rather than by moving the animal, and fix the substrate for next time.

One more thing that catches people: a crab frequently changes shells immediately after a molt, because it has just grown. If there is nothing better available it will squeeze back into the old one, and a shell that is now too small compromises both protection and the water reservoir. Having spares in a range of sizes is not a nicety — it is part of molt support.

🎉 Four More Things Worth Knowing

🎉 Fun Fact #5

The largest land hermit crab on earth is the coconut crab, which reaches three feet across the legs and can weigh nine pounds — the largest land-living arthropod there is. Adults outgrow shells entirely and harden their own abdomen instead. Their claws generate a grip force measured in the hundreds of newtons, comparable to the bite of a large mammal, and they are genuinely capable of opening a coconut. They are close relatives of the animals in your tank.

🎉 Fun Fact #6

Hermit crabs have been documented using discarded human rubbish as shells — bottle caps, broken glass necks, plastic fragments. A 2024 study reviewing wildlife photography found the behavior across most terrestrial hermit crab species worldwide. It is not adaptability to be celebrated: artificial shells offer worse protection and worse water retention, and the trend tracks the decline of the large sea snails whose shells they actually need.

🎉 Fun Fact #7

A molting crab eats its own shed exoskeleton, and this is why you should never remove it. The old shell is the cheapest and most bioavailable calcium the animal will ever have access to, and a crab that eats its molt hardens faster and more completely than one that does not. If you find a shed exoskeleton in the tank, leave it exactly where it is — and note that its presence, intact and with the crab gone, is the clearest confirmation you will get that a molt went well.

🎉 Fun Fact #8

Land hermit crabs are genuinely social and appear to recognize each other. In the wild they aggregate in groups of dozens to hundreds, travel together at night, and gather en masse around a newly available shell. Research on shell-exchange behavior shows they assess not only the shell but the crab holding it. This is the evidence behind the rule that you should never keep one alone: a solitary hermit crab is not a low-maintenance version of the same pet, it is an animal missing something it is built for.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

My crab has buried itself and has not moved for weeks. Is it dead?

Almost certainly not — it is molting, and you must leave it alone. Molting takes two weeks to several months underground depending on size, and digging the crab up will usually kill it. The reliable test is smell: a dead hermit crab produces a strong, unmistakable rotting-seafood odor within a day or two, and a molting one smells of nothing at all. If there is no smell, mark the spot, hold the humidity and temperature steady, and wait.

How long do hermit crabs actually live?

Twenty to thirty years or more with correct care, and the record for a pet purple pincher is over forty. The two-year figure in most care pamphlets is not a lifespan, it is an average survival time under the husbandry those pamphlets describe. If a crab dies at eighteen months, something in the enclosure was wrong — almost always humidity, substrate depth or heat placement.

Can I keep just one?

You can, but you should not. Land hermit crabs are colonial in the wild, living in groups of dozens and gathering together to exchange shells. A single crab is noticeably less active and less likely to thrive. Two is the practical minimum and three or four in a 20-gallon long is better.

Why did my crab drop a leg?

Limb loss is a stress response, not a disease. The usual causes are heat above the low 80s, humidity below about 65%, chlorine in the water, or airborne chemicals — paint, cleaning sprays, scented candles or air freshener used in the same room. Check both gauges and think about what has been sprayed nearby recently. The limbs regrow over the next one or two molts.

Do they really need saltwater as well as fresh?

Yes, and it is one of the most commonly skipped requirements. They use fresh water for drinking and the shell reservoir and salt water for the mineral balance their gills and molting depend on, and they will move between the two deliberately. Use marine aquarium salt mixed to the packet instructions, never table salt, and dechlorinate the water before you add the salt.

Can I take my crab out to handle it?

Briefly and rarely. Some crabs tolerate gentle handling with the shell supported from underneath; many do not and will pinch, which is a defensive response rather than aggression. Never pull on the shell — a crab holds on hard enough to injure itself rather than let go. Sitting with your hand flat in the tank and letting a crab walk onto it is a much better approach than lifting one out.

Is sand or coconut fiber better?

Both, mixed. Roughly five parts plain play sand to one part expanded coco fiber gives a substrate that holds a burrow without collapsing and holds moisture without going swampy. The consistency you want is damp sandcastle sand. Avoid calcium sand entirely — it sets hard when wet and can trap a molting crab.

What temperature and humidity exactly?

75–85°F and 70–85% relative humidity, both held steady day and night. The humidity figure is the one people miss, and it is not a comfort preference: land hermit crabs breathe through modified gills that must stay damp, so a dry tank suffocates them slowly over weeks. Use digital gauges, not the dials that come in starter kits.

My crabs are fighting over a shell. Should I intervene?

Usually not. Shell competition is normal and mostly consists of shoving and the chirping sound they make by stridulation. Add more shells in a range of sizes and the pressure resolves itself. Intervene only if one crab is persistently harassing another over days, or if a crab has been pulled from its shell, in which case offer several empty shells immediately and separate them if it continues.

Where should I buy them?

Wherever you buy, the animal will be wild-caught — commercial captive breeding essentially does not exist because the larvae develop in the ocean. Given that, prefer a seller keeping them in a humid, deep-substrate tank with natural shells over one with a dry gravel display and painted shells, and expect any new crab to bury and molt within its first weeks as it recovers from collection and shipping.

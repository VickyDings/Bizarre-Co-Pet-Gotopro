      What to do next

        Show the math

        Why indoor cats gain weight on the “recommended” amount

        Feeding charts on cat food packaging assume an active, often intact cat. The cat actually eating the food is usually neutered, indoor, and spends eighteen hours a day asleep — a combination that cuts energy needs by 30 to 40 percent compared with the chart. Add free-fed kibble, which most cats will graze past their requirement, and you have the reason more than half of American cats are overweight.

        The fix isn't a special diet. It's calculating what your individual cat burns, and measuring meals against that number.

        The two-step formula this calculator uses

        Step one — resting energy. RER = 70 × (ideal body weight in kg)0.75. Same equation as dogs, and the exponent matters just as much: a 5 lb kitten burns far more per pound than a 15 lb Maine Coon.

        Step two — the multiplier. This is where cats part company with dogs. Cat factors are lower and the range is narrower:

        Intact adults add roughly 0.2. Indoor-only cats run slightly below the table and outdoor cats slightly above, so the calculator applies a small lifestyle correction. The practical upshot: a typical 10 lb neutered indoor cat needs only 200–250 kcal a day — about what's in two-thirds of a cup of many kibbles. Most owners are genuinely surprised by how small that is.

        Vet tip

        Feed the cat you want, not the cat you have. An overweight cat fed for its current weight stays overweight forever — the math has to run on target weight. But unlike dogs, cats must never be crash-dieted: slow is not just kinder, it's a safety requirement.

        Hepatic lipidosis — the cat-specific danger

        A cat that loses weight too fast, or stops eating for more than a day or two, can develop hepatic lipidosis (fatty liver) — a life-threatening emergency unique to cats. Cap weight loss at about 1% of body weight per week, make any food change gradually over 7–10 days, and treat a cat refusing food for 24–48 hours as a veterinary problem, not a standoff to win. A dieting cat must keep eating every single day.

        Wet, dry, or both — the hydration question

        Cats descend from desert hunters that got most of their water from prey, and the low thirst drive came along for the ride. Many cats on an all-dry diet simply never drink enough, and chronic mild dehydration is one of the quiet contributors to the kidney and urinary problems that dominate feline medicine in later life.

        Wet food fixes this mechanically: at roughly 78 percent moisture, every meal carries water into the cat whether it feels thirsty or not. A practical routine many households land on — and the one we recommend — is at least one wet meal a day plus measured dry food, with fresh filtered water always available. Cats that ignore a still bowl will often drink happily from a fountain; moving water reads as safe to a cat's instincts.

        Protein matters more for cats

        Cats are obligate carnivores. Their minimums are meaningfully higher than a dog's: AAFCO requires at least 26% protein on a dry-matter basis for adult maintenance and 30% for growth, versus 18% and 22.5% for dogs — plus taurine, an amino acid cats cannot make themselves, which every complete cat food must supply and dog food does not. This is also why cats can't live on dog food.

        When you use the calculator's estimate mode, it converts the label to a dry-matter basis — the only way to compare a can against a bag honestly — and flags anything below the cat minimums for your cat's life stage.

        The can price is not the food price

        Wet food looks expensive per can and cheap decisions get made on that impression. The honest comparison is cost per 1,000 kcal: divide what you pay by the calories in the bag or case. Wet food nearly always does cost more per calorie — but the gap is usually smaller than the shelf suggests, and a mixed routine (one wet meal, measured dry for the rest) buys the hydration benefit at a fraction of the all-wet price. The calculator's cost panel does this arithmetic for you; run it once for each food you're weighing up.

        Free-feeding: the habit to break

        Leaving the bowl topped up all day works for a minority of naturally lean cats and quietly overfeeds everyone else. Measured meals — two at minimum, three or more small ones ideally, since cats are natural grazers of many small prey — put you back in control of the number that matters. Puzzle feeders and lick mats slow a fast eater, add activity to an indoor cat's day, and make a modest portion feel like more work and therefore more food.

        Adjust, then adjust again

        • Weigh your cat every two weeks — same scale, same time of day.
        • Check body condition by hand: ribs should feel like the back of your knuckles under a thin glove.
        • If the trend is wrong, change the portion by 10% and hold for two more weeks.
        • Cap deliberate weight loss at ~1% of body weight per week — for a 14 lb cat that's about 1 oz a week. Slower is fine; faster is dangerous.
        • Recalculate after neutering, after any lifestyle change, and at each senior check-up.
        • Unexplained weight loss in a cat eating normally is a medical sign — hyperthyroidism, kidney disease and diabetes all present exactly this way, especially past age 10. That's a vet visit, not a bigger portion.

        Frequently asked questions

        How many calories does my cat need per day?

        70 × (ideal weight in kg)0.75, multiplied by 1.0–1.2 for a neutered indoor adult, up to 1.4 for intact or very active cats, and 2.0–2.5 for kittens. A 10 lb neutered indoor cat lands around 200–250 kcal — far less than most people expect, and the reason the calculator above exists.

        How many cans of wet food should I feed?

        Daily calorie budget ÷ calories per can. A 10 lb indoor cat needing ~220 kcal eats roughly two and a half 3-oz cans of a typical pâté, or one 5.5-oz can plus a topper. Can calories vary hugely by recipe — a gravy-style 3 oz can might be 60 kcal while a rich pâté is 110 — so use the number printed on your can, which is exactly what the can fields above are for.

        Is it safe to put my cat on a diet?

        Yes — slowly. No more than about 1% of body weight per week, and the cat must keep eating every day. Rapid loss or food refusal beyond 24–48 hours risks hepatic lipidosis, a life-threatening liver condition specific to cats. For an obese cat, do the plan with your vet from the start.

        Wet or dry food?

        Both have a job. Dry is dense, convenient and cheaper per calorie; wet carries water into a species that chronically under-drinks. Our standing recommendation: at least one wet meal a day, measured dry for the rest, filtered fresh water always down — and a fountain if your cat ignores a still bowl.

        Can I free-feed my cat?

        A minority of naturally lean cats self-regulate; most don't, and with over half of US cats overweight the odds aren't in your favour. Measured meals two to four times a day give you control of the calories, and puzzle feeders restore the "work for food" pattern indoor cats are missing.

        When should I skip the calculator and call my vet?

        Pregnancy and nursing, any prescription diet, kidney or urinary disease, diabetes, hyperthyroidism, an obese cat starting weight loss, and any cat losing weight while eating normally. Cats hide illness well — weight change is often the first visible sign.

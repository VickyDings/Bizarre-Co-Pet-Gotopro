Most bad home grooming is not clumsiness. It is using the wrong tool on the wrong coat, in the wrong order. A slicker brush that transforms a Cavalier will skate uselessly over a Labrador. The undercoat rake that pulls handfuls out of a Husky will tear a Maltese apart. And the single most common piece of "helpful" summer advice — shave the fluffy dog — permanently damages the coat it was meant to cool.

So this guide starts where it should: with your dog's coat type. Work out which of the eight you have, and everything else — the brush, the shampoo, how often to bath, whether the clippers come out at all — follows from it. Then we walk the whole home groom, step by step, including the parts people rush: drying, nails, and knowing when a mat has stopped being your job.

Brush, then bath, then dry — in that order. Reverse any two of them and you make the job harder and the dog less willing.

Step one: find your dog's coat type

Run your fingers backwards through the coat, against the lie of the hair, and look at what is underneath. If you find a second, softer, denser layer close to the skin, your dog is double-coated. If the hair is one texture all the way down, it is single-coated. That one distinction changes almost everything that follows.

Fur and hair are not the same thing

Which one your dog grows decides whether it sheds or needs haircuts.

Fur — grows to a set length, then sheds

Most dogs. Each hair grows to a genetically fixed length, rests, dies and falls out, and is pushed out by the next one. That is why a Labrador never needs a haircut and why it covers your sofa twice a year.

A fur coat should be brushed out, not cut. Removing the dead undercoat is the entire job.

Hair — grows continuously, barely sheds

Poodles, Bichons, Maltese, Yorkies, Shih Tzus and the Doodles. The growth phase does not stop, so the coat keeps getting longer, like ours.

Hardly anything falls out — which is why these breeds get called hypoallergenic, and also why the dead hair stays in the coat and turns into mats. These dogs need haircuts every 6–8 weeks, forever.

⚠️ Never shave a double-coated dog to cool it down

A double coat is insulation in both directions. The dense undercoat traps a layer of air that keeps heat out in summer just as it keeps warmth in through winter, and the coarse guard hairs block UV. Shave it and you remove the dog's sun protection and its cooling system at the same time.

Worse, it often does not grow back properly. The undercoat returns fast and woolly while the guard hairs return slowly or not at all, leaving a patchy, dull, permanently damaged coat — post-clipping alopecia, and it can be permanent in Spitz breeds especially.

The correct answer to a hot Husky is a thorough de-shed: rake the dead undercoat out until air can move through the coat again. That genuinely cools the dog. Shaving does the opposite.

Fun fact

Shedding is triggered by day length, not temperature. Dogs moult in response to changing daylight hitting the pineal gland — which is why a dog living under artificial indoor lighting all year sheds a steady, low-level amount forever instead of two clean seasonal blows.

The toolkit, and what each tool is actually for

You do not need everything. Most owners need three or four items, chosen by coat type. Here is what each one does, so you can tell which of them apply to you.

Brushes and combs

  • Slicker brush — fine bent wire pins on a flat pad. The workhorse for curly, silky and long double coats. Works through the coat, not over it. A cheap one with unprotected pin tips will scratch the skin; the tips should be smooth.
  • Undercoat rake — one row of long, rounded, widely spaced teeth. Reaches past the guard hairs and pulls out dead undercoat. The single most useful tool a double-coated dog owner can own.
  • Steel comb (the "greyhound comb") — coarse teeth one end, fine the other. This is your inspector, not your brush. If the comb will not pass from skin to tip anywhere on the dog, that area is not finished.
  • Pin brush — rounded-tip pins on a cushioned pad. Gentle; for silky coats and for fluffing after drying. It smooths the surface — it does not remove undercoat.
  • Bristle brush / hound glove — for smooth coats. Lifts loose hair and spreads skin oils to a shine.
  • Rubber curry — a rubber oval with nubs. Brilliant on short coats, and doubles as a shampoo-massager in the bath.
  • De-shedding blade — a fine-toothed blade that cuts as well as pulls. Effective, but easy to overuse: it damages guard hairs if you keep going after the undercoat is out. Use it occasionally, not weekly.
  • Mat splitter / dematting rake — hooked blades that cut a mat into strips you can then comb out. Sharp. Always cut away from the skin.
  • Stripping knife — for wire coats only. It grips dead hair so you can pull it; it should not be used as a cutting tool.

💚 The comb is the only honest tool in the box

Any brush will make a coat look finished while mats sit undisturbed underneath. A steel comb cannot lie. After brushing, run the comb from skin to tip across the whole dog — behind the ears, in the armpits, the groin, under the tail, between the toes. Those five places are where every mat starts.

If the comb snags, go back to the brush. If it still snags, you have a mat, not a tangle — see the mat section below.

⚠️ Clipper blades get hot enough to burn in under a minute

A metal blade running at speed heats fast, and the first sign of trouble is usually a red stripe on the dog's skin the next day. Touch the blade to the inside of your own forearm every two or three minutes. If you cannot hold it there comfortably, the dog cannot either.

Use coolant spray, or buy a second blade of the same size and swap them so one is always cooling. And never run clippers over a mat — the mat lifts the skin into a ridge and the blade goes through it.

Shampoos: which one, and why human shampoo is out

Dog skin sits at roughly pH 6.2–7.4 — close to neutral. Human skin is considerably more acidic, around pH 5.5, and our shampoos are formulated to match it. Put human shampoo on a dog and you strip the acid mantle that keeps bacteria and yeast in check. It does not burn and it does not look dramatic; it just leaves a dog that is dry, flaky and itching a week later, and it is the hidden cause of a great many "my dog has allergies" visits.

⚠️ Four things never to put on a dog

Human shampoo or conditioner — wrong pH, damages the skin barrier. Dish soap — only ever on veterinary instruction, for oil or chemical contamination, and never as a flea treatment. Undiluted tea tree oil, and shampoos heavy in it — genuinely toxic to dogs, causing tremors and weakness. Flea shampoo below the labelled minimum age or weight, and never a cat product on a dog or the reverse — permethrin is lethal to cats.

If your dog's skin is red, weeping, smelly, greasy or crusted, that is a veterinary problem, not a shampoo-shopping problem. Buying a medicated shampoo without a diagnosis is how a yeast infection gets treated for six weeks as dandruff.

The beginner's home groom, step by step

Do these in order. The order is not stylistic — each step makes the next one possible, and skipping one creates the problem the next step was meant to prevent.

  1. Brush the dog out completely, dry. Every tangle, everywhere, before any water. Water shrinks and tightens hair: a loose tangle that would have combed out in ten seconds becomes a solid felted mat the moment it gets wet, and then it has to be cut out. Never bath a dog you have not brushed.
  2. Comb-check, then trim the sanitary areas. Run the steel comb over everything. Then, with ball-tipped scissors, tidy the hair around the bottom, the belly, and the pads between the toes — long pad hair collects grit and makes floors slippery. Point scissors away from the body, always.
  3. Clip now if you are clipping. Clippers go through a clean, dry, brushed coat only. A dirty coat blunts blades fast, a damp coat drags, and a matted coat is a skin injury waiting to happen. Go with the lie of the hair, in long smooth passes, letting the clipper do the work rather than pressing.
  4. Bath: lukewarm water, and start at the neck. Wrist-test the temperature — dogs run warmer than us and what feels pleasantly warm to you is hot to them. Work backwards from the neck so fleas and suds move away from the face, not into it. Save the head for last and use a damp cloth, never the sprayer, around the eyes and ears.
  5. Lather, wait, and rinse far longer than feels necessary. Medicated shampoos need a timed ten minutes of contact. Ordinary shampoos need a couple. Then rinse until the water runs completely clear and the coat squeaks — leftover shampoo is the single most common cause of post-bath itching. Then rinse again, especially the armpits, groin and under the tail.
  6. Condition long and curly coats. Work it right down to the skin, leave it the labelled time, rinse. This is the step that decides how much brushing you do for the next three weeks.
  7. Towel, then dry properly. Squeeze rather than rub — rubbing a long coat tangles it. Then see the drying section below, because this is the step almost everyone gets wrong.
  8. Finish: nails, ears, teeth — then a proper reward. Do the fiddly bits last, when the dog is tired and compliant, and end every session with something genuinely good. The dog's memory of grooming is built from the last two minutes of it.

💚 The one rule that prevents most home-grooming disasters

Brush before the bath. Always. No exceptions. If a dog is too matted to brush out, it is too matted to bath — take it to a groomer instead. Water turns a brushable tangle into a felted mat that has to be shaved, and every professional groomer can tell you about a dog that arrived pelted because a well-meaning owner bathed it first.

Drying: the step everyone rushes

On a Whippet, towel-dry and let it finish in a warm room. On anything with an undercoat or a curly coat, air-drying is a mistake.

A dense undercoat left damp holds moisture against the skin for hours. That warm, wet, airless layer is exactly what a bacterial skin infection needs — which is why acute moist dermatitis, the hot spot, so often shows up two days after a bath. On a curly coat, air-drying is worse still: the hair shrinks back into its curl as it dries, and every hair that dries touching its neighbour locks in place. A Doodle left to dry on its own can go from clean to matted overnight.

Do

  • Use a high-velocity dryer — it blows water out rather than heating it off
  • Keep the nozzle moving; never hold it still on one spot
  • Start at the lowest speed and let the dog get used to the noise from a distance
  • Brush as you dry — "fluff drying" — on curly and long coats
  • Dry right down to the skin, especially chest, armpits and under the tail
  • Cotton wool in the ears if your dog tolerates it

Don't

  • Use a human hairdryer on hot — it burns skin you cannot see under coat
  • Let a double or curly coat air-dry
  • Blow air directly into ears, eyes or nose
  • Crate a damp dog to "finish off"
  • Assume the dog is dry because the surface is
  • Rush a first session — a dryer panic is very hard to undo

Nails, ears, teeth and paws

Nails

If you can hear your dog's nails clicking on a hard floor, they are too long. Overlong nails push the toes out of alignment and change how a dog stands, which over years puts real strain on the wrists, elbows and shoulders — especially in older dogs.

Inside each nail is the quick: a core of blood vessel and nerve. Cut it and it bleeds and hurts. On pale nails you can see it as a pink line — stop a couple of millimetres short. On black nails you cannot, so take thin slices and look at the cut face each time. You will see chalky white, then a small grey or black dot appearing in the centre. That dot is the leading edge of the quick. Stop there.

Trim little and often, weekly or fortnightly. The quick recedes as the nail shortens, so frequent small trims let you get nails back to a good length over a few weeks — something a single dramatic session can never do safely.

⚠️ Keep styptic powder within arm's reach before you start

You will quick a nail eventually; everyone does. Press styptic powder firmly into the nail for 30 seconds — cornflour or plain flour will do in an emergency. It stops quickly. What makes the difference long-term is your reaction: stay calm, treat, and carry on with a different paw if the dog will let you. Panic teaches the dog that nails are frightening far more effectively than the cut itself does.

Ears

Drop-eared breeds — Cockers, Bassets, Poodles, Labradors — have poor airflow in the canal and get far more ear infections than prick-eared dogs. After every bath, dry the ear flap and use a proper veterinary ear cleaner: fill the canal, massage the base for twenty seconds until it squelches, then let the dog shake and wipe only what you can see.

Never push a cotton bud into the canal. A dog's ear canal is L-shaped, so you cannot reach the bend and you will pack debris down against the eardrum.

On plucking ear hair: it used to be routine and is now genuinely debated, because plucking causes micro-trauma that can itself trigger inflammation. The current consensus is to pluck only if the dog has a history of recurrent ear infections and a vet has advised it. For everyone else, leave it alone.

Book a vet for a head shake that will not stop, a bad smell, a dark discharge, redness, or an ear the dog will not let you touch. Ear infections are painful and get worse fast.

Teeth

Brush daily if you can, three times a week at minimum, with a dog toothpaste — enzymatic, meant to be swallowed. Human toothpaste is never acceptable: many contain xylitol, which causes a catastrophic drop in blood sugar and liver failure in dogs, at very small doses.

Start by letting the dog lick the paste off your finger for a few days before a brush ever appears. Focus on the outer surfaces of the back teeth, where tartar actually forms. Dental chews help; they do not replace brushing.

Paws and the bits in between

Check between the toes every groom, particularly on long-coated dogs — grass seeds, mats, and impacted mud all live there. Trim the hair level with the pads, never down into the webbing. In winter, rinse salt and grit off after walks; in summer, check pads for cracking and burns from hot pavement.

💚 Anal glands are not a home job

Scooting, licking at the rear, or a strong fishy smell means the anal glands need attention — from a vet or a trained groomer. Expressing them yourself, and especially expressing them when they are not full, risks bruising, inflammation and abscess. This is one of the very few grooming tasks where the amateur version is meaningfully worse than none at all.

Mats: when to work on it, when to stop

A mat is not just tangled hair. As it tightens it pulls the skin up into it, so the skin you think you are cutting above is actually inside the mat. Every groomer has repaired a wound from a well-meaning owner with kitchen scissors, and the cuts are usually long and deep.

The test: can you slide a comb flat between the mat and the skin? If yes, you can work on it — hold the hair firmly at the base so you are not pulling the skin, and tease the mat apart from the outer edge inwards with a comb tip or a mat splitter, a few hairs at a time, with detangler spray. Never yank.

If the comb will not pass underneath, stop. That mat is against the skin and needs clippers going in flat beneath it — a groomer's job, not a scissor job.

And if the coat has gone past individual mats into a solid sheet — pelted — the only humane answer is a full shave-down by a professional. It will look shocking. Do it anyway: a pelt pulls constantly at the skin, traps urine and faeces, hides sores and parasites, and stops the dog moving comfortably. The coat grows back. Ask the groomer to show you the skin underneath afterwards; it is usually a lesson you only need once.

Nobody brings in a matted dog because they don't care. They bring one in because they brushed the top of the coat for months and never once put a comb through to the skin.

What each coat actually needs, week to week

Smooth and short single coats

Ten minutes a week with a rubber curry and a hound glove. Bath rarely — over-washing a short coat strips oils and causes flaking. Thin-coated breeds like Whippets and Boxers sunburn on the belly and nose, so a dog-safe sunscreen matters more than any brush.

Short double coats

People are always shocked by how much a Labrador or a Pug sheds. Rubber curry twice a week, undercoat rake during the spring and autumn blows, and a de-shedding bath at the start of each. Never shave. The coat is short, so mats are not the issue — volume is.

Long double coats

The biggest time commitment of any coat that does not get cut. Line-brush with a slicker, then rake the undercoat out, then comb to check, three or four times a week and daily when they blow. Behind the ears, the trousers and the tail base mat first. A high-velocity dryer turns this from a chore into a routine.

Silky, long single coats

Daily, without negotiation — the hair is fine, it grows continuously, and it mats in days. Pin brush, then a steel comb through to the skin, with detangler spray. Many owners keep a Shih Tzu or Yorkie in a short puppy trim, and honestly, the dog is usually happier for it.

Curly and wool coats

The coat that catches out first-time Doodle owners. Curl hides mats completely — the surface looks perfect while the layer against the skin is felting. Line brushing is the only method that works: part the coat, brush the exposed line from the skin outwards, drop down an inch, repeat. Daily, plus a professional trim every 6–8 weeks.

Wire and broken coats

Wire coats are hand stripped, not clipped: the dead outer hairs are pulled out so new harsh, richly coloured ones grow in. Clipping cuts those hairs instead, leaving only the soft pale base — which is why so many pet Schnauzers and Border Terriers end up woolly and faded. Strip every 8–12 weeks, or accept the softer look.

Corded coats

Pulis and Komondors are never brushed. The cords are separated by hand as they form, right down to the skin, and any brush destroys the structure. Bathing is a serious undertaking — a corded coat can take 24 hours or more to dry, and a cord left damp inside will rot and smell. This coat needs a specialist.

Hairless breeds

Treat it as skincare. Chinese Cresteds and Xolos get blackheads, acne and sunburn, and need gentle washing every week or two, a dog-safe moisturiser, and sunscreen or a shirt outdoors. Do not use human acne products. They also need coats in winter — there is no insulation at all.

Fun fact

A dog's whiskers are not decorative hair — they are vibrissae, thick tactile hairs rooted in dense bundles of nerves and blood vessels, and a dog uses them to judge gaps, navigate in the dark and sense air movement near its face. Show trimming has made cutting them common; there is no good reason to, and a whiskerless dog is genuinely a little less aware of its surroundings.

Skin and coat: what is normal, and what is not

💚 Healthy

  • Skin pale pink or evenly pigmented, no redness
  • Coat glossy, lying flat, springy to the touch
  • No smell beyond a faint warm doggy one
  • Shedding heavier in spring and autumn, then settling
  • Occasional scratching — seconds, not minutes
  • Comb passes from skin to tip everywhere

⚠️ Book the vet

  • Red, weeping or crusted patches — often a hot spot
  • A strong musty or rancid smell — usually yeast
  • Bald patches, or symmetrical thinning on both flanks
  • Black, thickened, elephant-like skin in the armpits
  • Constant licking of one spot, or chewing at paws
  • Scabs along the spine or at the tail base — often fleas
  • A coat that suddenly changes texture or stops growing back

A coat is a good early-warning system. Dull, brittle or thinning hair, or a sudden change in shedding, often shows up before anything else does — hypothyroidism, Cushing's disease, parasites and food reactions all announce themselves through the coat first. If grooming suddenly gets harder for no obvious reason, that is worth a conversation with your vet.

Teaching a dog to enjoy this

  1. Handle the awkward bits from day one. Paws, ears, mouth, tail, belly — a few seconds each, every day, paired with something good. Long before any tool appears.
  2. Introduce each tool switched off and still. Let the dog sniff the clippers, the dryer, the nail grinder. Treat. Put them away. Repeat for several days before anything is turned on.
  3. Then turn it on across the room. Noise at a distance, treat, off. Move a foot closer each session. The dog should be bored of the sound before it ever touches them.
  4. Keep the first real sessions absurdly short. Thirty seconds and a jackpot beats twenty minutes and a struggle. One paw. One side. Build up over weeks.
  5. Stop before the dog wants to. Always finish while it is still going well. The last thirty seconds is what the dog remembers next time.
  6. Book the first professional groom by 16 weeks for any coat that will need regular trims. A short, easy, positive first visit while the puppy is still confident is worth far more than a perfect haircut.

More fun facts for the grooming table

Fun fact

A double coat is air conditioning, not a jumper. The undercoat holds a layer of still air against the skin, and still air is one of the best insulators there is — it works against incoming heat exactly as well as it works against cold. A shaved Husky in July is hotter than an unshaved one, and sunburnt as well.

Fun fact

Hand stripping exists because of where the colour lives. In a wire coat the rich colour and harsh texture are in the tip of each hair, and the soft pale part is at the base. Pulling a dead hair out brings a fully coloured new one through; cutting it leaves only the pale soft base behind. That is why clipped Schnauzers and Airedales fade to grey and go woolly.

Fun fact

In Chinese Cresteds, the hairless gene is homozygous lethal — a puppy inheriting it from both parents does not survive. Every hairless Crested therefore carries one copy of the coated gene, and every litter contains fully coated siblings called powderpuffs. The two types are the same breed and turn up in the same litter.

Fun fact

The Puli's cords are not styled — they form themselves. The breed's woolly undercoat and wavy outer coat naturally felt together, and the handler's only job is to keep separating them into ropes as they develop so the dog ends up with cords instead of one solid pelt. A full corded coat can take four years to reach the ground.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I bath my dog?

It depends entirely on coat type. Smooth coats every 8–12 weeks, short double coats every 6–8, long double coats every 8–12, silky coats every 2–4, curly coats every 3–4, and hairless breeds every 1–2 weeks. Over-bathing strips the oils that keep skin healthy, so more is not better. A dog that has rolled in something is a separate matter.

Can I use human shampoo on my dog?

No. Dog skin sits around pH 6.2–7.4 and human shampoo is formulated for our far more acidic skin. Using it strips the protective acid mantle, leaving dry, flaky, itchy skin that is more prone to bacterial and yeast infection. Baby shampoo is milder but still the wrong pH.

Should I shave my Husky or German Shepherd in summer?

No. The undercoat insulates against heat as well as cold and the guard hairs block UV, so shaving makes a dog hotter and exposes it to sunburn. It also frequently grows back patchy or woolly — post-clipping alopecia, which can be permanent. De-shed with an undercoat rake instead; that genuinely cools the dog.

What brush do I need for a Goldendoodle or Labradoodle?

A slicker brush and a steel comb, used daily with the line-brushing technique — part the coat, brush the exposed line from the skin outwards, drop down an inch, repeat. Curly coats hide mats completely: the surface can look perfect while the layer against the skin is felting. Plus a professional trim every 6–8 weeks.

How do I cut black dog nails without hitting the quick?

Take thin slices and look at the cut face after each one. You will see chalky white, then a small grey or black dot appear in the centre — that dot is the front of the quick, so stop there. Trim little and often; the quick recedes as the nail shortens, so weekly small trims get you to a good length over a few weeks.

Can I cut a mat out with scissors?

Only if you can slide a comb flat between the mat and the skin. A tight mat pulls skin up into itself, so what looks like hair above the skin often is not. If the comb will not pass underneath, it needs clippers going in flat beneath the mat — a groomer's job. Scissor wounds from home dematting are common and usually deep.

Do I have to brush my dog before bathing?

Yes, always. Water shrinks and tightens hair, so a tangle that would have combed out in seconds becomes a felted mat that has to be cut out. If a dog is too matted to brush, it is too matted to bath — take it to a groomer first.

Is it safe to let my dog air-dry?

For a smooth coat in a warm room, yes. For anything with an undercoat or a curly coat, no. Damp undercoat held against the skin for hours is what causes hot spots, and curly coats lock into mats as they dry untouched. Dry to the skin with a high-velocity dryer, and brush as you go on long coats.

How much should I expect to spend to groom at home?

A short or smooth coat needs about $60–$120 of kit. A double coat adds a rake and ideally a dryer, so $150–$300. A curly or silky coat that you intend to clip yourself is $250–$450 once clippers, blades, scissors and a dryer are counted — and a professional groom every 6–8 weeks is a perfectly reasonable alternative.

Should I express my dog's anal glands at home?

No. Expressing glands that are not full, or doing it with the wrong technique, causes bruising, inflammation and sometimes abscesses. Scooting or a fishy smell means a trip to the vet or a trained groomer.

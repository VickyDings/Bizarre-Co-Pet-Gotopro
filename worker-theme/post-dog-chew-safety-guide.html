The chew aisle is the most dangerous shelf in the pet shop, and nothing on it carries a warning. There is no legal limit on how hard a dog chew is allowed to be. No standard, no test, no label. A product can be hard enough to split a tooth in half and still sit on the shelf next to the soft ones, in the same packaging, at the same price.

Veterinary dentists see the result constantly, and it is nearly always the same tooth: the upper fourth premolar, the big three-rooted carnassial at the back of the jaw that a dog uses to crack things. It splits vertically, a sheet of enamel flakes away, and the living pulp underneath is exposed. The repair is a root canal or an extraction under general anesthetic, and the dog usually gives no sign at all until it is well advanced. This guide is about which chews do that, which ones do not, and the simple test that separates them.

Chewing is a genuine need, not a luxury. The question is never whether to give a dog something to chew — it is what, how big, and for how long.

Fun fact: A dog's bite force at the back of the jaw is somewhere between 200 and 450 pounds per square inch, and the carnassial teeth are shaped like scissor blades rather than grinders. When those blades close on something that will not give, the force has nowhere to go except back into the tooth. That is why the damage is nearly always a vertical split rather than a worn-down surface.

The thumbnail test

The American Veterinary Dental College gives owners one rule, and it is the most useful sentence in this entire article: if you cannot make a dent in it with your thumbnail, it is too hard for your dog's teeth.

Try it in the shop, before you pay. Press your thumbnail into the chew hard enough that it would hurt if you kept going. If the surface marks, gives, or leaves an indent, it is in the safe range. If your nail slides off a surface that feels like a stone, put it back.

There is a second version of the same test that works just as well: would you be happy to be hit on the kneecap with it? If the answer is no, it does not belong in a dog's mouth either.

How a slab fracture actually happens

The mechanics

The carnassials are not grinders. They pass each other like shears, and the dog's jaw is built to generate enormous force through a very small contact area.

On something that yields — a tendon, a trachea, a bully stick — the chew deforms and absorbs that force. On something that does not, the energy goes straight back into the tooth, and a slab of enamel and dentin shears off the outer face.

Underneath is pulp: nerve and blood supply, now open to the mouth.

The upper fourth premolar, highlighted. It is the largest shearing tooth in the jaw and the one most often found split at a dental check.

Why you will not notice

Dogs almost never stop eating over a fractured tooth. They chew on the other side and carry on, and most owners find out at a routine dental or when the face swells from an abscess months later.

What to look for: chewing on one side only, dropping food, pawing at the mouth, sudden reluctance to take a hard treat, or a tooth with a visibly flat, discolored face.

An exposed pulp is painful and it is a route for infection into the jaw. It does not heal.

The thing that kills them

Obstruction. A fractured tooth is painful and expensive. A piece of chew lodged in the esophagus or the intestine is the one that is actually fatal, and it moves fast.

The highest-risk moment with any chew is the end of it. A dog that has worked a bully stick down to two inches will often try to swallow the rest whole rather than give it up, and a two-inch plug of compressed muscle fiber is exactly the wrong size and shape for a dog's esophagus.

Rawhide is its own case. It is a leather byproduct, not a food, and it does not break down in stomach acid the way meat protein does. Worse, it swells. A swallowed piece that fitted comfortably going down can expand enough to block the gut, and roughly 40 to 50 percent of chew-related foreign body cases trace back to it. Surgery runs $3,000 to $7,000, and that is when it goes well.

Signs, in the hours after a chew: repeated unproductive retching, drooling, refusing water, a hunched or praying posture, a swollen or painful belly, vomiting that will not stop. Any of those, same day, no waiting.

Size is not about the dog's enthusiasm, it is about its mouth. A chew a small dog can fit entirely behind the back teeth is a choking hazard whatever it is made of.

Size: longer than the muzzle, always

The rule is simple and it is worth being rigid about. A chew should be longer than the length of your dog's muzzle, measured from the tip of the nose to the back of the jaw. A chew that long physically cannot be turned sideways and swallowed, because it will not fit.

This is why a Labrador should not be given a six-inch bully stick as a routine item, and why a dachshund absolutely can. The number on the packet is meaningless on its own; it only means something next to your dog's head.

Weight matters too, but less than people assume. A determined 20-pound terrier does more damage to a chew than a placid 70-pound retriever. Watch what your own dog actually does with one before deciding what size it needs.

And every chew has an endpoint. When it is small enough to swallow whole, it is finished — take it, bin it, and swap it for something else. A holder or a safety handle helps with the last inches of a bully stick, and trading up for a better treat beats taking it out of a dog's mouth by hand.

Cooked bone: the one absolute

There is very little in dog care that deserves the word never, and this is one of them. No cooked bone, from any animal, of any size, ever. Not the Sunday roast, not a rib, not a chicken carcass, not a "smoked" or "baked" bone sold as a dog chew.

Cooking drives the moisture out of bone and changes its structure. Raw bone is slightly flexible and crushes; cooked bone is brittle and shatters into shards with edges like broken glass. Those shards can lacerate the mouth, lodge in the throat, perforate the stomach or the intestine, or pack into a cement-like mass in the colon.

This is the part that catches people out: a smoked bone is a cooked bone. So is a baked one. The pet trade sells plenty of both, often in the same aisle as the raw frozen ones, and the word on the packet is the only thing distinguishing them.

Hard nylon and plastic chews fail the thumbnail test as completely as antler does, and they add shards to the fracture risk.

What about nylon and "indestructible" chews?

They sell on the promise that a heavy chewer cannot destroy them, and that promise is the problem. A chew a dog cannot mark is a chew harder than the dog's teeth, and the force has to go somewhere.

Hard nylon bones fail the thumbnail test outright. They also shed small plastic shards as they wear, which are not digestible and do occasionally cause obstruction. The softer rubber chews are a different proposition entirely — a rubber toy that flexes under a thumbnail is fine, and a hollow one stuffed with food and frozen gives most dogs more occupation than any hard chew does.

The honest summary is that "lasts forever" and "safe for teeth" are close to opposites. A chew should be consumed. That is what consuming it is for.

Rawhide, honestly

Rawhide deserves a section of its own because the picture is more mixed than either side of the argument admits. It is not poison, it is not banned, and millions of dogs chew it without incident. It is also behind a disproportionate share of the obstruction surgeries vets perform.

The problem is digestibility. Rawhide is the inner layer of cattle hide — the raw material of leather. Stomach acid softens it slowly and incompletely, and a swallowed piece can sit in the gut absorbing fluid and swelling rather than breaking down. A chunk that went down easily can become one that cannot come back up or pass through.

Not all rawhide is equal. The knotted bone shapes are the worst offenders, because the knots come loose as a single dense lump that a dog can gulp. Compressed rawhide is pressed into a solid form that wears down rather than coming apart in sheets, and it is a genuinely lower risk — though still rawhide.

Where it was processed matters too. Rawhide has historically been treated with bleaches and preservatives, and manufacturing standards vary enormously by country of origin. If you use it at all, buy from somewhere that will tell you where it was made.

Introducing a new chew without a trip to the vet

  1. Thumbnail-test it in the shop. Before it is paid for, not after. If the surface will not mark, it does not come home.
  2. Measure it against the muzzle. Nose to the back of the jaw. The chew should be longer than that, with room to spare.
  3. Give it on an empty-ish stomach, on a hard floor. Not on carpet, where you cannot see what has come off, and not in a crate where you cannot watch.
  4. Stay in the room. The whole time. Not the next room, not with the television on. This is the step everybody skips and it is the one that matters.
  5. Cap the first session at ten minutes. A new chew on an unaccustomed gut causes loose stools surprisingly often, and a short first go tells you whether this one agrees with your dog.
  6. Watch the next 24 hours. Loose stools, reduced appetite or an uncomfortable-looking dog means that chew is not for them, whatever the packet says.
  7. Take it at the swallow point. The moment the remaining piece would fit whole down the throat, the chew is over. Trade it for something better rather than taking it by hand.
  8. Rotate. Three or four different textures through the week keeps a dog interested and stops any single one dominating the calorie budget.

Hand it over rather than putting it on the floor and walking away. The first few seconds are when you find out whether the size is right.

The trade, not the grab

Taking a chew out of a dog's mouth with your hand is how resource guarding starts, and how people get bitten by dogs that have never bitten anyone. The alternative takes a week to teach and lasts a lifetime.

Walk up with something genuinely better — a piece of chicken, not a biscuit. Say "swap", put it on the floor just in front of the dog, and pick up the chew while they take it. Then, most of the time, give the chew back. That is the part that makes it work: a dog that learns your approach predicts a bonus and the return of their property stops caring that you are approaching at all.

The chews that last the longest are the ones that break teeth. That is not a coincidence or a design flaw — it is the same property described two different ways. A chew that survives a determined dog for three weeks is, by definition, harder than the dog.

Puppies, seniors, and dogs with a history

Puppies are chewing because they are teething, and they need something soft. Milk teeth and the new adult teeth coming in behind them are both more fragile than a mature tooth, and a hard chew at four months can damage a tooth that has not even finished erupting. Frozen carrot, a wet frozen flannel, soft tripe sticks or a rubber toy stuffed and frozen all do the job. Save the serious chews for a year old.

A gray muzzle changes the shopping list. Anything that dents easily and still takes time to get through is the target.

Senior dogs often have teeth that are already worn, already cracked, or have had work done. Anything that dents easily and still takes time to get through is the target — tripe, trachea, soft bully sticks. A dog with existing dental disease should be on nothing harder than a tendon until a vet has looked.

Dogs with a previous fracture are the ones to be strictest with. A tooth that has already been repaired is weaker than its neighbors, and the dog will usually chew on the opposite side, which concentrates everything onto teeth that are now doing double duty.

Separate rooms, every time. It is not about whether they get on -- it is that a dog racing another dog swallows what it would normally work down.

Multiple dogs change the calculation entirely. Two dogs with chews in the same room will often race each other, and a racing dog swallows things it would normally work down. Separate them — different rooms, or crates — every time. This is not about whether they get on.

Why dogs need to chew at all

It is worth being clear that chewing is not a vice to be managed. It is a normal, necessary canine behavior, and a dog denied any outlet for it will find one — usually a chair leg, a baseboard, or the corner of a door.

A dog that has settled with something to work on. This is what a chew is actually for.

Chewing releases endorphins. It is genuinely calming, which is why a dog settles after ten minutes with something to work on and why a chew is one of the most effective tools for a dog that struggles to switch off. It is also the single best thing you can give a dog that has to be left alone for a short period, or that gets wound up when visitors arrive.

That is the real argument for getting this right rather than giving up on chews altogether. The answer to the risks in this article is not "no chews" — it is the right chew, the right size, supervised, and taken away at the right moment.

How much a dog needs varies enormously. Terriers, working breeds and anything bred to use its mouth generally need more than a placid companion breed. Puppies and adolescents need far more than adults. A dog that is destructive in the house is very often a dog whose chewing need is going somewhere, and the cheapest fix is usually to give it somewhere better to go.

The frozen stuffed toy

If you only take one practical idea from this article, make it this one. A hollow rubber toy packed with something wet — wet food, plain yogurt, mashed banana, soaked kibble — and frozen solid will occupy most dogs longer than anything hard, and it carries no fracture risk whatever.

Freezing is the whole trick. A stuffed toy at room temperature is gone in four minutes; the same toy frozen takes forty. Make up several at once and keep them in the freezer, and you have a solution for rainy days, visitors, and the half hour you need to get something done.

Reading the packet

Chew packaging is close to unregulated, and most of the words on the front mean nothing in particular. A few are worth decoding.

"Natural" has no legal definition in this context. An antler is entirely natural and will still split a tooth. Natural tells you about origin, not safety.

"Long-lasting" and "for aggressive chewers" are the warning signs. A chew that outlasts a determined dog is harder than that dog's teeth, and the marketing is describing the hazard as the feature.

"Digestible" is meaningful, but check what it is made of rather than trusting the word. Single-ingredient muscle, organ or tendon genuinely does digest. Rawhide labeled digestible is still rawhide.

Country of origin and processing is the thing most worth knowing and the thing most often missing. Dried animal products have been behind salmonella recalls affecting both dogs and the people handling them, and standards vary a great deal by where the product was processed. A company that states plainly where its chews are made is giving you more information than one that does not.

Calorie content is rarely on the packet at all, which is why chews slip past the diet so easily. If it is not stated, assume a hard-dried muscle chew is in the region of 15 calories per inch and plan accordingly.

Storing them, and when to throw one out

Dried chews are shelf-stable but they are still animal products, and they are not sterile. Handle them like raw meat: wash your hands afterward, keep them away from food preparation surfaces, and be particularly careful if anyone in the house is very young, elderly or immunocompromised.

Dried chews — bully sticks, tripe sticks, ears, feet — keep in a sealed container somewhere cool and dry. Humidity is the enemy; a damp chew grows mold, and a moldy chew goes in the bin rather than being wiped off.

Raw frozen items — poultry necks, raw bones — stay frozen until the day they are used, thaw in the refrigerator rather than on the counter, and are used or discarded within a day of thawing. Never refreeze a thawed one.

A partly chewed chew that has been slobbered on and abandoned is a bacterial culture by the following day. If a dog leaves a chew half-finished, either refrigerate it in a bag and use it within a day or two, or accept that it is finished.

Throw one out when it smells rancid rather than just strong, when there is any visible mold, when it has gone soft and slimy, or when it has worn down to a size the dog could swallow. The last one is the one people hesitate over, and it is the one that matters most.

What chews cannot do

It is easy to come away from an article like this thinking that getting the chew right is dental care sorted. It is not, and the gap is worth naming.

Most canine dental disease is periodontal — it starts at the gum line, where plaque hardens into tartar and inflames the gum, and it ends in bone loss and lost teeth. Chewing does very little about that, because the mechanical scraping happens on the crown of the tooth and not at the margin where the trouble starts.

Brushing is the only thing that genuinely addresses it, and it needs to be daily to matter. A dog toothpaste, a soft brush or a finger brush, thirty seconds a side, worked up to gradually over a couple of weeks so it is not a fight. Never human toothpaste — xylitol is toxic to dogs and fluoride is not meant to be swallowed.

Beyond that, an annual dental check is worth more than any chew on the market. A vet can see a slab fracture, a resorptive lesion or gum recession that you cannot, and the small problem found early is a far cheaper afternoon than the abscess found late.

What supervision actually means

Every article about chews says supervise, and almost none of them say what you are watching for. Sitting in the same room with your phone is not supervision. These are the four things worth noticing, and each one has a response.

Gulping rather than grinding. A dog working a chew properly uses the back teeth in a steady rhythm. A dog tipping its head back and trying to get the whole thing down is about to swallow something it should not. Take it, trade it, and come back to it later with a longer piece.

The chew changing shape suddenly. Most safe chews wear down evenly. A piece coming off in a sheet or a chunk — rawhide peeling, a knot loosening, a bone fragment splitting away — is the moment the risk changes. That chew is over.

Blood. A smear on a cow ear from a gum is common and means little. Blood that keeps coming, or a dog that flinches and switches sides mid-session, means stop and look in the mouth.

The size of what is left. This is the one that matters most and the one people misjudge, because a dog that has been happily occupied for twenty minutes does not look like a dog about to choke. Hold the remaining piece up against the dog's muzzle, not against your own sense of how big it is.

Twenty minutes of actual attention is not a lot to ask, and it is the difference between a chew being enrichment and a chew being a gamble. If you cannot give it that, a frozen stuffed toy needs none of it.

What it costs, and what it costs to get wrong

A chew habit is not expensive. A medium dog getting four or five chews a week, rotated across a few types, runs somewhere between $20 and $45 a month. Over a year that is perhaps $300 to $500, which is roughly what people spend on toys and treats anyway.

Set that against the other column. A slab fracture repaired by root canal costs $1,500 to $3,000 per tooth, and a dog that has broken one carnassial frequently breaks the other, because it has switched sides and is now concentrating everything onto teeth doing double duty. An extraction of a three-rooted carnassial is oral surgery under general anesthetic, and a dog with a history of dental work needs more of it, not less.

An obstruction is worse again. Surgery to remove a foreign body from the intestine runs $3,000 to $7,000, carries real risk, and involves a week of recovery with a dog that does not understand why it feels terrible.

Neither of those is a scare story and neither is likely on any given day. The point is simply that the arithmetic is lopsided. The expensive chews are the cheap ones.

Questions people actually ask

Are antlers really that bad?

Yes, and they are among the most common causes of fractured teeth that veterinary dentists see. An antler is dense bone with no give at all, it fails the thumbnail test completely, and it lasts for months precisely because nothing about it yields.

They are not toxic and most dogs chewing one on any given day will be fine. The risk is cumulative: every session is another chance for one bad bite at the wrong angle. If your dog loves them, a split antler is marginally softer than a whole one, but the honest answer is that there is no safe way to use something harder than tooth enamel.

How do I know if my dog has broken a tooth?

Often you cannot, which is the problem. Dogs rarely stop eating over it. Look for chewing on one side, dropping food, pawing at the face, a sudden reluctance to take hard treats, or bad breath that is new.

Lift the lip and look at the big tooth at the back of the upper jaw, on both sides. A slab fracture shows as a flat face where there should be a rounded one, sometimes with a dark spot in the middle. If you find one, that is a vet appointment, not a wait-and-see.

My dog swallowed a big piece. What now?

Do not make them vomit unless a vet tells you to — a sharp or swollen piece coming back up can do more damage than going down. Call your vet and describe the size, the material and the time.

Watch for repeated unproductive retching, drooling, refusing water, a hunched posture, a tight belly, or vomiting that keeps going. Any of those is an emergency. A small soft piece of bully stick usually passes uneventfully; a chunk of rawhide or cooked bone is a different conversation and the clock matters.

Is rawhide banned anywhere?

No, it is legal and widely sold. The concern is digestibility rather than toxicity. If you use it, compressed rawhide is meaningfully safer than the knotted bone shapes, because it wears down rather than coming apart as a swallowable lump, and the knot is the usual culprit.

Supervise, buy from a maker who will tell you where it was processed, and take it away at the point where the remaining piece would fit down the throat.

What can a puppy chew?

Soft things. A teething puppy needs relief, not a challenge, and its teeth — both the milk set and the adult ones erupting behind them — are more fragile than a mature dog's.

Frozen carrot, a wet frozen washcloth, soft tripe sticks, a rubber toy stuffed and frozen. No antlers, no hooves, no nylon, no hard bones. Save those for never, in fact, but certainly not before a year old.

How long should a dog chew for?

Fifteen to thirty minutes is plenty for most dogs, and that is as much about the gut and the calories as the teeth. A dog that works at something for two hours is usually bored rather than satisfied, and the fix for that is more exercise and more to do, not a longer-lasting chew.

Chews count as food. A twelve-inch bully stick is a third of a small dog's daily calories, and dogs gain weight quietly on treats nobody is counting.

Can I give my dog the bone from my dinner?

No. Cooked bone of any kind, from any animal, is the one absolute in this article. Cooking makes bone brittle, and brittle bone shatters into shards that can perforate the gut.

That includes the Sunday roast bone, rib bones, chicken carcasses, and the smoked or baked bones sold in pet shops as chews — smoked is cooked.

Do chews actually clean teeth?

A little, and less than the packaging suggests. A soft chew worked over by the back teeth does scrape some plaque away mechanically, which is genuinely useful — but it does not reach the gum line, and gum disease is where most canine dental trouble starts.

Chews are a supplement to brushing, not a replacement. Daily brushing with a dog toothpaste is still the thing that actually works.

Two dogs, one room. Is that fine?

No, and this catches out people whose dogs get along perfectly. Two dogs with chews in the same space will often race each other, and a dog in a hurry swallows what it would normally spend ten minutes on.

Separate rooms or separate crates, every time, and collect what is left before they come back together. It is about speed, not aggression.

What is the single best chew?

For most dogs, a bully stick of the right length, supervised, retired at the last two inches. It dents under a thumbnail, it is one ingredient, anything swallowed digests like meat, and dogs find it genuinely worth the effort.

The best chew for your dog specifically is whichever safe option they will actually settle down with, rotated with two or three others so no single one takes over the calorie budget.

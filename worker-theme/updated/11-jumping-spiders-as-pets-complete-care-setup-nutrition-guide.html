A jumping spider will look up when you walk into the room. Not startle — look. It will turn its whole body, track you across the desk, and hold your gaze with eyes that resolve detail better than almost any other animal its size. That is the reason people keep them, and it is also the reason so many of them die in their first month: an animal this responsive feels robust, and it isn't.

Jumping spiders (family Salticidae) are the largest spider family on earth — more than 6,000 described species — and over the last few years they have become one of the most popular invertebrate pets in the United States. They are cheap to house, silent, odorless, need no walking, and fit on a bookshelf. For a first invertebrate they are close to ideal.

They are also small, short-lived, and completely unforgiving of three or four specific mistakes. This guide covers what they actually need, what genuinely kills them, and what to buy — with the honest version of the lifespan question up front, because that is the single thing new keepers most often aren't told.

📚 A note on sources — and why this guide flags its own uncertainty

There is essentially no peer-reviewed literature on keeping jumping spiders as pets. Arachnology covers their biology superbly — vision, development, behavior — but nobody has run a controlled study on enclosure humidity or feeding intervals. Meanwhile a large share of the jumping spider care pages that rank on Google are machine-generated and recycle each other's numbers, including some that are flatly wrong.

So this guide separates three things: published science, keeper consensus (widely agreed, never formally tested), and genuinely contested points where experienced keepers disagree. Where a number is soft, we say so rather than inventing precision.

⚠️ Read this before you buy: they live about a year

Published laboratory work on Phidippus puts adult life at roughly three to four months after the final molt, and experienced keepers consistently report 8–14 months total from hatching. Retailers advertising “1–3 years” or “3+ years” are at the optimistic edge of the evidence.

This matters commercially too: buying a mature adult means buying the shortest-lived option. A freshly matured spider may have a few months left. A captive-bred juvenile or sub-adult has most of its life ahead. If you want time with the animal, buy young.

👀 Eight Facts That Explain Everything Else

  1. They see better than any other animal this size. The two huge forward-facing principal eyes are effectively telephoto lenses with a moveable retina behind them, giving spatial acuity that outclasses many mammals. The smaller side eyes are motion detectors covering nearly 360°. This is why a jumping spider notices you.
  2. They are strictly diurnal. They hunt by sight, in daylight, and retreat to a silk nest at night and in bad weather. A dark room is a genuine husbandry problem — a spider that can't see won't hunt.
  3. They don't build webs to catch food. Salticids stalk and pounce. The silk goes into a retreat, a dragline safety rope, and egg sacs — never a snare.
  4. They don't jump with muscles. The leap is hydraulic: a rapid surge of hemolymph pressure extends the rear legs. That same pressurized system is why an abdominal puncture is so serious — the fluid is under pressure and doesn't clot like blood.
  5. They stop growing permanently at maturity. Jumping spiders are araneomorphs, and araneomorphs molt only until they reach adulthood. Unlike tarantulas, a mature jumping spider never molts again and cannot regrow a lost leg. Any care sheet claiming adults molt annually is simply wrong.
  6. They see ultraviolet, and use it socially. Published work on Cosmophasis and Phintella shows UVA carries sex recognition and UVB reflectance drives female mate choice. Fascinating — and not a reason to buy a UVB bulb. There is no evidence pet salticids need UV for health, and a bulb mostly adds heat and dryness.
  7. They digest their food outside their body. Enzymes go in, liquefied tissue comes out, the husk gets discarded. This is why dusting feeders with calcium powder does nothing — the powder is on the outside of a meal the spider never swallows. Reptile supplement protocols do not transfer.
  8. They learn. In a published predation study, Phidippus spiders offered chemically defended fireflies attacked, rejected them on contact, and dropped from a 60% attack rate to zero by the seventh trial. They form aversions from experience.
🎉 Worth knowing

The “fireflies are lethal to jumping spiders” warning you'll see repeated everywhere overstates the evidence. Fireflies are chemically defended — they carry steroidal lucibufagins, which are documented as killing captive reptiles — but the actual jumping spider study found the spiders rejected them after contact and were released apparently unharmed, with no spider deaths recorded. Still don't feed fireflies. Just don't repeat the mechanism as fact.

🦙 Which Species Should a Beginner Actually Get?

There is one right answer, and the rest of the list exists so you know what you're being offered when a vendor uses an unfamiliar Latin name.

Regal Jumping Spider

Phidippus regius

Start here

The hobby default and the correct first spider. Largest North American jumper (females ~15 mm body), boldest, most visually responsive, and the only species with genuinely common captive-bred stock. Buy CB, juvenile or sub-adult, $20–$60.

Bold Jumper

Phidippus audax

Free alternative

Native across most of North America, so usually collected rather than bought. Hardy and bold, slightly smaller than regius. Legal to collect on your own property in most of the US — the trade-off is unknown age and possible pesticide exposure.

Tan & Canopy Jumpers

Platycryptus · P. otiosus

Occasional

Bark-dwellers that turn up in trade sporadically and on porch walls constantly. Fine to keep, but rarely sold as established captive-bred lines, so you are usually buying an adult of unknown age.

Zebra Jumper

Salticus scenicus

Skip as a first

Charming, common on sunny walls, and only 5–9 mm. That size is the problem: every husbandry error is less forgiving, the spider needs the smallest fruit flies available, and you can barely see what it is doing. A second spider, not a first.

⚠️ Hyllus diardi is not a beginner spider — and neither is a $160 juvenile

The big Southeast Asian Hyllus species are gorgeous and heavily marketed. Reputable vendors classify them as advanced, require higher temperatures (80–84°F) and humidity than Phidippus, and some screen buyers before selling. Juveniles run $110–$165. If a seller pitches one as a starter species, that tells you what you need to know about the seller.

Also: color-morph pricing on jumping spiders is not supported. Locale names like “Bahamas,” “Everglades” or “Apalachicola” describe where the founder animals were collected — they are not bred lines. As one long-time keeper put it, colors “are not proven out in jumpers.” Don't pay ball-python morph prices for a lottery ticket.

Telling males from females

The reliable tell is the pedipalps — the small leg-like appendages either side of the face. A mature male's palps end in swollen bulbs that everyone describes as boxing gloves, with a distinct dip in the last segment. A sub-adult male's palps look thickened and rounded but lack that terminal indentation, which makes it the single best “one molt from adult” signal. Female palps stay slim for life; a mature female instead has an epigyne, a small shiny puckered plate on the underside of the abdomen, best seen through the enclosure floor.

P. regius is unusually easy: juvenile females develop pale hairy faces and mottled orange coloring, while males stay black and white with a bare dark face — sometimes distinguishable as early as the third instar.

💚 Two sexing myths to ignore

Chelicerae color does not indicate sex. Those iridescent green, blue and purple fangs occur in both sexes and vary by species. Size does not indicate sex or maturity either — individual and locale variation swamps the difference.

🏠 The Enclosure: Vertical, Ventilated, Front-Opening

Almost every enclosure mistake traces back to one biological fact: a jumping spider builds a silk retreat — a little hammock — and sleeps in it every night, molts in it, and in the case of females lays eggs in it. In captivity they almost invariably build that retreat in the top corner. Design around that and most problems disappear.

⚠️ Do not put a spiderling in an adult enclosure

It feels generous. It isn't. A sling in a large enclosure cannot locate its prey — and prey it can't find is prey that survives, hides in the substrate, and is still alive when the spider seals itself in to molt. That is one of the most concrete causes of death in the hobby. Upsize as the spider grows, not before.

Ventilation — the one thing nearly everyone agrees on

Air must enter on one face and exit from another. A lid full of holes is not ventilation; it's a chimney with no intake. Put fine mesh or drilled holes on two opposing sides, ideally offset in height so air crosses the whole volume.

Size the holes against your feeders, not your spider — roughly 1 mm or less if you're culturing flightless fruit flies, or you'll be dosing the room instead of the enclosure. If you're drilling your own, use a rotary tool rather than a knife: ragged plastic edges catch tarsi.

Substrate and furnishings

Substrate is genuinely debated — some keepers run bare-bottom with paper towel to spot problems early, others go full bioactive. One to two inches of coco fiber, or a coco and sphagnum blend, is the safe default, because it does three jobs at once: buffers humidity, cushions falls, and anchors décor.

For furnishings, think crevices and forks, not surfaces. A vertical slab of cork bark plus a couple of branches reaching toward the top corners gives the spider somewhere to build. Add a plant or two for cover. Then stop — leave open space to jump through.

🌡️ Temperature, Humidity and Light

Temperature: 72–80°F, and your living room probably already is

Sources spread from 68°F to 85°F, and the disagreement is partly transatlantic — UK keepers heat, US keepers mostly don't. 72–80°F (22–27°C) is the safe target for Phidippus. Below about 60°F they go lethargic and stop eating, which is a slow starvation rather than an acute event. Above about 85–88°F risk climbs fast, because a small enclosure gives the spider nowhere to escape to.

⚠️ Direct sunlight is the fastest way to kill one

A small acrylic or glass enclosure on a sunny windowsill becomes an oven in minutes. This is a documented, common, entirely preventable death. Bright indirect light only.

If your home genuinely runs cold, a small heat mat is the only safe supplement, and it comes with three non-negotiable rules: on a separate thermostat (not a thermometer), mounted beside or behind the enclosure and never underneath, and never a heat bulb — radiant heat plus desiccation is rejected by every source that discusses it.

Humidity: stop chasing the number

This is the most contested parameter in the hobby. Published recommendations range from 40% to 90%, which should tell you how little anyone actually knows. One camp targets ~70% on the reasoning that Florida ambient humidity is 74–85%; the other says provide ventilation, mist lightly, and manage by observation.

Here is the part everyone does agree on, and it's the part that matters: poor ventilation combined with high humidity kills more jumping spiders than dryness does — through mold, bacterial growth and stagnant air. A beginner who buys a cheap hygrometer, reads 45%, and floods the enclosure has just created the actual problem.

So: mist the walls lightly every two to three days, let the substrate dry between mistings, and read the enclosure instead of the gauge. Persistent condensation on the inside, visible mold, or a musty smell all mean the same thing — too wet, not enough air.

💚 Mist the walls, never the spider

The spider drinks the droplets off the glass. Spraying it directly risks its book lungs, and for a spiderling a droplet bigger than about a quarter of its body can trap it by surface tension. Aim at the wall, not the animal.

Light: bright, indirect, on a schedule — and no UVB

Ten to twelve hours of daylight or a small LED on a timer is all that's needed. No special bulb. As covered above, salticids genuinely do see and use ultraviolet, but that is a courtship-signalling story, not a husbandry requirement. Adding a reptile UVB lamp to a jumping spider enclosure imports heat and desiccation risk in exchange for no demonstrated benefit.

🦟 Nutrition: What to Feed, How Much, How Often

Bottle flies are the enthusiast's choice for adults, and for good reasons: they're a natural prey type, they trigger a strong feeding response, they can't injure the spider, and they don't burrow into the substrate to hide. Buy them as spikes or pupae and hatch a few at a time.

Prey size and feeding frequency

The rule is simple: prey smaller than the spider's body — roughly half to three-quarters of its body length, never larger. And there's a neat heuristic for knowing when to size up: if the spider tries to grab a second fly while the first is still in its fangs, the prey is too small.

As a schedule, slings eat daily to every other day, juveniles every two to four days, and adults every five to seven. But the calendar is the weaker method. Judge by the abdomen: if it's narrower than the carapace, feed; if it's noticeably wider, skip. A distended abdomen raises rupture risk and is associated with failed molts.

⚠️ Never leave live prey in overnight — this is the number one killer

An uneaten cricket doesn't wait politely. It will chew on a molting or freshly molted spider, and it can kill it outright. Remove uneaten prey within 24 hours, every time, and stop feeding entirely at the first sign of pre-molt. Of all the advice in this guide, this is the piece that saves the most spiders.

Water, gut-loading, and what not to bother with

Misting the walls every two or three days is the primary water source, and for most keepers it is sufficient on its own. A water dish is optional and disputed — drowning is a real reported cause of death, so if you use one, keep it very shallow with an obvious climb-out, and skip it entirely for slings.

Gut-loading helps modestly. Feed and hydrate your feeders before offering them; newly hatched fruit flies benefit from sugar water or honey. Much of the spider's water and nutrition comes from prey body fluids, so a starved dry feeder is a poor meal.

Dusting does nothing. Spiders digest externally and discard the husk, so calcium powder on the outside of a feeder is never ingested. There is no metabolic bone disease analogue in spiders. Leave the reptile supplements in the cabinet.

⚠️ Feeders to avoid or handle carefully

Wild-caught insects — never. Two independent reasons: systemic pesticide residue persists in insect tissue and your spider weighs almost nothing, and wild insects import mites and parasites. This includes houseflies you swat indoors.

Mealworms — small ones only. They have strong mandibles and can injure a jumping spider. Use mini mealworms, or crush the head first. Skip adult darkling beetles entirely.

Ants, other spiders, fireflies — no. Formic acid, predation risk, and chemical defense respectively.

🕶️ Molting: The Most Dangerous Week of Their Life

Molting is when any arthropod is at maximum risk, and jumping spiders get a finite number of attempts. Cornell's arachnology material puts the vulnerability window bluntly: the new exoskeleton stays pliable for perhaps an hour before it begins to harden. Get it wrong and there is no second chance — and after the final molt, there is no molting at all, ever again.

Reading pre-molt

  1. It stops eating. The earliest and most reliable sign, and it can start anywhere from a few days to four weeks ahead. Stop offering food.
  2. It slows down. More time in the retreat, less patrolling. Sometimes restless pacing first, then stillness.
  3. The colors dull. The abdomen darkens and the body looks thickened and compact.
  4. The retreat seals shut. This is definitive. Once the spider closes the entrance to its hammock, the molt usually follows within about a day. From this moment, hands off.

⚠️ The sealed retreat rule — six things not to do

Do not open the retreat. Not to look, not to check, not once.
Do not move the décor the retreat is attached to.
Do not handle — from sealing until at least a week after it re-emerges.
Do not feed, and remove every live feeder immediately.
Do not try to help a spider that looks stuck. There is no documented successful intervention for a salticid this small.
Do not panic-soak the enclosure. High humidity with poor airflow creates its own problems. Light misting of the walls is the ceiling of useful action.

How long, and when to feed again

Be skeptical of any per-instar day table you find online — the precise numbers circulating are invented. What is supportable: the molt itself takes several hours to about a day, and the spider typically stays sealed in for several days to a couple of weeks, longer in older animals. The behavioral rule beats any calendar: the spider tells you it's finished by reopening the retreat and moving normally.

After that, wait 24 to 72 hours, then offer one small soft feeder — a fruit fly or bottle fly, not a cricket or mealworm. The chelicerae harden last.

Molt frequency drops off sharply with age. The first four instars run at roughly two-and-a-half week intervals; by the fifth and sixth they stretch toward two months with enormous variation. Expect something like six to ten molts total, males at the low end, females at the high end — then never again.

💚 Pre-molt or starving? The discriminator

Both produce a spider that isn't eating. The difference is behavioral: a pre-molt spider withdraws and seals itself in. A starving spider stays out and keeps hunting. If it's active, visible and getting thinner, the problem is prey size, enclosure size or temperature — not a molt.

🏥 Health: What Actually Goes Wrong

Let's be direct about something most care guides fudge. There is no realistic veterinary care for a 15 mm spider. Specialist invertebrate vets exist, but their work is overwhelmingly on tarantulas and larger invertebrates, and no diagnostics or drugs exist at this scale. Any guide that tells you to “see your vet immediately” for a jumping spider is padding.

💚 Caring for an old jumping spider

Most guides skip this, and it's the part keepers actually need. When a mature spider starts falling off the walls, it isn't sick — it's old, and you have weeks rather than months. Make the end comfortable: move it to a shorter enclosure or lower the décor so falls are survivable, line the floor with paper towel or soft substrate, add low-angle objects it can still climb, offer smaller and slower prey (a bottle fly with clipped wings, or pre-killed), and mist more often since an old spider drinks less efficiently.

📚 About “DKS”

You'll see dyskinetic syndrome mentioned in invertebrate forums — jerky movement, leg shaking, staggering, progressing to death. It's a tarantula-hobby descriptor, not a veterinary diagnosis, and even the best hobby sources say plainly that nobody knows what causes it. It has not been documented in Salticidae specifically. Treat those signs as a prompt to audit for pesticide exposure, not as a named disease with a treatment.

🤚 Handling: The Risk Runs the Other Way

Salticidae do not appear on standard lists of medically significant spiders. Bites are rare, happen only when the spider is cornered or squeezed, and produce localized redness, itching and swelling that resolves in a day or two. Washington State University's entomology department gives the honest framing: unlikely to bite, but the bite is painful and can penetrate skin. Keepers describe a regius bite as a tiny prick. Soap, water, a cold compress, done.

Jumping spiders also have no urticating hairs, so the tarantula-keeper dermatitis literature simply doesn't apply. If anything in this hobby is going to sensitize you, it's the cricket and roach colonies, not the spider.

⚠️ The real danger is a fall — and it's yours to prevent

A drop from standing height onto a hard floor can rupture a jumping spider's abdomen. That is the injury that actually kills handled spiders, and it is entirely a function of where you're standing.

Handle sitting down, at floor level, over a bed, carpet or towel. Never standing. Never over tile or hardwood.

Beyond that the rules are short. Wash your hands first — soap residue, lotion and especially any insecticide are serious hazards at this body size. Never grab; let the spider walk onto an open palm, or coax it with a soft brush or a cup. Use hand-over-hand walking, offering the next palm in front of the current one. Keep sessions short, move slowly, and never handle during pre-molt, during a molt, or in the week after one. And never force it — a spider that doesn't want to come out is telling you something useful.

🛒 Buying Your First Jumping Spider

Buy captive-bred, and buy a juvenile or sub-adult. Those two decisions matter more than anything else on this page.

Captive-bred stock comes with a known age — which is the whole ballgame when the animal lives about a year. It also arrives without a parasite load or a lifetime of accumulated pesticide exposure, already feeding on commercial feeders, and without removing anything from a wild population. Sub-adult is the sweet spot: old enough to sex, robust enough to survive one beginner mistake, with most of its life still ahead.

Assume wild-caught unless the seller explicitly says otherwise. Mainstream reptile retailers do list field-collected P. regius, and keepers specifically flag expo tables run by non-specialists as a problem.

⚠️ Eleven red flags when buying

1. No stated captive-bred or wild-caught status. 2. No stated instar or age — “adult” can mean two months left. 3. No live arrival guarantee. 4. Willing to ship during a heatwave or a freeze. 5. No heat pack when shipping from a cold hub. 6. Color-morph pricing. 7. Ships to Florida or Hawaii without discussion. 8. Advertises Hyllus diardi as beginner-friendly. 9. Ground shipping for live invertebrates, ever. 10. Stock photos instead of the actual animal. 11. Sold at a general reptile expo by a vendor whose main line is something else.

Shipping

Overnight express only, labeled LIVE ANIMAL. Reputable vendors hold shipments when the origin, destination, or the sorting hub in between falls below 40°F or rises above 95°F — and that middle one matters more than people expect, since a Memphis sort at 20°F kills spiders that were fine at both ends. Live arrival guarantees are strict: expect to collect from the hub the same day with ID and tracking, and to report any DOA within an hour of delivery with photos. Order in spring or fall if you can.

Legality — briefly, but do check

Federally, USDA APHIS requires a PPQ 526 permit to import invertebrates and to move them interstate, and APHIS explicitly puts the burden on the buyer: you are responsible for verifying a valid permit before purchasing, even if the vendor claims to hold one. In practice, enforcement on hobby-scale sales of native species like P. regius and P. audax is minimal — but the requirement exists, and it is an entirely different question for imported species, where a permit is unambiguously required.

At state level, Florida runs its own arthropod import permit system through FDACS and many vendors simply refuse to ship there, and Hawaii is highly restrictive on all live animal imports. “No Florida or Hawaii” is a common listing note. Elsewhere we found no general state bans on keeping jumping spiders — but check your own state's agriculture department rather than taking a blog's word for it, including this one.

⚠️ The Beginner Mistakes That Actually Kill Them

Ranked by how often keepers report them, not by drama.

  1. Leaving live prey in the enclosure. Especially crickets, especially during pre-molt. Remove uneaten feeders within 24 hours, always.
  2. Disturbing a molting spider. Opening the sealed retreat “just to check.” Irreversible.
  3. Poor ventilation plus over-misting. Condensation, mold, stagnant air. This kills more jumping spiders than dryness does.
  4. Chasing a humidity number. Reading 45% on a cheap hygrometer and flooding the enclosure in response.
  5. A top-opening enclosure. Destroying the retreat at every feeding, and eventually catching a molt.
  6. An enclosure too big for the spider. It can't find its prey — and the prey survives to become mistake number one.
  7. Prey too large, or mealworms with intact mandibles. Prey smaller than the body, small mealworms only.
  8. Wild-caught feeders. Pesticide residue and imported parasites.
  9. Direct sunlight, or an unregulated heat mat. Fast, fatal, completely avoidable.
  10. Handling standing up over a hard floor. A fall ruptures the abdomen.
  11. Housing two together. They are solitary and they cannibalize. One per enclosure, no exceptions.
  12. Deep-cleaning the enclosure. Destroys the retreat and the spider's spatial map. Spot-clean, leave the silk.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Are jumping spiders good pets for beginners?

Yes, with one caveat. They are inexpensive, silent, odorless, need no walking, and fit on a shelf — genuinely one of the easiest exotic pets to house. The caveat is lifespan: expect roughly a year. If you want a pet that grows old with you, this isn't it. If you want a fascinating animal to observe closely for a season or two, it is close to perfect.

How long do pet jumping spiders live?

Realistically 8–14 months from hatching, with two years being exceptional. Published lab work on Phidippus gives roughly three to four months of adult life after the final molt. Females consistently outlive males. Buying a mature adult means buying the shortest-lived option — a captive-bred juvenile or sub-adult gives you most of that time.

Do jumping spiders bite, and is it dangerous?

They can bite if cornered or squeezed, but Salticidae do not appear on lists of medically significant spiders. Expect localized redness, itching and swelling for a day or two — soap, water and a cold compress. The genuine risk in handling runs the other way: a fall from standing height can rupture the spider's abdomen and kill it.

Do jumping spiders recognize their owners?

They have extraordinary vision and will reliably track you, approach the glass, and respond to movement and to your presence. Whether that constitutes recognition in any meaningful sense is not established, and anyone telling you definitively either way is going beyond the evidence. What's certain is that they are visually engaged with you in a way almost no other invertebrate is.

My jumping spider stopped eating — should I worry?

Usually not. Refusing food is the earliest and most reliable sign of pre-molt, and it can start weeks ahead. The discriminator is behavior: a pre-molt spider withdraws and eventually seals its retreat shut; a starving spider stays out and keeps hunting. If it's active, visible and getting thinner, check prey size, enclosure size and temperature.

Do jumping spiders need UVB or a heat lamp?

No to both. They need bright indirect light on roughly a 10–12 hour cycle — a window or a small LED on a timer is plenty. Salticids genuinely do see and use UV in courtship, which is a wonderful fact and not a husbandry requirement. Heat lamps are rejected across the board: radiant heat plus desiccation in a small enclosure is dangerous.

Can I keep two jumping spiders together?

No. They are solitary outside of courtship and they will fight and cannibalize. One spider per enclosure, always. The only exception is a narrow breeder practice with very young slings, which is not a pet-keeping recommendation.

Can I catch a jumping spider from my garden instead of buying one?

In most of the US, collecting a common native like Phidippus audax on your own property is legal and ethically defensible — they're abundant and not declining. The trade-offs are real though: unknown age (it may be an adult with weeks left), possible pesticide exposure, and possible mites. Check your own state's rules, and never collect from treated land.

How much does it cost to set up and keep one?

Roughly $70–$130 for a complete first setup excluding the spider, most of it one-time. The spider itself runs $20–$60 captive-bred depending on stage and sex. Ongoing costs are a feeder culture every few weeks — a few dollars a month.

The Short Version

Buy a captive-bred Phidippus regius juvenile. House it in something taller than it is wide, with a door on the front and vents on two opposing sides. Mist the walls every couple of days and let them dry. Feed prey smaller than its body, and take out anything it doesn't eat by the next day. When the retreat seals shut, walk away and don't come back for a week.

That's the whole job. Everything else in this guide is either the reasoning behind those five rules or the honest answer to what happens when one of them slips. Get them right and you'll spend the next year with an animal that watches you back — which, for something the size of a shirt button, is a genuinely strange and wonderful thing to have on your desk.

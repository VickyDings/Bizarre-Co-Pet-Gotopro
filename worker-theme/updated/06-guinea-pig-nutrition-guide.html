Feeding a guinea pig properly is genuinely simple once you understand the proportions — and genuinely dangerous when you get them wrong. 

Almost every serious guinea pig health problem we see traces back to the food bowl: dental disease from too little hay, obesity and gut trouble from too many pellets, bladder stones from the wrong greens, and scurvy from missing vitamin C.

The frustrating part is that the internet is full of contradictory guinea pig feeding charts, several of which are flatly wrong. This guide sticks to what veterinary sources actually say, tells you exactly what to put in the bowl each day, flags the two most common myths doing the rounds, and covers the emergency food every guinea pig owner should keep in the cabinet before they need it.

🥬 The Daily Plate, At a Glance

Grass hay Unlimited — roughly 80% of the diet
Fresh vegetables 1 cup per pig, per day
Pellets 1 tablespoon per pig, per day
Veg variety 5–6 different types daily
Vitamin C (adult) 10–25 mg/kg every day
Vitamin C (young/ill) 30+ mg/kg every day

Fruit & carrot Treats only — 1–2× a week
Fresh water is always available, changed daily.
🎉 Fun Fact #3

All twenty of a guinea pig's teeth grow continuously — not just the four incisors you can see, but every molar at the back too, at roughly 2 to 2.5 mm a week for life. Hay is what keeps them in check, and not because it is tough: grasses accumulate silica from the soil, so chewing hay is closer to working with a fine abrasive than to biting. Pellets and soft vegetables are swallowed with a very different jaw action and barely wear the molars at all, which is how a pig on an unlimited-pellet diet develops sharp molar spurs that cut the tongue and cheek. A pig that drops food, chews on one side or drools is almost always telling you about its back teeth.

🌾 Hay: 80% of Everything

If you remember one thing from this guide, make it this: unlimited grass hay, available 24 hours a day, forever. A guinea pig should eat roughly its own body size in hay every single day, and a pile bigger than the pig itself is exactly right.

Hay isn't filler — it does two jobs nothing else can. Guinea pig teeth grow continuously at around 2–3 mm a week, and only the long sideways grinding motion of chewing hay wears them down; without it, teeth overgrow into spurs that cut the tongue and cheeks. Hay's long fiber also keeps the gut physically moving, and a guinea pig gut that stops moving is a medical emergency.

Which hay?

  • Timothy hay — the standard staple for adults. Good fiber, correct calcium level. Second cutting is the usual sweet spot for softness versus stem.

  • Orchard grass — softer and sweeter; excellent for fussy eaters and a good alternative for owners with a timothy allergy.
  • Oat hay — crunchy with seed heads; brilliant as a variety hay mixed in, though not as a sole diet.
  • Meadow hay — a natural mix of grasses, great for foraging enrichment.
  • Alfalfa — not a daily hay for healthy adults. It's a legume, very high in calcium and protein, appropriate only for pups under six months, pregnant or nursing sows, or underweight pigs on veterinary advice. Fed to healthy adults it contributes to bladder stones.

Mixing two or three grass hays keeps things interesting and encourages more eating overall, which is exactly what you want.

💚 A simple hay test

Good hay smells sweet and grassy, feels slightly springy, and is green-to-pale-gold. Reject anything that smells musty or sour, looks gray or brown, or feels damp — moldy hay causes serious respiratory and digestive illness. Store hay somewhere cool, dry and airy, never sealed in plastic.

🥗 Fresh Food: One Cup Per Pig, Per Day

Each guinea pig should get about one cup of fresh vegetables daily, made up of small amounts of 5–6 different types, and weighted heavily towards leafy greens. Variety matters more than quantity: rotating foods spreads the mineral load and supports urinary tract health, which is why "the same bowl of kale every day" is a genuinely poor plan even though kale is a good food.

✅ Daily staples — safe every single day

These form the bulk of the fresh portion. Low in calcium, low in sugar, high in the fiber and moisture guinea pigs thrive on:

  • Romaine lettuce — the workhorse green
  • Green leaf, red leaf and butter lettuce
  • Cilantro (cilantro) — almost universally adored
  • Endive and escarole
  • Rocket / arugula — small amounts
  • Cucumber / zucchini — hydrating, but in moderation; too much cucumber causes loose droppings

🌶️ Daily vitamin C stars

These are the ones actually doing the vitamin C work, and they should appear most days:

  • Bell peppers — all colors. The single best daily vitamin C source, and most guinea pigs adore them. Red and yellow contain more vitamin C than green, but all are excellent. Remove the seeds and stalk.
  • Cherry tomatoes — small amounts. Genuinely high in vitamin C (see the myth section below).

⚖️ A few times a week — not daily

All perfectly safe and nutritious, but high in calcium, oxalates or gas-producing compounds, so rotate rather than repeat:

  • Kale, spinach, Swiss chard, spring greens, parsley — nutrient-dense and high in vitamin C, but the calcium and oxalate load contributes to bladder sludge and stones if fed daily
  • Broccoli, cauliflower leaves and stalks, cabbage, Brussels sprouts — fine in small amounts; too much causes uncomfortable gas
  • Pak choi, watercress, chicory, celery leaves, carrot tops, beetroot, asparagus, green beans, peas in the pod, parsnip
  • Herbs — basil, dill, mint, oregano, rosemary, sage, thyme. Small amounts, and brilliant for tempting a pig who has stopped eating

🌿 Garden forage — free, natural and excellent

If you have access to a chemical-free garden, guinea pigs love foraged greens: dandelion (leaves and flowers), clover, chickweed, plantain, cleavers, yarrow, sow thistle, chamomile, calendula, and the leaves and twigs of apple, pear, willow, bramble, strawberry and raspberry.

⚠️ Forage safety rules

Never pick from roadside verges (exhaust residue), anywhere dogs walk, or from lawns treated with weedkiller, fertilizer or pesticide. Never feed grass clippings from a mower — they begin fermenting almost immediately and cause serious digestive upset. Wash everything, and introduce one new plant at a time. If you can't identify it with certainty, don't feed it.

🍓 Treats only — once or twice a week

Guinea pigs have no off switch for sugar, and excess sugar drives obesity and gut imbalance. Keep these to a thin slice or a couple of berries, twice a week at most: apple (no seeds or pips), banana, strawberry, blueberry, melon, orange segment, grape, pear, and carrot — which surprises people, but carrots are high in sugar and are a treat, not a daily vegetable.

🚫 Never Feed These

⚠️ Genuinely dangerous

Toxic: rhubarb (leaves and stalk), avocado, chocolate, onion, garlic, leeks, chives, raw potato and potato tops, tomato leaves and vines, fruit seeds and pits, most houseplants and garden bulbs, and anything from a plant you can't identify.

Wrong for their digestive system: any meat, fish, eggs, dairy, peanut butter, nuts and seeds, bread, cereal, pasta, rice, crisps, and any processed human food.

Useless or harmful: iceberg lettuce (nearly no nutrition and causes diarrhea), muesli-style guinea pig mixes with seeds and colored pieces, yoghurt drops, and anything sold as a "treat stick" glued together with honey and seeds.

🔍 Two Feeding Myths Worth Correcting

You'll find plenty of guinea pig feeding charts online, and two errors turn up again and again. Both matter.

💚 Myth 1: "Guinea pigs can't have tomatoes"

Ripe tomato flesh is safe and genuinely high in vitamin C — the PDSA lists it among the vitamin C-rich vegetables, and cherry tomatoes in small amounts are a good addition to the rotation. What is toxic is the green part of the plant: the leaves, stems and vines contain solanine and should never be fed. Charts that ban tomatoes outright are confusing the fruit with the foliage. Give the flesh in moderation (it's acidic and a bit sugary), and bin the green bits.

💚 Myth 2: "Pellets should be 20% of the diet"

Several popular pie-chart graphics show pellets at 20% of a guinea pig's intake. That's far too much. Veterinary guidance — including the PDSA — is one tablespoon of pellets per guinea pig per day, which works out at roughly 5% of intake, with hay making up around 80% and fresh food the rest. Pellets are a supplement that guarantees vitamin C and trace minerals; they are not a meal. Over-feeding them displaces hay, and that's how dental disease and obesity start.

🎉 Fun Fact #1

Guinea pigs cannot make their own vitamin C, and the reason is a broken gene. Almost every mammal manufactures vitamin C in the liver using an enzyme called GULO; guinea pigs carry the gene for it, but theirs is a non-functional relic — and so is ours. Humans, other primates, fruit bats and guinea pigs are the odd ones out in the same way. That shared defect is precisely why guinea pigs became the animal of choice for scurvy and nutrition research in the early twentieth century, and it is the origin of "guinea pig" meaning a test subject. The animal on your lap is the reason we understand vitamin C at all.

💊 Vitamin C: The Daily Non-Negotiable

Guinea pigs lack the enzyme L-gulonolactone oxidase, which means — exactly like humans — they cannot manufacture their own vitamin C and must eat it every single day. Without it they develop scurvy, and it is not a slow or gentle illness.

💚 How much they need

Healthy adults: 10–25 mg per kilogram of body weight daily.
Young, pregnant, nursing or unwell pigs: 30 mg/kg or more.
For a typical 1 kg adult that's about 10–25 mg a day — comfortably covered by a portion of bell pepper plus fresh fortified pellets. A supplement tablet adds certainty on top.

Early scurvy is easy to miss because it looks like general malaise: reluctance to move, a rough or puffed-up coat, swollen or painful joints, limping, poor appetite, weight loss, bleeding gums, loose teeth, slow wound healing and diarrhea. It needs veterinary treatment.

⚠️ Never use vitamin C drops in the water bottle

They're widely sold and they're the worst option available. Vitamin C degrades quickly in water and light, so the dose is unreliable, and the drops taste unpleasant — so many guinea pigs simply drink less, risking dehydration and bladder problems on top of the deficiency you were trying to prevent. Use fresh peppers, fresh pellets, or a chewable tablet given by hand.

🪨 Bladder Stones and the Calcium Conversation

If a guinea pig is going to have a diet-driven health problem, this is overwhelmingly the one. Urinary stones and gritty "sludge" are common, painful, and expensive — surgery is frequently the only answer once a stone has formed — and the advice circulating about them online is some of the most confused in the hobby.

Here is the honest version. Guinea pigs absorb calcium from their food very efficiently and excrete the surplus through the kidneys rather than storing it, which is why their urine is naturally chalky and often dries to a white patch. That is normal. The problem arises when excess calcium meets oxalate and the two crystallize, most often as calcium carbonate or calcium oxalate stones.

What actually raises the risk:

Alfalfa hay past six months of age. Alfalfa is a legume, not a grass, and it carries roughly two to three times the calcium of timothy. It is right for pups under six months, for pregnant and nursing sows and for a pig recovering from illness. For a healthy adult it is a treat, not a staple — and adult pellets made on an alfalfa base are the version of this mistake people most often miss, because the bag does not say "alfalfa" on the front.

Too many pellets. An eighth of a cup per pig per day is plenty. Pellets are the most calcium-dense thing in the diet by a distance, and free-feeding them is a far bigger contributor than any vegetable.

Hard water. Rarely mentioned and genuinely relevant — in a very hard water area, a real share of daily calcium arrives through the bottle. If your kettle furs up quickly and you have had one stone already, filtered water is a reasonable conversation to have with your vet.

Not drinking enough. Dilute urine is the single best protection there is. Wet vegetables help, a second water bottle helps, and a pig that drinks well is doing more for its bladder than any amount of ingredient-avoidance.

And what does not deserve its reputation: the blanket ban on kale, parsley and spinach that gets repeated in every forum. Kale is high in calcium but very low in oxalate; spinach and parsley are high in both and are genuinely worth limiting. But these are a few grams of a one-cup daily portion. For a healthy pig with no stone history, rotating them in a couple of times a week is not what causes stones — a bowl of alfalfa pellets left down all day is. Treat variety as the goal and save the strict restriction for a pig that has actually had a stone, on the advice of the vet who removed it.

⚠️ Blood in the urine is a same-day call

Pink or red urine, straining, squeaking while urinating, a hunched posture or a wet, stained bottom all point at the urinary tract. Beetroot and some reds can tint urine harmlessly, so it is not automatically blood — but a stone lodged in the urethra is an emergency in hours, not days, and is far more common in boars because their urethra is longer and narrower. Do not wait to see whether it settles overnight.

🥕 Pellets: Small, Plain and Fresh

One tablespoon per pig per day, of a plain, uniform, grass-hay-based pellet fortified with stabilized vitamin C. That's it.

Adults need a timothy-based pellet with calcium in the 0.35–0.75% range. Pups under six months, and pregnant or nursing sows, need an alfalfa-based "young" formula for the extra calcium and protein — then switch across to the adult food at six months.

⚠️ Avoid muesli-style mixes entirely

Colorful mixes with seeds, flakes and dried fruit invite selective feeding — guinea pigs pick out the sweet bits and leave the fortified pellets behind. The result is an unbalanced diet, obesity and dental disease. Buy plain pellets where every piece looks identical, so there's nothing to pick around.

Buy pellets in small bags and store them cool and dark. The vitamin C in pellets degrades over roughly 90 days from manufacture, so a bargain sack is false economy — you end up feeding a vitamin C supplement that no longer contains much vitamin C.

🔎 How to Read a Pellet Label

Pellets are the smallest part of the diet and the easiest part to get wrong, because the bag is designed to appeal to you rather than to the pig. Four things decide whether a pellet is worth buying, and you can check all of them in the shop.

1. The first ingredient must be timothy or another grass. If it says alfalfa and the pig is over six months, put it back — that is the calcium problem above, sold by the sack. The exception is a pellet bought deliberately for a pup, a pregnant sow or a convalescent.

2. Nothing in it should be a different color from anything else. Muesli-style mixes with seeds, corn, colored biscuit pieces and dried banana are the worst thing on the shelf, and not because any one component is toxic. Given a mix, a guinea pig eats the sweet, fatty, low-fiber parts and leaves the fibrous pellet — selective feeding — so it ends up on a diet precisely inverted from the one the bag advertises. The bowl looks eaten; the nutrition is not. Plain, uniform, boring green-brown pellets are the correct answer.

3. The analysis. Aim for fiber above 20 percent (higher is better), protein around 13–18 percent, fat under about 4 percent, and calcium near 0.6 percent for an adult. Fiber is the number to lead on.

4. A mill or best-by date, and stabilized vitamin C. This is the one people skip. Vitamin C degrades in the bag — with light, heat and time — and a pellet that was adequate at milling can be worth very little six months later. Buy a bag you will finish in about six to eight weeks, buy the smaller size rather than the bulk sack, and store it sealed somewhere cool and dark. Never in the garage in summer.

Two habits worth building. Write the date you opened the bag on the bag. And treat pellets as a supplement to hay rather than a meal in their own right: an eighth of a cup, measured rather than poured, once a day.

⚠️ Why this matters so much for guinea pigs

A guinea pig that stops eating can develop gut stasis within 12–24 hours — the digestive system slows and then shuts down, which is painful and can be fatal. Because guinea pigs are prey animals that hide illness expertly, you often notice only once things are already serious. Having Critical Care already in the cabinet, rather than trying to source it at 9pm on a Sunday, genuinely saves lives.

How it's used

  • Mixing: roughly 2 parts water to 1 part powder for syringe feeding; about 1.5 parts water to 1 part powder for spoon feeding. Mix only what you're about to use — reconstitute fresh at each feed.
  • Amount: for a mammal still eating some food of its own, roughly 3 tablespoons per kilogram of body weight per day. If the animal is eating nothing at all, your vet will work from the full-feeding charts.
  • Frequency: divide the daily total into 4–6 small feeds across the day rather than one or two large ones.
  • Flavors: Anise and Apple-Banana are the common herbivore versions; there's also a Fine Grind formulation for feeding through narrower syringes and tubes.

🎉 Fun Fact #2

Roughly half of a guinea pig's nutrition never comes from the bowl. They produce two different kinds of dropping: the firm oval ones you clean up, and soft, shiny, strong-smelling cecotropes that they take directly from the anus and swallow whole, usually at night. Cecotropes are not waste — they are a package of B vitamins, vitamin K, amino acids and protein synthesized by the bacteria in the cecum, and eating them is the only way the pig gets that bounty. It matters practically: an overweight, arthritic or heavily pregnant pig that physically cannot reach can become malnourished on a textbook-perfect diet. Uneaten cecotropes squashed in the bedding are a genuine warning sign, not a housekeeping problem.

⚖️ Weight: The Number That Catches Everything Early

Guinea pigs are prey animals, and prey animals conceal illness for as long as they possibly can. By the time a pig is visibly unwell — hunched, fluffed, quiet in a corner — it has usually been unwell for some time. A digital kitchen scale that reads in grams costs very little and is the single most useful piece of husbandry equipment you will own, because weight falls before behavior changes.

Weigh weekly, on the same day, and write it down. A phone note is fine. What you want is the trend, not the number.

A healthy adult sow typically runs 700–1,050 g and a boar 900–1,200 g, but the breed and frame vary enough that your pig's own baseline matters far more than any published range.

What the changes mean: a swing of 20 to 30 g week to week is normal, and often just a full or empty gut. A loss of 30 to 50 g means look closely — check the teeth, check that hay is being eaten rather than just tossed around, check for a heat wave or a new food. A loss of 100 g, or any loss that continues for two weeks running, is a vet appointment, and dental disease is the first thing to rule out. Sudden gain matters too: it can be weight, but in a sow it can also be pregnancy or an ovarian cyst.

The reason this works so well is that the commonest chronic problem — molar overgrowth — produces almost no early signs except a pig that eats slightly less. On a scale it shows up weeks before anyone would notice it by eye.

🚨 A Guinea Pig That Stops Eating Is an Emergency

This is the most important paragraph in the guide. A guinea pig's digestive system is built to have food moving through it more or less constantly. They are hindgut fermenters: the work happens in a large cecum full of bacteria, and that system is driven by a continuous supply of fiber arriving from the front. Take the fiber away and the whole thing slows and then stops.

The condition is gastrointestinal stasis, and it is not simply "off their food." Once the gut slows, gas builds and becomes painful, the pain suppresses appetite further, the cecal bacteria shift toward the wrong populations, and the animal spirals. Twelve to twenty-four hours without eating is serious. Anything beyond that is life-threatening. Unlike a dog or a cat, a guinea pig cannot safely be left to see whether it picks up tomorrow.

Signs the gut has slowed: no droppings, or droppings suddenly much smaller and drier; refusing a favorite food offered by hand; sitting hunched with the coat fluffed; grinding teeth; a tight or tympanic belly; pressing the abdomen against the floor; a pig that is quiet when it would normally wheek at the fridge.

What to do: Call an exotics vet the same day — this is not a next-available-appointment problem. While you are arranging it, offer the most tempting foods you have (fresh cilantro, bell pepper), check that the water bottle is actually working rather than airlocked, and keep the pig warm. If you already have critical care formula in the cabinet, begin syringe-feeding: a guinea pig that is not eating needs fiber going in, and the formula is exactly that, ground fine enough to syringe.

Which is the argument for buying a tub of it before you need it. It keeps for a long while, it costs less than an emergency consultation, and the night you need it at 9pm no shop will be open. Warm it into a porridge-like paste, load a 1 ml syringe with the tip cut off, and give small amounts at the side of the mouth, letting the pig swallow between each. Never squirt a bolus to the back of the throat.

🔄 Changing Foods Safely

Guinea pig digestive systems dislike sudden change. When you switch pellet brands, introduce a new hay, or add a vegetable they've never had:

  • New pellets: mix over 7–14 days, starting at roughly a quarter new to three quarters old and shifting the ratio gradually.
  • New vegetables: one at a time, in a small amount, and wait 24–48 hours before adding another. That way if droppings go soft you know exactly which food caused it.
  • New guinea pigs: ask what they've been eating and continue it at first, changing only after they've settled in.
🎉 Fun Fact

Guinea pigs produce two kinds of droppings, and they eat one of them. Caecotrophs are soft, nutrient-rich pellets taken directly from the source and swallowed whole — a second pass through the gut that unlocks B vitamins and protein the first pass missed. A pig too overweight or arthritic to reach can become genuinely deficient, which is one more reason to keep them lean and mobile.

✅ Feeding: Do and Don't

Do

  • Keep unlimited grass hay in front of them at all times
  • Measure pellets — an eighth of a cup per pig, per day
  • Give one cup of fresh food per pig, per day, split over two meals
  • Provide a vitamin C source every single day, from food or a tablet
  • Rotate greens so no single vegetable dominates the week
  • Weigh weekly on a gram scale and write the number down
  • Buy a bag of pellets you will finish in six to eight weeks
  • Keep critical care formula in the cabinet before you need it

Don't

  • Don't feed alfalfa hay or alfalfa-based pellets to a healthy adult
  • Don't buy muesli mixes with seeds, corn or colored pieces
  • Don't put vitamin C drops in the water bottle — it degrades and puts them off drinking
  • Don't change foods abruptly; introduce one new item at a time
  • Don't feed iceberg lettuce, and never anything from the allium family
  • Don't leave pellets down all day for a pig to graze on
  • Don't assume a full bowl means a fed pig — check the hay is going down
  • Don't wait overnight on a pig that has stopped eating
Get the proportions right and almost everything else follows. Hay is the diet; the fresh cup is the vitamin C and the variety; the pellets are a small insurance policy. Most of the illness a guinea pig vet sees comes from those three being stacked in the wrong order. — The Pet-GoToPro Editorial Team

Frequently Asked Questions

Can guinea pigs eat tomatoes or not? The charts disagree.

They can. Ripe tomato flesh is safe and high in vitamin C — the PDSA lists it among the vitamin C-rich vegetables. Feed cherry tomatoes in small amounts as part of the rotation, since they're mildly acidic and sugary. What you must never feed is the green part of the plant: leaves, stems and vines contain solanine and are toxic. Charts that ban tomatoes outright are conflating the two.

How much are pellets really meant to be?

One tablespoon per guinea pig per day — roughly 5% of total intake. Charts showing pellets at 20% are wrong and lead to obesity, dental disease and reduced hay intake. Hay should be about 80% of what they eat, fresh vegetables the remainder.

Can guinea pigs eat rabbit food?

No. Rabbit pellets contain little or no vitamin C, because rabbits produce their own — feed them to a guinea pig and you're heading for scurvy. Always buy food labeled specifically for guinea pigs, and check the vitamin C is listed as a stabilized form.

My guinea pig has stopped eating. What do I do right now?

Treat it as an emergency and phone your vet today — gut stasis can develop within 12–24 hours. While arranging the appointment, offer their most tempting foods (fresh cilantro, bell pepper), make sure the water bottle is actually flowing, and if you have Critical Care and your vet advises it, begin syringe feeding. Don't wait until tomorrow to see if things improve.

Do I need to give vitamin C if I feed good pellets?

Fresh, well-stored pellets plus daily bell pepper usually covers it. But pellet vitamin C degrades within about 90 days of manufacture, and you rarely know how long a bag sat in a warehouse — so a daily tablet is cheap certainty, and it becomes genuinely important for pregnant, young, elderly or unwell pigs whose requirements are higher.

Is spinach or kale bad for guinea pigs?

Neither is bad — both are nutritious and high in vitamin C. The issue is frequency. They're high in calcium and oxalates, which contribute to bladder sludge and stones when fed every day. Include them a few times a week within a rotation of 5–6 different vegetables rather than as a daily staple.

How do I get a fussy guinea pig to eat more hay?

Offer two or three different grass hays side by side, place hay in more than one spot (including inside a hide, where nervous pigs feel safer eating), keep it off soiled bedding in a rack, and cut pellet quantity back to the correct tablespoon — pigs filling up on pellets have no appetite for hay. Fresh herbs scattered into the hay pile also work surprisingly well.

The Bottom Line

Guinea pig nutrition comes down to four numbers: unlimited hay, one cup of fresh vegetables per pig, one tablespoon of pellets per pig, and vitamin C every single day. Get those right, rotate the greens rather than repeating them, and keep fruit and carrot as occasional treats.

Then do the one thing most owners don't: buy a bag of Critical Care before you ever need it. Guinea pigs go downhill fast when they stop eating, and having it already in the cabinet — alongside the phone number of a guinea-pig-experienced vet — is the difference between a manageable problem and an emergency you're not equipped for.

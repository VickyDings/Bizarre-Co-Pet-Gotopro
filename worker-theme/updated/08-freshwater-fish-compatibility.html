Fish compatibility isn't about which species look nice together. It's about temperament, adult size, water temperature, swimming level, and whether one animal considers the other food. 

Almost every aquarium disaster starts the same way: someone stands in front of a wall of tanks, likes the look of six different fish, and buys them. Two weeks later the guppies have shredded fins, the shrimp have vanished, and the "algae eater" has grown to the size of a shoe.

The chart below covers the 22 groups you'll actually find in a US pet shop, and every pairing in it has been cross-checked so it never contradicts itself.

Print it, screenshot it, take it shopping. It'll save you more money than any product on this site.

⚠️ The Pairings People Get Wrong

Eight combinations account for most of the compatibility problems we see. If you're planning any of these, read this bit twice.

⚠️ Betta + male guppies — the classic mistake

Bright colors and long flowing fins are exactly what a male betta is programmed to attack — a fancy guppy looks like a rival betta. It rarely ends well. Female guppies in a large planted tank sometimes work, but it's a gamble on an individual fish's temperament.

⚠️ Angelfish + neon tetras — fine, until it isn't

The most-repeated pairing in the hobby, and it works beautifully for about eight months. Then the angelfish reaches adult size and neons become correctly-sized food. Angels are cichlids; if a fish fits in the mouth, it eventually goes in. Pair adult angels with larger tetras — black skirt, lemon, rummy-nose — instead.

⚠️ Fin-nippers + anything with long fins

Tiger barbs, serpae tetras and black skirt tetras nip. It's not aggression exactly — it's what they do — and the targets are bettas, gouramis, angelfish, guppies and fancy-finned anything. Keeping nippers in groups of eight or more genuinely helps, because they take it out on each other instead, but never house them with trailing fins.

⚠️ Snail-eating loaches + your snails

Clown loaches, yoyo loaches and skunk loaches are sold as the natural solution to a pest snail problem. They are — and they don't stop at pest snails. Your £8 nerites and mystery snails are on the same menu. Clown loaches also reach a foot long and need a 75-gallon-plus tank, which the shop won't mention.

⚠️ Goldfish + tropical fish

Two separate problems. First temperature: goldfish are coolwater fish that do best around 65–72°F, while most tropicals want 76–80°F — one of them is always uncomfortable. Second waste: goldfish produce enormous amounts of it, which overwhelms the filtration a community tank runs on. And once a goldfish is grown, small tetras are snacks. Keep goldfish with goldfish.

⚠️ Crayfish + literally anything

Sold as "freshwater lobsters" and marketed as community-safe. They aren't. Crayfish hunt at night, catching sleeping fish off plants and the substrate, and they'll clear a shrimp colony and a snail population in weeks. They're genuinely interesting animals — in a tank of their own.

⚠️ Anything + baby shrimp

Adult cherry shrimp survive alongside most peaceful community fish. Shrimplets do not. If you want a colony that actually breeds, you need either a shrimp-only tank or a heavily planted one with moss and hiding places — or you accept that your shrimp population will never grow.

⚠️ "Freshwater" crabs that aren't

Red claw crabs are the ones you'll see most often, and they need brackish water and land to climb out onto — kept fully submerged in freshwater they slowly decline. Vampire crabs need a paludarium. Thai micro crabs are the genuine exception: fully aquatic, tiny, and completely peaceful. Check which one you're actually buying before it goes in a community tank.

🎉 Fun Fact #1

A betta does not only breathe through its gills. Bettas are labyrinth fish — they carry a folded, lung-like organ above the gills that lets them take oxygen straight from the air, which is how their ancestors survived rice paddies that all but dried out. The practical consequence surprises people: a betta must be able to reach the surface. Seal a tank to the brim with no air gap, or fill it so high the lid sits on the water, and a betta will eventually drown in perfectly well-oxygenated water. Leave an inch of air, and keep it warm — gulping cold air can chill the organ.

🐟 Species Profiles: What Each One Actually Needs

Centerpiece & personality fish

1 · Betta (Siamese fighting fish)

Betta splendens
Size: 2.5–3"Min tank: 5 gal Temp: 76–82°F Level: Top/mid Temperament: Territorial

Never house two males together — that part is famous. What's less well known is how variable individuals are: some bettas ignore tank mates entirely, others attack everything. Always have a spare tank or a divider ready.

Good with: corydoras, kuhli loaches, otocinclus, snails, harlequin rasbora, ember tetras (in 10 gal+).

Avoid: guppies, gouramis, other bettas, fin-nippers, anything with flowing fins.

13 · Angelfish

Pterophyllum scalare
Size: 6" tall, 8"+ with fins Min tank: 29 gal (40+ better) Temp: 78–84°F Level: Mid Temperament: Semi-aggressive cichlid

Beautiful, intelligent and genuinely interactive — but they're cichlids, and they grow tall, so they need height as well as footprint. A breeding pair will claim half the tank and defend it fiercely.

Good with: corydoras, bristlenose plecos, larger tetras, rams, kuhli loaches.

Avoid: neon tetras (as adults), guppies, shrimp, fin-nippers, bettas.

12 · Gouramis (dwarf, honey, pearl)

Trichogaster / Trichopodus species
Size: 2–5"  Min tank: 20 gal (pearl: 29+)Temp: 75–82°F Level: Top/mid Temperament: Peaceful but territorial

Honey gouramis are the gentlest and the best beginner choice; dwarf gouramis are prettier but prone to a well-known iridovirus, so buy from a good source. Males of any species will squabble — keep one male per tank unless it's large.

Good with: tetras, rasboras, corydoras, livebearers, plecos, loaches.

Avoid: bettas, fin-nippers (they shred the long feelers), other male gouramis in small tanks.

14 · Rams & apistogramma (dwarf cichlids)

Mikrogeophagus ramirezi / Apistogramma spp.
Size: 2–3"Min tank: 20 gal Temp: 78–85°F (German blue), 72–79°F (Bolivian)Level: Bottom/mid Temperament: Peaceful, territorial when breeding

All the personality of a cichlid in a community-safe package. Bolivian rams are far hardier than German blue rams, which are widely captive-bred to fragility and want very warm, clean, soft water — a poor first cichlid despite being sold as one.

Good with: tetras, rasboras, corydoras, plecos, angelfish, gouramis.

Avoid: African cichlids, large cichlids, other pairs of the same species in small tanks.

Schooling fish — buy 6 minimum, 8–10 is better

2 · Small tetras & rasboras

Neon, cardinal, ember, rummy-nose · harlequin, chili rasbora
Size: 0.75–2"Min tank: 10–20 gal Temp: 72–80°FLevel: Mid Temperament: Peaceful

The backbone of most community tanks. A tight school of ten looks vastly better than three of four different species — resist the urge to mix. Neons and cardinals are lovely but genuinely sensitive to imperfect water, so put them in a tank that's been running for a few months rather than a freshly cycled one.

Good with: almost every peaceful community fish, corydoras, plecos, gouramis, rams.

Avoid: adult angelfish, African and large cichlids, goldfish.

3 · Fin-nippers (tiger barb, serpae tetra, black skirt)

Size: 2–3"Min tank: 20 gal long Temp: 74–80°F Level: Mid Temperament: Boisterous, nippy

Genuinely great fish in the right tank — active, colorful and hardy. The rule is simple: groups of 8+, and nothing with long fins. A small group of tiger barbs is far nippier than a large one, because the pecking order has nowhere to go.

Good with: danios, other barbs, corydoras, plecos, loaches, fast robust fish.

Avoid: bettas, gouramis, angelfish, guppies, shrimp.

4 · Danios (zebra, pearl, celestial pearl)

Danio rerio and relatives
Size: 1–2.5"Min tank: 10–20 gal Temp: 64–77°F Level: Top Temperament: Peaceful but very active

Among the hardiest fish sold and excellent for a newer tank. They're fast and constantly busy, which can stress out slow, placid fish. Their tolerance for cooler water makes them one of the very few tropicals that can share a tank with goldfish — though the waste load still makes it a compromise.

Good with: barbs, livebearers, corydoras, loaches, plecos.

Avoid: long-finned bettas, very shy or slow species.

🎉 Fun Fact #2

Removing the male does not stop the babies. Female livebearers — guppies, platies, mollies, swordtails — store sperm for up to six to eight months and can drop a brood every four weeks from a single mating months earlier. A female sold to you as "the only one in the tank" is very often already carrying four or five broods' worth. This is why livebearer tanks go from six fish to sixty in a year, and why rehoming the male in month one changes nothing at all.

Livebearers — the ones that breed whether you want them to or not

5 · Guppies & Endler's

Poecilia reticulata / Poecilia wingei
Size: 1–2.5"Min tank: 10 gal Temp: 74–82°F Level: Top/mid Temperament: Peaceful

Cheerful, colorful and enthusiastically reproductive. One female arrives already pregnant and can store sperm for months, so a mixed group becomes a population. Keep all-male groups unless you want fry, and if you do keep females, aim for two or three per male so no single female gets harassed.

Good with: tetras, rasboras, corydoras, otocinclus, plecos, other livebearers.

Avoid: bettas, fin-nippers, angelfish, any cichlid.

6 · Platies, mollies & swordtails

Xiphophorus / Poecilia species
Size: 2–5" Min tank: 20 gal (mollies 29+) Temp: 72–82°F Level: Mid/top Temperament: Peaceful, males can chase

Platies are among the best beginner fish there are — hardy, colorful and undemanding. Mollies are the fussy one: they want hard, mineral-rich water and often do poorly in soft water, which surprises people who bought them as easy. Swordtail males can be pushy, so give them length to swim.

Good with: tetras, danios, corydoras, gouramis, plecos, other livebearers.

Avoid: aggressive cichlids, goldfish, fin-nippers (with fancy varieties).

Bottom dwellers & clean-up crew

7 · Corydoras catfish

Corydoras species
Size: 1–3"Min tank: 10 gal (pygmy) / 20 gal Temp: 72–79°F Level: Bottom Temperament: Peaceful, social

The most universally compatible fish in the hobby — the one row on our chart that's green almost the whole way across. They're shoaling animals, so six minimum; a lone cory is a stressed cory. Give them sand or smooth gravel, because sharp substrate wears down their barbels and causes infections.

Important: they are not a cleaning crew. Corydoras need their own sinking food — they will starve while politely vacuuming up nothing in a tank where everything is eaten at the surface.

Good with: essentially every peaceful community fish, plus angelfish, gouramis and rams.

Avoid: African and large cichlids, goldfish, crayfish.

8 · Bristlenose pleco

Ancistrus species
Size: 4–6"Min tank: 20–29 gal Temp: 73–82°F Level: Bottom Temperament: Peaceful, males territorial

This is the pleco to buy — the common pleco sold beside it reaches 18–24 inches. Bristlenose stay manageable, genuinely eat algae, and are tough enough to live alongside semi-aggressive fish. They need driftwood: the cellulose is a dietary requirement, not decoration. Supplement with algae wafers and zucchini.

Good with: nearly everything, including angelfish and (often) African cichlids.

Avoid: other male bristlenose in small tanks, goldfish, crayfish.

9 · Otocinclus

Otocinclus species
Size: 1.5–2"Min tank: 10 gal Temp: 72–79°F Level: Bottom/glass Temperament: Peaceful, timid

Tiny, charming and the best true algae eater for a small tank — but they need a mature, established aquarium with real biofilm and soft green algae to graze on. Put them in a new tank and they starve. Most are still wild-caught and arrive stressed, so buy the plumpest ones you can see and keep six or more.

Good with: all peaceful community fish, shrimp, snails.

Avoid: new tanks, cichlids, anything large enough to eat them.

10 · Kuhli loach

Pangio kuhlii
Size: 3–4"Min tank: 20 gal Temp: 75–86°F Level: Bottom Temperament: Peaceful, shy

Little eel-like loaches that are entirely harmless and enormously entertaining once settled. Keep five or more — in smaller numbers they hide permanently and you'll never see them. Sand substrate and caves are essential, and a tight lid, because they explore.

Good with: bettas, tetras, rasboras, corydoras, gouramis, angelfish.

Avoid: large cichlids, crayfish, bare tanks with no hiding places.

11 · Snail-eating loaches (clown, yoyo, skunk)

Chromobotia / Botia species
Size: 5–12"Min tank: 40 gal (clown: 75+)Temp: 75–86°FLevel: Bottom Temperament: Boisterous, social

Intelligent, playful and genuinely long-lived — clown loaches can pass 20 years and reach a foot, which the 2-inch juvenile in the shop tank does not suggest. They're excellent fish for a big tank and a terrible impulse buy. They must be kept in groups of five or more.

Good with: barbs, danios, larger tetras, plecos, gouramis — in a large tank.

Avoid: all snails, shrimp, small tanks, being kept singly.

Invertebrates

18 · Cherry shrimp (neocaridina)

Size: 1–1.5"Min tank: 5 gal Temp: 65–78°F Level: Bottom/plants Temperament: Peaceful, prey

Fascinating, hardworking and cheap to start a colony with. The compatibility question isn't whether fish will kill adult shrimp — most won't — it's whether shrimplets survive to adulthood. In a fish community they usually don't. A dense carpet of java moss changes the odds dramatically.

Never use copper-based medications or many snail treatments: copper is lethal to all shrimp.

Good with: corydoras, otocinclus, bristlenose, snails, small peaceful nano fish.

Avoid: angelfish, all cichlids, fin-nippers, crayfish, goldfish, loaches.

19 · Amano shrimp

Caridina multidentata
Size: 2" Min tank: 10 gal Temp: 65–80°F Level: Bottom Temperament: Peaceful

Bigger and tougher than cherry shrimp, and the best algae-eating invertebrate you can buy — they'll clear hair algae that nothing else touches. Because of their size they survive in community tanks where cherries would be eaten. They won't breed in freshwater (larvae need brackish), so the population stays exactly where you put it.

Good with: most peaceful community fish, gouramis, rams, corydoras.

Avoid: cichlids, goldfish, crayfish, large predatory fish.

20 · Snails (nerite, mystery, ramshorn)

Size: 0.5–2" Min tank: 5 gal Temp: 65–82°F Level: All surfaces Temperament: Peaceful

Nerites are the best algae eaters and won't breed in freshwater — they lay harmless white eggs that never hatch. Mystery snails are big, personable and only breed above the waterline, so they're easy to control. Ramshorns multiply to match the food available, which makes them either a free clean-up crew or a nuisance depending on how much you overfeed.

All snails need calcium in the water for shell health, and none of them tolerate copper-based medications.

Good with: nearly all community fish and shrimp.

Avoid: snail-eating loaches, pufferfish, assassin snails, most cichlids, crayfish.

21 · Freshwater crabs

Size: 1–3" Setup: Usually paludarium Temp: 75–82°F Level: Bottom/land Temperament: Opportunistic

Read the species carefully before buying. Red claw crabs need brackish water and dry land to climb out onto — the most commonly mis-sold invertebrate in the trade. Vampire crabs need a planted paludarium, not an aquarium. Thai micro crabs are the genuine exception: fully aquatic, thumbnail-sized, and safe with nano fish and shrimp.

Most crabs are also escape artists and will catch small or sleeping fish given the chance.

Good with: a species-appropriate setup of their own; micro crabs with nano fish.

Avoid: standard community tanks, small fish, shrimp, anything they can grab.

22 · Crayfish / "freshwater lobsters"

Size: 3–6" Min tank: 20 gal, species only Temp: 65–80°F Level: Bottom Temperament: Predatory

Genuinely interesting animals with real personality — and completely unsuited to a community tank. They hunt at night, pulling sleeping fish off plants, and they'll eliminate a shrimp colony and a snail population within weeks. They're also cannibalistic when molting, so even keeping two together needs plenty of caves.

Good with: a tank of their own, with a secure lid.

Avoid: everything else.

🎉 Fun Fact #3

Amano shrimp cannot breed in your tank, and it is not for want of trying. They will spawn happily, but the larvae hatch as tiny free-swimming forms that need brackish water to develop and die within days in fresh water. Every Amano in the hobby is therefore either wild-caught or raised in a specialist facility that steps them through salinity by hand. It explains the price next to a cherry shrimp, and it is also why a group of Amanos will never overrun a tank the way cherries do — whatever you see in there is what you bought.

Cichlids & coldwater

15 · African cichlids (mbuna)

Size: 4–8" Min tank: 55 gal (75+ better) Temp: 76–82°F Level: All Temperament: Aggressive

Spectacular colors, and effectively their own hobby. They want hard, alkaline water (the opposite of what most community fish prefer), a rock-piled tank, and — counter-intuitively — heavy overstocking, which spreads aggression so no single fish is targeted. Almost nothing else can live with them; bristlenose plecos and some synodontis catfish are the usual exceptions.

Avoid: mixing with any community fish, soft water, understocking, small tanks.

16 · Large American cichlids (oscar, jack dempsey, convict)

Size: 6–14" Min tank: 55–125 gal by species Temp: 74–82°F Level: All Temperament: Aggressive, territorial

Oscars are famously personable — they recognize their owners and can be hand-fed — and famously messy, needing oversized filtration and big weekly water changes. A single oscar needs 75 gallons minimum. Convicts are the small exception at 5–6 inches, but they breed relentlessly and defend their fry against everything in the tank.

Avoid: any fish small enough to swallow, shrimp, snails, undersized tanks.

17 · Goldfish

Carassius auratus
Size: 6–8" fancy, 12"+ common Min tank: 20–40 gal fancy; pond for commons Temp: 65–72°F Level: All Temperament: Peaceful but messy

Coldwater fish that belong with other goldfish. Don't mix fancy varieties (fantails, orandas, ryukins) with single-tailed ones (commons, comets, shubunkins) either — the streamlined single-tails are far faster and outcompete the fancies for food, and commons reach a foot or more and truly need a pond.

Good with: other goldfish of the same body type; white cloud minnows in cooler setups.

Avoid: tropical fish, shrimp, small snails, anything needing warm water.

🎉 Fun Fact #4

A goldfish is a cold-water carp, and the bowl is the cruelest thing in the hobby. Given the space they need, common and comet goldfish reach ten to fourteen inches and routinely live twenty to thirty years; the verified record holder, a fish called Tish won at a fairground, died at 43. They are also heavy waste producers and prefer 65–72°F, which is why they belong nowhere near a tropical community tank — not out of aggression, but because the temperature that suits a tetra slowly cooks a goldfish's lifespan down to a couple of years.

🐟 The Aggression Nobody Warns You About

Every compatibility chart in the hobby, this one included, is built around which species can live together. That framing quietly hides the most common cause of a tank going wrong, which is not one species attacking another at all. It is a fish being kept in the wrong number of its own kind.

Too few of a schooling fish. A school is not decoration. For tetras, rasboras, danios and corydoras, the group is the animal's security system: it spreads risk, it tells each fish where to be, and it gives a nervous species somewhere to disappear into. Keep three neons instead of ten and you do not get a smaller school — you get three stressed fish that hide, color down, stop eating properly and pick on each other, because the behavior that would normally be spread across a dozen bodies is now concentrated on two. Six is the floor. Eight to ten is where they actually start behaving like the fish in the photograph.

The wrong sex ratio in livebearers. Male guppies, platies and swordtails court more or less constantly, and a lone female facing two or three males is harassed to exhaustion. The rule is one male to two or three females, or all-male, or all-female — never a pair, and never males outnumbering females.

Two males of anything territorial. Two male bettas is the famous case, but two male dwarf gouramis in a four-foot tank will do the same thing more slowly, and so will two male rams once one pairs off. With dwarf cichlids the violence usually arrives the week a pair forms, because the tank stops being a community and becomes somebody's nest site.

The odd one out. Three of something that pairs — three rams, three gouramis, three angelfish — is the worst possible number. Two bond, the third is hounded relentlessly, and there is nowhere in the tank it is allowed to be. Keep a pair, or keep six and let the aggression spread.

If a tank is tense and the chart says everything in it should get along, count the fish before you blame the species.

💧 Water Chemistry: The Half of the Chart Nobody Prints

Two fish can be perfectly peaceful, identically sized, and still be a bad idea, because the water that keeps one in good health slowly wears the other down. Compatibility charts almost never show this, and it is responsible for a great many of the tanks where "nothing is wrong, they just keep dying."

Three numbers matter, and they matter in this order.

Temperature is the one nobody can fudge. It has to genuinely overlap, not merely touch at the edges. Goldfish want 65–72°F and white cloud mountain minnows are similar; most community tropicals want 75–80°F; discus and German blue rams want 82–86°F. Housing a ram with a goldfish means one of them lives its whole life at the wrong end of its range, and the cost is not dramatic — it is a shorter life and a weaker immune system.

General hardness (GH) is dissolved calcium and magnesium, and it is what livebearers need and soft-water fish resent. Guppies, platies, mollies and African cichlids come from hard, mineral-rich water and struggle in very soft water — mollies especially, which are prone to shimmies and fin problems below about 10 dGH. Cardinal tetras, rasboras, chocolate gouramis and most wild-caught South Americans come from water so soft it barely registers, and in hard water they live, breed poorly and die early.

pH follows from KH and usually sorts itself out once hardness is right. A stable pH anywhere between 6.5 and 7.8 suits the overwhelming majority of community fish. Stability beats any target number — a rock-steady 7.8 is far kinder than a 6.8 that swings half a point every time you top the tank up.

Two practical notes. First, work with your tap water rather than against it — find out what comes out of your faucet, then stock to suit it. A tank chemically propped up with buffers is one forgotten water change away from a swing, and fish tolerate the wrong stable parameters far better than the right unstable ones. Second, the local shop's fish are already acclimated to local water, which is a genuine argument for buying locally rather than mail-ordering soft-water wild-caughts into a hard-water tank.

📏 Six Rules That Matter More Than the Chart

💚 Get these right and most compatibility problems never appear

1. Tank size fixes aggression. Most "incompatible" pairings are really space problems. The same two fish that fight in a 20 gallon often coexist in a 55 because there's room to avoid each other.

2. Buy schooling fish in proper groups. Six is the minimum, eight to ten is better. Under-schooled fish are stressed fish, and stressed fish nip, hide and get sick. A small group of tiger barbs is far nastier than a large one.

3. Match temperature ranges, not just temperament. Two peaceful species that want 68°F and 82°F will never both thrive. Check the overlap before you buy.

4. Match water hardness too. Mollies and African cichlids want hard, alkaline water; cardinal tetras, rams and most shrimp prefer soft and acidic. This quietly kills more fish than aggression does.

5. Fill the levels, don't crowd one. A good community uses the whole tank — top swimmers, mid-water schoolers, bottom dwellers. Six species that all occupy mid-water will feel far more crowded than the same number spread across all three.

6. If it fits in the mouth, it's food. Not aggression — just biology. Always compare adult sizes, never the sizes in the shop tank.

🛡️ Quarantine: The Four Weeks That Save the Tank

Nothing in this guide protects a community tank as reliably as a quarantine tank, and almost nobody sets one up until after the first wipeout. It does not need to be much: a bare ten-gallon, a sponge filter squeezed from the main tank's media, a heater and a lid. No substrate and no decor beyond a length of PVC pipe to hide in, because bare glass is easy to dose and easy to clean.

Four weeks is the number. Two weeks catches the obvious — ich, visible fungus, torn fins — but several of the things that flatten a stocked tank have longer runways. Ich has a temperature-dependent cycle that can stall at cooler temperatures; internal parasites from wild-caught stock surface slowly as weight loss and stringy white waste; columnaris often waits for the fish to be stressed again by the move into the display tank.

What to watch for, daily: flashing or rubbing against decor, clamped fins, rapid or one-sided gill movement, white spots the size of a grain of salt, cotton-like tufts, red or ulcerated patches, stringy pale feces, and any fish that is not eating by day three.

Treat only what you can see. Prophylactically dosing a quarantine tank with everything on the shelf breeds resistance, stresses the fish and can flatten the sponge filter's bacteria. The exception most keepers agree on is a general anti-parasite course for wild-caught species — otocinclus, many loaches, most Amano shrimp — which arrive carrying more than the shop can see.

One last use for the same tank: it is the hospital. A fish that needs medicating in a community tank means dosing shrimp, snails, plants and a biofilter that may not survive it. Having somewhere to move a sick fish converts a tank-wide emergency into one fish's problem.

⚠️ When It Goes Wrong Anyway

Sooner or later a tank turns. A gourami that was fine for six months starts patrolling, a pair of rams claims two-thirds of the floor, or the tiger barbs find the angelfish's fins. Acting early is the whole game, because fin damage invites infection and a fish that is being chased cannot rest.

Rearrange before you rehome. A great deal of aggression is territorial rather than personal. Moving the hardscape breaks up established territories and forces everyone to renegotiate, and it resolves a surprising number of disputes outright. Add height and sightline breaks while you are in there — tall plants, a piece of wood standing upright, anything that stops one fish seeing the entire tank from one corner.

Add numbers, not fish. If nipping started when a school dropped to four, the fix is usually to take it back up to ten rather than to remove the nipper.

Use a divider as a timeout, not a solution. A mesh divider gives a battered fish somewhere to heal and lets you keep feeding it properly. It is not a permanent arrangement for anything that needs the full tank.

Then be honest. If a fish has outgrown the tank or simply will not settle, the kind answer is to move it on — to a local fish club, a shop that takes returns, or another keeper with the right setup. Most independent shops will take a healthy fish back, and many will give store credit. What is never acceptable is releasing it into a pond, a stream or a storm drain: tropical fish mostly die slowly, and the ones that survive — goldfish and plecos especially — have established damaging wild populations on several continents.

✅ Stocking: Do and Don't

Do

  • Check adult size, not the size in the shop tank
  • Buy schooling fish six at a time, eight or ten for preference
  • Keep livebearers at one male to two or three females
  • Match temperature and hardness before temperament
  • Stock the bottom, middle and top so the tank is used evenly
  • Quarantine everything for four weeks, invertebrates included
  • Add one species at a time, a week or two apart
  • Plan the tank around the water your faucet actually gives you

Don't

  • Don't buy three of anything that pairs — the third gets hounded
  • Don't mix goldfish with tropicals; the temperature suits neither
  • Don't trust "they were fine together in the shop tank"
  • Don't keep a single cory, oto or kuhli — they need their own kind
  • Don't add a fin-nipper to anything long-finned and hope
  • Don't buy the last fish in a tank of dead ones, at any price
  • Don't buy a common pleco for a 20 gallon — it reaches 18 inches
  • Don't ever release an unwanted fish into local water
The chart tells you which fish can share water. It cannot tell you whether the tank is big enough, whether the school is large enough, or whether your faucet gives you the water either species wants. Those three questions decide far more outcomes than temperament ever does. — The Pet-GoToPro Editorial Team

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I keep a betta with other fish or not?

Often yes — in a 10 gallon or larger, heavily planted, with peaceful bottom dwellers like corydoras and kuhli loaches, or calm mid-water fish like harlequin rasbora and ember tetras. But temperament varies enormously between individual bettas, so always have a divider or spare tank ready. Never with guppies, gouramis or fin-nippers.

Why did my "peaceful" fish suddenly turn aggressive?

Usually one of four things: it matured and became territorial, it's breeding and defending a nest, the group is too small so the pecking order has nowhere to disperse, or the tank is too crowded for anyone to hold territory peacefully. Adding hiding places and breaking up sight lines with plants and decor fixes a surprising amount.

Will my fish eat my shrimp?

Adult shrimp survive alongside most peaceful community fish. Shrimplets get eaten by almost anything with a mouth. If you want a breeding colony, either keep shrimp alone or plant heavily with java moss so the babies have somewhere to hide. Never keep shrimp with cichlids, loaches, crayfish or large-mouthed fish.

How many fish can I actually put in my tank?

Ignore the inch-per-gallon rule — it's meaningless. Work from adult sizes, research each species' minimum tank, add stock slowly in small batches, and let your nitrate readings be the judge: if you can't hold nitrate under 40 ppm with a weekly 25% water change, you're overstocked regardless of what any rule says.

Can I add new fish to an established tank?

Yes, but slowly — a few at a time with two to three weeks between additions, so the bacteria colony can grow to match. Quarantine new arrivals in a separate tank for two to four weeks if you can; almost every disease outbreak in an established aquarium arrives with new fish. Rearranging the decor when you add fish also helps disrupt existing territories.

What's the most beginner-proof community you can build?

A 20 gallon long with harlequin rasbora, corydoras and a honey gourami. Everything in it is hardy, peaceful, wants the same temperature and water, and occupies a different level of the tank. Add a few amano shrimp and nerite snails and you have a genuinely low-drama aquarium.

The Bottom Line

Compatibility comes down to three questions, asked before you buy rather than after: will it fit in anyone's mouth when fully grown, does it want the same water and temperature, and does it have enough space and company of its own kind? Get those right and the chart above will rarely surprise you.

And when a shop assistant tells you a fish "stays small" or "grows to fit the tank" — check for yourself. That single habit will prevent most of the mistakes in this guide.

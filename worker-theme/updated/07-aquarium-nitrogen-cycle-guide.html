Here's the single most important fact in fishkeeping, and the one almost nobody is told at the till: a brand new aquarium cannot safely hold fish. 

Not on day one, not on day three. Fill a tank with water, add fish the same afternoon, and you are running a slow poisoning experiment — one that kills the majority of first fish and convinces a lot of people they've simply got "bad luck with fish."

What's missing isn't equipment or skill. It's bacteria. An aquarium is a closed box in which your fish are permanently living in their own waste, and the only thing that makes that survivable is an invisible colony of nitrifying bacteria that converts deadly waste into something harmless. Growing that colony is called cycling the tank, and it takes weeks.

This guide explains the nitrogen cycle in plain English, walks you through cycling a tank step by step with the exact test readings you're looking for, lists the equipment worth buying, and then gives you eight realistic tank-size scenarios — from a 2.5-gallon nano to a 75-gallon showpiece — with what fish actually work in each, and what filter and heater to pair with them.

🔬 The Nitrogen Cycle, Explained Simply

Think of it as a three-step relay race that your tank has to learn to run before fish can live in it.

Step 1 — Waste becomes ammonia

Fish excrete ammonia constantly, mostly through their gills. Uneaten food, fish poop, dying plant leaves and any deceased tank inhabitant break down into more of it. Ammonia is extremely toxic. Even 0.25 ppm burns gill tissue; sustained exposure causes gasping at the surface, red or inflamed gills, lethargy and death. There is no safe level of ammonia in a fish tank.

Step 2 — Bacteria turn ammonia into nitrite

Given time, a colony of ammonia-oxidizing bacteria (chiefly Nitrosomonas) establishes itself on every surface in the tank — but overwhelmingly inside your filter media, where water flows constantly past them. They consume ammonia and excrete nitrite.

Unfortunately nitrite is also highly toxic. It binds to fish hemoglobin and prevents oxygen transport — effectively suffocating the fish even in well-oxygenated water. Nitrite must also read zero.

Step 3 — Different bacteria turn nitrite into nitrate

A second colony (mainly Nitrospira) then consumes nitrite and produces nitrate. Nitrate is dramatically less toxic — fish tolerate moderate levels indefinitely — and this is where the natural cycle stops in a glass box.

Step 4 — You become the final stage

In a river, nitrate gets diluted away and consumed by plants. In your tank it simply accumulates. Your weekly water change is the last step of the nitrogen cycle, and that's why it isn't optional housekeeping — it's a core biological function you're performing on the tank's behalf. Live plants help by consuming nitrate, but they don't replace water changes.

🎉 Fun Fact #1

The bacteria you are growing never eat fish food. Nitrifiers are autotrophs — they build their bodies out of dissolved carbon dioxide exactly the way a plant does, and they take their energy from the ammonia molecule itself rather than from sunlight. This is why the old advice to "drop in a pinch of flake to feed the bacteria" misses the point. The flake does nothing for them directly; it only helps because it rots and releases ammonia. You can cycle a tank with a bottle of pure ammonia and nothing else, and the colony will be perfectly well fed.

💚 The one-sentence version

Fish waste → ammonia (deadly) → nitrite (deadly) → nitrate (tolerable) → water change (you). Cycling a tank simply means growing enough bacteria to complete the first two conversions before you add any fish.

☠️ New Tank Syndrome: Why First Fish Die

"New tank syndrome" is what happens when fish go into an uncycled tank. There are no bacteria yet, so ammonia climbs for a week or two, then nitrite climbs as the first colony appears, and the fish live through both spikes. Symptoms include gasping at the surface, clamped fins, hiding in corners, sitting on the bottom, loss of color, red or inflamed gills, and refusing food. Left unaddressed it is lethal.

It's tragically common because the standard shop advice — "let it run for 24 hours, then add a few hardy fish" — is simply wrong. Twenty-four hours dechlorinates the water; it does nothing whatsoever to grow bacteria.

🧫 Where the Bacteria Actually Live

Ask most new fishkeepers where the beneficial bacteria are and they will say "in the water." It is an easy assumption and it is almost entirely wrong, and the mistake leads directly to two of the most common ways people destroy a working cycle.

Nitrifying bacteria are sessile: they attach to a surface, secrete a sticky biofilm, and stay there. They do not drift. The overwhelming majority of a tank's colony — comfortably more than 90 percent in a filtered aquarium — lives on the filter media, because that is where the most oxygen-rich water passes over the most surface area per hour. The rest sits on the substrate, the glass, the decor and the plant leaves.

Two practical consequences fall out of that, and they matter more than almost anything else in this guide:

A water change cannot hurt your cycle. You could siphon out 90 percent of the water and the colony would barely notice, because the colony is not in the water you removed. People sit on their hands during a fish-in cycle, terrified that a water change will "reset the tank," and let ammonia climb while they wait. It will not reset anything. Change the water.

Filter maintenance absolutely can. Rinsing a sponge under a running tap does two things at once: the chlorine kills bacteria on contact, and the temperature shock kills more. Replacing a cartridge wholesale — which is exactly what the cartridge packaging tells you to do every month — throws the colony in the trash. Squeeze the media out in a bucket of the tank water you just siphoned off, and put the same media back.

The one place people get caught out is a filter that has been switched off. With no flow there is no oxygen delivery, and a packed biofilm starts dying within a couple of hours. If the power has been out for more than three or four hours, or you unplugged the filter for a water change and forgot it, do not simply switch it back on — rinse the media in tank water first so the dead material does not get pumped straight into the aquarium as an ammonia spike.

🎉 Fun Fact #2

Nitrifiers are among the slowest-growing bacteria you will ever deliberately cultivate. Under good conditions they double roughly every 15 hours. E. coli, for comparison, doubles about every 20 minutes. That single number explains the entire frustration of cycling: a colony that has to double fifteen or twenty times to reach useful size simply cannot do it in a weekend, and nothing you buy, add, warm or stir changes the arithmetic by much. Four to six weeks is not the manufacturer being cautious. It is the bacteria's growth rate.

🧪 How to Cycle a Tank: Fishless Cycling (Recommended)

Fishless cycling means growing the bacteria colony before any fish arrive, using a bottle of ammonia as the food source instead of live animals. Nothing suffers, you can push the colony harder than fish would survive, and you end up able to stock more heavily on day one. It's the method every experienced fish keeper uses.

What you need

  • A liquid test kit that reads ammonia, nitrite, nitrate and pH — the API Freshwater Master Kit is the standard. Avoid paper test strips; they're notoriously inaccurate, and during cycling accuracy is the whole game.
  • A pure ammonia source — aquarium ammonium chloride (Dr Tim's, Fritz Fishless Fuel) or plain household ammonia with no detergent, perfume or surfactant.
  • Bottled nitrifying bacteria — Dr Tim's One & Only, Fritz Zyme 7, or Seachem Stability. These genuinely shorten the wait.
  • Dechlorinator — chlorine and chloramine in tap water kill the very bacteria you're trying to grow.

The step-by-step

  1. Set the tank up completely. Substrate, filter running, heater set to about 26–28°C (79–82°F) — bacteria multiply faster when warm — decor and plants in, lid on. Dechlorinate the water.
  2. Dose ammonia to about 2 ppm and test to confirm. Follow the bottle's dosing instructions; overshooting to 5+ ppm actually stalls the cycle rather than speeding it up.
  3. Add your bottled bacteria and leave the filter running 24/7. Never turn a filter off overnight — the colony begins dying within hours without flow.
  4. Test every day or two. First you'll watch ammonia fall while nitrite rises. That's stage one working, and it usually appears within the first one to two weeks.
  5. Keep topping ammonia back up to 2 ppm whenever it drops to zero. Starve the new bacteria and they die back.
  6. Wait for nitrite to fall and nitrate to appear. This second stage is slower and is where most people give up. Nitrite can sit stubbornly high for a week or more — that's normal, keep going.
  7. The finish line: your tank is cycled when it can process 2 ppm of ammonia down to 0 ammonia AND 0 nitrite within 24 hours, with nitrate clearly present. Confirm it twice on consecutive days.
  8. Do a large water change (50%+) to bring the accumulated nitrate down, match the temperature, then add your first fish.

⚠️ The mistakes that stall a cycle


Rinsing filter media in tap water — chlorine kills your colony instantly. Always rinse media in old tank water you've siphoned out, and never replace all media at once.
Dosing ammonia far too high — above roughly 5 ppm the process inhibits itself.
Adding medications or "ammonia removers" mid-cycle — you're removing the bacteria's food supply.
Turning the filter off at night — the colony needs constant oxygenated flow.
Using test strips — they'll tell you the cycle is finished when it isn't.

How to speed it up legitimately

The single biggest accelerator is seeded media: a used filter sponge, a handful of gravel or a piece of biomedia from an established, healthy tank. A friend with a mature aquarium or a good local fish shop can cut weeks off the process. Beyond that: keep the water warm, keep the pH above 7 if you can (cycling slows badly in acidic water), keep the filter well oxygenated, and leave the lights off — you don't need an algae bloom on top of everything else.


🧬 Seeding From an Established Tank

If there is one shortcut worth taking, this is it. Seeded media does not speed the bacteria up — nothing does — it simply lets you skip most of the doubling by starting with a large colony instead of a handful of cells that drifted in.

What to ask for. In order of usefulness: a squeezed-out filter sponge, a bag of ceramic biomedia, a cup or two of established gravel, or in a pinch a piece of well-colonized decor. A cup of gravel from a friend's healthy tank will typically cut a six-week cycle to two. Half a mature filter's media can cut it to days.

How to move it. Bacteria start dying as soon as the media dries out or runs out of oxygen, so treat it like a live animal. Put it in a sealed bag or tub with tank water — enough to cover, but leave an air gap — and get it into your filter the same day. An hour in a bag is nothing. Overnight in a sealed bag with no air is a meaningful loss. Never let it dry, never rinse it in tap water, and do not refrigerate it.

Ask one question before accepting it. Has the donor tank had any illness, or any treatment, in the last three months? Seeded media carries whatever else lives in that tank, and that includes ich, columnaris, snails, planaria and the occasional unwanted hitchhiker. A cycle is four weeks. A disease introduced on day one can cost you the whole stock. If you do not know the tank's history well, take gravel or biomedia over a sponge and quarantine anything visible.

The bottled starter cultures occupy a middle ground. The ones that contain genuinely live, refrigerated nitrifying species can knock real time off a cycle; the shelf-stable ones sitting in a warm aisle mostly cannot, whatever the label claims. Treat any bottle as a helpful accelerator and keep testing — never as permission to add fish on day two.

🐟 Fish-In Cycling: If You Already Have Fish

If you're reading this after buying fish, don't panic — but you now have daily work to do until the tank catches up.

  • Test daily. Any reading of ammonia or nitrite above 0 needs action today.
  • Do 30–50% water changes whenever ammonia or nitrite is detectable, using dechlorinated, temperature-matched water. During a bad spike this may be daily.
  • Use Seachem Prime (or an equivalent detoxifier), which temporarily binds ammonia into a form fish tolerate while remaining available to your bacteria.
  • Feed lightly — every other day, tiny amounts. Less food in means less ammonia out.
  • Don't add any more fish until you have two consecutive weeks of 0 ammonia and 0 nitrite.

Expect this to take six to eight weeks, and expect some losses if the spike was severe before you caught it. It's harder on you and on the fish than fishless cycling — which is exactly why we recommend the other way round.

⚠️ Cycle crashes — why an established tank can suddenly go wrong

A mature tank can lose its bacteria overnight if you: replace all the filter media at once, rinse media in tap water, treat with antibiotics, leave the filter off for hours, or add far too many fish at once. If ammonia appears in a tank that's been fine for months, one of those five is nearly always the reason. Treat it like a fish-in cycle until the readings recover.

⚖️ Ammonia vs Ammonium: Why the Same Reading Means Different Things

This is the single most useful piece of chemistry in fishkeeping, and it is almost never explained at the point of sale. It is also the reason two people can post the identical test result and get opposite advice, with both of them right.

What your test kit measures is total ammonia nitrogen — everything in the tank in that chemical family, added together. But that total is actually two different substances in equilibrium. Free ammonia (NH₃) is uncharged, slips straight through a fish's gill membrane, and is the thing that burns. Ammonium (NH₄⁺) carries a charge, cannot cross the membrane nearly as easily, and is dramatically less harmful.

Which one you have is decided by pH, and to a lesser extent temperature. Acidic water pushes the balance hard toward harmless ammonium; alkaline water pushes it toward free ammonia. And because pH is a logarithmic scale, the shift is not gentle — it moves by roughly a factor of ten for every single pH unit.

Read across that table and the practical lesson is stark: 1 ppm of ammonia in a soft, slightly acidic tank at pH 6.5 is roughly seventy-five times less dangerous than the identical reading in a hard African cichlid tank at pH 8.5. Same number on the card, completely different emergency.

Two cautions before you relax about it. First, this is not a license to tolerate ammonia in soft water — it is an explanation of why you have a little more time to react, not a reason to skip the water change. Second, if you dose a conditioner that "detoxifies" ammonia, understand what it actually does: it binds free ammonia into a temporarily harmless form that your filter can still consume. That protection lasts somewhere around 24 to 48 hours, and standard liquid test kits read the bound form as though it were still there. A scary-looking number after dosing does not necessarily mean the product failed.

🎉 Fun Fact #3

Because the ammonia balance is set by pH, a water change can make things briefly worse if you get it wrong. Swap a large volume of soft, acidic tank water for hard, alkaline tap water while ammonia is present, and the pH jump converts a pool of harmless ammonium into free ammonia in minutes. The total reading does not move at all — the toxicity does. It is a genuinely counterintuitive trap, and the fix is simply to do two smaller changes a few hours apart rather than one big one.

🫙 2.5–5 gallons — the nano

Be honest with yourself about this size. Small volumes swing fast: a little waste becomes a big ammonia reading, and the temperature follows the room. These are advanced tanks marketed as beginner ones.

2.5 gallons realistically holds: a colony of cherry shrimp and a couple of snails. That's it, and it's genuinely charming — shrimp tanks are fascinating and low-maintenance once cycled.

5 gallons opens up: one betta (with a heater, a gentle filter and a lid — bettas jump), plus a nerite snail. Or a shrimp colony. Or a small group of chili rasbora if heavily planted, though 10 gallons suits them far better.

Never in this size: goldfish of any kind, any "starter" community fish, or a betta without a heater and filter. The cup-and-vase betta myth has killed more fish than almost any other piece of pet shop misinformation.

🐟 10 gallons — the true nano community

A big step up in stability and the smallest size we'd suggest for a beginner keeping actual fish. Best choose one of these:

  • Betta + cleanup crew — one betta, a nerite snail, maybe a few amano shrimp (temperament permitting)
  • A nano school — 8–10 chili rasbora, ember tetra or celestial pearl danio. Small fish in a proper group look far better than a mixed handful of singles
  • Pygmy corydoras — a group of 6+ C. pygmaeus or habrosus, which stay tiny and shoal beautifully
  • Male Endler's livebearers — males only, unless you want a population explosion

Not in a 10: fancy goldfish, common plecos (they reach 18 inches), most tetras, and anything sold as "grows to fit its tank" — that's a myth; stunted fish suffer organ damage.

🌿 20 gallons (long) — where it gets easy

The sweet spot for a first community tank, and the 20 long (30×12) beats the 20 high because swimming length matters more to fish than height. Stable, forgiving, and big enough for a proper display.

A classic stocking: 10–12 neon or cardinal tetras + 6 corydoras + one honey gourami as a centerpiece. Or harlequin rasbora with cherry barbs. Or a livebearer tank of platies and guppies (all-male groups avoid endless fry). A bristlenose pleco works here as a genuine algae eater — unlike the common pleco, which does not.

🪸 29 gallons — the tall display

Same 30-inch footprint as a 20 long but nine inches taller, which suits planted aquascapes and taller-bodied fish. Everything a 20 does, with room for bigger schools and a more substantial centerpiece: dwarf or pearl gourami, a Bolivian ram, or a small group of dwarf rainbowfish. Angelfish are possible but genuinely happier in a 40 breeder and up.

⭐ 40 gallon breeder — the enthusiast's favorite

If we could only recommend one tank, this is it. The 36×18 footprint gives far more usable floor space than a 55, which matters enormously for bottom dwellers and territorial fish, and it fits on a standard stand.

Options open up: a pair of angelfish, German blue or Bolivian rams, apistogramma, larger corydoras groups, a bristlenose pleco, big schools of tetras or rasbora. It's also the realistic minimum for two fancy goldfish if you'd rather keep coldwater — and remember goldfish are messy, so filter heavily.

📏 55 gallons — long, but narrow

The classic "big tank" and often the cheapest per gallon second-hand. One caveat worth knowing before you buy: it's only 13 inches front to back, which limits big-bodied fish and makes aquascaping tricky.

It's brilliant for: large schools that use the length — 20+ tetras, rainbowfish, danios, larger rasbora — plus a substantial cleanup crew and a centerpiece pair. Less good for: oscars and other large cichlids, who need turning room a 13-inch depth doesn't give them.

🏞️ 60 gallons — the better 55

Usually a 48×18 footprint: the same length as a 55 with five more inches of depth, and a much better tank for it. Everything a 55 can do plus genuine room for larger-bodied fish, proper aquascaping with depth, and bigger cichlid communities. If you're choosing between a 55 and a 60 and both fit, take the 60.

👑 75 gallons — the showpiece

The point where the hobby really opens up, and still a size most homes can accommodate. Enough water volume that the chemistry is remarkably forgiving, and enough footprint for genuine choice.

Realistic options: a large peaceful community with several big schools, a group of discus for the patient, a proper cichlid community, larger gourami and rainbowfish, or a single oscar (though 75 is the minimum for one, and they're extraordinarily messy — go canister filter and plan on big weekly changes).

At this size, plan the practicalities: 850 lbs needs a proper stand and a solid floor, and a Python water changer stops becoming a luxury and starts being the reason you keep up with maintenance.

🚫 Five Myths That Kill Fish

⚠️ Worth unlearning before you start

1. "One inch of fish per gallon." Useless. A 10-inch fish does not belong in a 10-gallon tank. Waste production scales with body mass, not length, and the rule ignores territory, swimming style and adult size entirely. Research each species' actual adult size and minimum tank.

2. "Goldfish live in bowls." No. Fancy goldfish need 20–40 gallons and heavy filtration; common and comet goldfish reach a foot or more and belong in ponds. They're among the messiest fish in the hobby.

3. "Bettas live in cups and vases." A betta needs 5 gallons minimum, heated to 25–27°C, filtered and lidded. They breathe air because their native waters are low in oxygen — not because they don't need clean, warm water.

4. "Fish grow to fit the tank." They don't. Body growth slows while internal organs keep growing, causing deformity and shortened life. That common pleco will reach 18 inches whatever tank it's in.

5. "A dirty tank needs a full clean." Stripping and scrubbing everything destroys your bacteria colony and triggers a new cycle. Change 25% of the water weekly, vacuum part of the substrate, and rinse filter media in old tank water only.

🚫 Why Your Cycle Stalled: The Eight Usual Causes

A stalled cycle looks like this: ammonia sitting stubbornly at the same number for a week, or nitrite that appeared and then simply refused to fall. It is almost never bad luck. In practice it is nearly always one of eight things, listed here roughly in order of how often they turn out to be the answer.

1. The pH crashed

This is the big one, and it is badly under-explained. Nitrification is an acid-producing process — it consumes carbonate hardness (KH) as it runs. In a tank with soft water and no buffer, weeks of cycling strip the KH out, and once it is gone the pH falls off a cliff. Below about pH 6.5 nitrification slows sharply; below 6.0 it effectively stops, and your colony sits there alive but idle. Test KH, not just pH. If KH is under 4 dKH, add crushed coral to the filter or a small dose of baking soda, bring it back up, and the cycle usually restarts within days.

2. You dosed ammonia too high

More is not faster. Above roughly 5 ppm, ammonia begins to inhibit the very bacteria that process nitrite, and people who dosed to 8 ppm "to be sure" routinely stall at the nitrite stage for weeks. Target 2 ppm, top back up to 2 ppm when it falls, and never chase a higher number.

3. Nitrite has gone off the chart

The same self-poisoning happens at the next step. Once nitrite climbs past about 5 ppm — and a purple-black test tube usually means far more than the card's top value — it suppresses its own consumers. A 50 percent water change to bring it back into a readable range will often restart a cycle that has been frozen for two weeks. Yes, changing water during a cycle is fine. The bacteria are in the filter.

4. The tank is too cold

Nitrifiers slow markedly below 68°F / 20°C and crawl below 60°F / 15°C. An unheated tank cycling in a cool room can take three times as long as the same tank at 80°F. During a fishless cycle there are no fish to consider, so push the heater to 80–84°F / 27–29°C and drop it back before stocking.

5. Untreated tap water went in

One forgotten top-up is enough. Chlorine kills the colony on contact, and chloramine is worse — it is more persistent, and when your conditioner breaks it apart it releases ammonia, which is why a tank can show an ammonia reading immediately after a water change with no fish in it. Dechlorinate everything, including top-ups, including the bucket you rinse media in.

6. Nothing was feeding the colony

A fishless cycle needs a continuous ammonia source. If you dosed once on day one and then waited, the colony starved and died back long before it reached useful size. Re-dose whenever ammonia reads zero.

7. Something in the water is antibacterial

Antibiotics will flatten a cycle, and so will many general-purpose medications, some algaecides, and copper treatments. So will residue from a bucket that was once used with household cleaner — keep one bucket, label it, and never let soap near it.

8. The test itself is wrong

Before you tear the tank apart, rule this out. Liquid test kits expire. The second reagent bottle in the common nitrate kits contains a solid that settles out and genuinely needs 30 seconds of hard shaking — an unshaken bottle reads low and has convinced a great many people that their cycle never finished. And nitrite and nitrate cards are easy to confuse at a glance. Test a glass of dechlorinated tap water as a control: it should read zero, zero, and whatever your supply's nitrate is.

🎉 Fun Fact #4

Half the bottled starter cultures on the shelf name the wrong bacterium. Textbooks credited Nitrobacter with the nitrite-to-nitrate step for most of a century, and product labels followed. Then in the late 1990s DNA work on actual aquarium filters found almost no Nitrobacter in them at all — the genus doing the work in freshwater tanks is Nitrospira. It gets stranger: in 2015 researchers discovered "comammox" Nitrospira, a single organism that performs the entire conversion from ammonia to nitrate by itself, overturning the two-organism model that the whole hobby's advice was built on. The cycle in your filter is very likely not the one in the diagram.

💚 Stock slowly, always

Even in a fully cycled tank, add fish in small batches with two to three weeks between them. Each addition increases the ammonia load, and the bacteria colony needs time to grow to match it. Dumping fifteen fish into a freshly cycled tank causes a mini-cycle and the losses that come with it.

✅ Cycling: Do and Don't

Do

  • Test KH as well as pH — a KH crash is the most common stall
  • Dose ammonia to 2 ppm and hold it there, never higher
  • Run the heater warm, 80–84°F, while there are no fish in the tank
  • Change water freely — the colony lives in the filter, not the water
  • Squeeze filter media out in old tank water, in a dedicated bucket
  • Beg a sponge or a cup of gravel from a healthy established tank
  • Wait for ammonia and nitrite to read zero in 24 hours before stocking
  • Add fish in small groups, a week or two apart

Don't

  • Don't rinse media under the tap — chlorine and shock, together
  • Don't replace a filter cartridge because the box says to
  • Don't trust "24 hours then add fish" — that only dechlorinates
  • Don't add more ammonia to go faster; above 5 ppm it stalls the cycle
  • Don't skip the dechlorinator on a top-up, however small
  • Don't restart the count because you did a water change — you did not undo anything
  • Don't add fish on the strength of a bottled starter alone
  • Don't read a stalled cycle as bad luck before you have tested KH
Nothing in this hobby rewards patience as directly as cycling does, and nothing punishes impatience as reliably. The four to six weeks are not a formality — they are the growth rate of a bacterium, and the fish are the ones who pay if you decide not to wait. — The Pet-GoToPro Editorial Team

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does cycling really take?

Typically 4–8 weeks unassisted, and 2–6 weeks with bottled bacteria. It can be as little as one to two weeks if you seed the filter with media from an established tank. The only thing that tells you it's finished is your test kit: 2 ppm of ammonia processed to 0 ammonia and 0 nitrite within 24 hours, with nitrate present.

Can I use bottled bacteria and add fish the same day?

Some products claim this and we'd be cautious. Bottled bacteria genuinely help and can shorten cycling substantially, but "instant" results depend on the bottle being fresh, correctly stored and correctly dosed — and you have no way to verify that. If you do try it, add only one or two hardy fish, test daily, and be ready to do water changes.

Where do the bacteria actually live?

Mostly in your filter media — that's why filter maintenance is the most dangerous routine job in fishkeeping. They also colonize substrate, decor and glass, but the filter holds the overwhelming majority, because it has constant oxygenated flow. Never replace all your media at once; if a cartridge must be changed, run the old and new together for a few weeks first.

Do live plants replace the need for water changes?

No. Plants consume nitrate and help a great deal — a heavily planted tank is more stable and grows less algae — but they don't remove everything that accumulates, and they can't replace the mineral replenishment a water change provides. Keep changing water weekly.

What's the best first fish for a beginner?

In a cycled 20 gallon: harlequin rasbora, platies, cherry barbs, zebra danios or a corydoras group are all hardy, peaceful and forgiving. Avoid starting with anything delicate — neons and cardinals are lovely but genuinely sensitive to imperfect water, so save them for a tank that's been running a few months.

My tank has been fine for months and ammonia suddenly appeared. Why?

Almost always one of five things: you replaced or rinsed filter media in tap water, you added too many fish at once, you medicated the tank, the filter stopped for a period, or something has died and is decaying out of sight. Check behind decor for a missing fish, and treat it as a fish-in cycle until readings recover.

Can I use tap water?

Yes, with a dechlorinator — that's what they're for. Just never add untreated tap water to a tank with fish or an established filter: chlorine and chloramine kill fish and your bacteria colony. If you're on well water, test it before use; nitrate levels in some well supplies are high enough to matter.

The Bottom Line

Fishkeeping isn't difficult, but it is unforgiving of the one shortcut everyone is encouraged to take. Cycle the tank before you buy a single fish. Buy a liquid test kit, dose ammonia, add bacteria, and wait the four to eight weeks — it's the least glamorous month in the hobby and the one that determines whether everything afterwards works.

Then go bigger than you were planning. A 20 long is easier than a 10, a 40 breeder is easier than a 20, and a 75 practically runs itself by comparison. More water means more stability, more room for fish that behave naturally, and far more forgiveness when — not if — you make a mistake.

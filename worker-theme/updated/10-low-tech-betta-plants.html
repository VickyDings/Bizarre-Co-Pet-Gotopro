Betta Tank Plants 2026: Low-Tech Planted Setup Guide

A betta in a bare glass box is a betta with nothing to do. Add live plants and you get a different fish within a week — one that patrols, rests on leaves near the surface, and flares at its own reflection far less often. This guide covers the low-tech planted betta tank: no CO₂ injection, no expensive lighting rig, and no plant that needs babysitting.

Wild Betta splendens don't live in open water. They live in shallow, slow, heavily vegetated habitat — rice paddies, marshes and roadside ditches across Thailand and Cambodia, where the water is warm, still and cluttered with plant matter from the substrate to the surface. A betta's entire body plan is built for that: long fins that are useless against current, a labyrinth organ for gulping air where oxygen is low, and an instinct to wedge itself into something and rest.

That last behavior is the one most owners never get to see, because a bare tank gives a betta nothing to wedge into. Plants aren't decoration in a betta tank. They're the difference between a fish that hides in a corner and a fish that uses the whole volume of water you bought for it.

🐟 What Live Plants Actually Do for a Betta

The water-quality argument is real but oversold: a few plants in a five-gallon tank will not replace your water changes. Plants do consume nitrate, and a well-planted tank drifts upward more slowly between changes, but the meaningful benefits for a betta specifically are behavioral.

Broad leaves near the surface become furniture. Bettas sleep resting on something, ideally within a body length of the air they need to breathe. An Anubias leaf, a Java Fern frond or a floating plant's root mass gives them that. Keepers who add a single broad-leaf plant near the top of the tank routinely report the fish claiming it within a day.

Sight breaks lower stress. A betta that can see the entire tank — and its own reflection in every wall — has no way to break line of sight. Planting so the fish can turn a corner and lose sight of what alarmed it visibly reduces persistent flaring and glass-surfing.

Biofilm becomes grazing. Mature plant surfaces grow a microbial film that bettas pick at throughout the day. It isn't nutrition in any serious sense, but it's occupation, and an occupied betta is a healthier one.

💚 The surface rule

Bettas breathe atmospheric air through a labyrinth organ and must be able to reach the surface easily. Floating plants are excellent — but leave roughly a third of the surface clear, and make sure the fish has an unobstructed vertical path to it. A surface completely carpeted in frogbit is a genuine hazard, not a lush aquascape.

The Five Plant Types — and How Deep Each One Goes

Here's the thing almost nobody tells beginners: the most common reason a new aquarium plant dies is not lighting, fertilizer or CO₂. It's planting depth. Two of the five plant types below rot within weeks if you bury them, and one of those two — Anubias — is simultaneously the plant most often recommended to beginners and the plant most often killed by them.

Every plant you can buy falls into one of these five categories, and each has exactly one depth rule.

1 · Stem Plants — Bury 2–3 Inches of Bare Stem

Examples: Bacopa caroliniana, Moneywort, Rotala rotundifolia, Ludwigia repens, Hornwort, Water Wisteria.

These are cuttings, not rooted plants. They arrive as a bundle wrapped in a metal strip or a rubber band, and the first thing you do is cut that off. Strip the leaves from the bottom two to three inches of each stem, then push the stems in one at a time, roughly an inch apart. Buried leaves rot, and the rot travels up the stem.

Stem plants are the fastest growers in a low-tech tank and the ones you'll be trimming most. That's a feature in a betta tank — fast growers soak up nitrate and shade the tank, and every trimming is a free new plant. Replant the top half and discard the woody bottom.

⚠️ Take the factory band off before planting

Every bundled stem plant ships wrapped this way. Left on, the band crushes the stems it's holding and the middle of the bundle turns to mush within two weeks — while the outside still looks fine, so you don't notice until the whole thing floats loose. Loose, lead-free strips used one stem at a time are a different thing entirely, and genuinely useful (see the gear section below).

2 · Rosette Plants — Crown Above the Substrate

Examples: Amazon Sword, Cryptocoryne wendtii, Vallisneria, Dwarf Sagittaria.

Rosette plants grow from a single central point, sending leaves up and roots down from the same collar. Bury the roots completely, but keep that collar sitting right at the substrate surface. Trim long, straggly roots back to about two inches first — short roots anchor, long roots coil and lift the plant back out overnight.

These are your heaviest root feeders. In plain gravel they will slowly strip their own outer leaves for nutrients, which is what yellowing lower foliage on an otherwise healthy sword actually means. Root tabs fix it.

💚 Crypt melt is not plant death

Nursery plants are usually grown out of water. When submerged, they shed almost every existing leaf and regrow submerged-form ones — a process that looks exactly like the plant dying. Leave the roots undisturbed, keep the water stable, and new leaves appear in two to four weeks. Pulling the plant out to "check it" is what actually kills it.

3 · Rhizome Plants — Rhizome Fully Exposed

Examples: Anubias barteri, Anubias nana, Java Fern, Bolbitis, Bucephalandra.

These grow from a thick horizontal stalk — the rhizome — that must stay in open water. Tie or glue it to driftwood, lava rock or a decoration and let the roots find their own grip over the following weeks. A dab of cyanoacrylate gel holds instantly; cotton thread works too and simply rots away once the roots have taken.

For a betta tank these are the best plants money can buy. They tolerate the lowest light of any group, need no substrate at all, and Anubias in particular produces thick, near-horizontal leaves that a betta will adopt as a bed within days. Position one about two-thirds of the way up the tank and watch what happens.

⚠️ The number one killer of Anubias and Java Fern

A buried rhizome suffocates, blackens and rots — and no amount of light, fertilizer or patience reverses it. If your Anubias is going soft and brown at the base while the leaves look fine, dig it out and re-attach it above the substrate today.

4 · Floating Plants — Never Planted at All

Examples: Amazon Frogbit, Salvinia, Red Root Floater, Dwarf Water Lettuce, duckweed.

Drop them on the water and leave them. They pull nutrients directly from the water column, they grow fast, and their trailing roots create exactly the kind of dim, cluttered surface zone a wild betta lives in. If you add one category of plant to a betta tank, make it this one.

The requirement is dry leaf tops and calm surface water. Angle your filter outflow downward, or clip a baffle over it. A spray bar that mists the leaves will melt them, and so will condensation dripping off a tight-fitting lid.

⚠️ Think hard before adding duckweed

Duckweed is effectively permanent. Each frond splits every few days, it survives being scooped, netted and siphoned, and a single leaf on a wet hand re-seeds a tank you just cleared. Frogbit and Salvinia give you the same surface cover in pieces large enough to remove by hand.

5 · Carpeting Species — Shallow and Spread Out

Examples: Monte Carlo, Micranthemum, Dwarf Hairgrass, Staurogyne repens.

Rinse the gel off a tissue-culture cup, split it into pea-sized portions and plant each one about half an inch deep on a one-inch grid. Planted deep, carpets stay stunted; planted in big clumps, they take months to knit together.

Be honest with yourself about this group: carpets need the most light of the five types, and in a low-tech tank without CO₂ they spread slowly at best. Monte Carlo and Staurogyne repens are the two most forgiving. If you want green ground cover in a low-tech betta tank with minimum effort, Java Moss on a stone achieves a similar look with none of the difficulty.

⚠️ Three things sold for betta tanks that don't belong in one

Plastic plants. Betta fins tear on stiff plastic edges, and torn fins invite fin rot. If you must use artificial plants, use silk.

"Lucky bamboo" sold as aquatic. Dracaena sanderiana is a houseplant. Fully submerged, it rots and fouls the water. Only the roots may sit in water, with the leaves in air.

Betta bulbs sold loose in tubs. Usually Aponogeton, and often long dead. If it hasn't sprouted in three weeks, remove it before it decays in the tank.

🧰 Planting Day, Step by Step

  1. Cycle the tank first. Plants do not replace cycling. If your tank isn't cycled, plant it anyway and cycle it planted — but the betta waits until ammonia and nitrite read zero.
  2. Drain to about a third full. Planting in shallow water is dramatically easier than reaching through a full tank, and disturbed substrate settles faster.
  3. Place hardscape before plants. Wood and stone go in first, because you'll be gluing rhizome plants to them and you don't want to move them afterward.
  4. Unpack and inspect everything. Cut the factory bands off bunches. Remove rock-wool from potted plants, teasing it out under a gentle tap rather than pulling. Rinse tissue-culture gel off completely. Trim damaged leaves and long roots.
  5. Plant back to front, tallest to shortest. Stems and rosettes at the back and sides, carpets at the front, rhizome plants glued to hardscape wherever the light is weakest.
  6. Push root tabs in near heavy feeders. One tab within an inch or two of each sword or crypt, buried fully. Skip this if you used aquasoil.
  7. Fill slowly onto a plate or your hand. A hard pour uproots everything you just planted. Then add floaters last, once the surface has stopped moving.

Expect the tank to look sparse and slightly sad for about three weeks. Low-tech planting is a slow-motion process, and resisting the urge to rearrange things during that window is most of the skill.

⚠️ Four Mistakes That Kill New Betta Tank Plants

  1. Leaving the factory band on. Cut it off and separate the stems before anything goes in the water. The crushed center of a bundle rots first and takes the rest with it.
  2. Burying the rhizome or the crown. Two of the five types are surface-sitters. This is the most common reason a healthy plant dies in its first month, and it's entirely avoidable.
  3. Panicking during the melt. Crypts and swords shed nearly every leaf after transplant. Leave the roots alone; submerged growth follows in two to four weeks.
  4. Adding light instead of patience. When a low-tech tank looks slow, the instinct is to run the light longer. That grows algae, not plants. Six to eight hours is the ceiling — if anything, shorten it.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Do betta fish actually need live plants?

They don't need them to survive, but they use them constantly when present. Plants provide resting surfaces near the surface, break line of sight to reduce stress and flaring, and give the fish a reason to explore the whole tank rather than one corner. Silk plants deliver some of the enrichment without the water-quality benefit; plastic plants risk tearing fins.

How many plants does a 5-gallon betta tank need?

Three to five plants is a realistic starting point: one rhizome plant on hardscape at mid-height, one or two substrate plants at the back, and a small handful of floaters. Aim for roughly half to two-thirds of the tank footprint planted, with clear swimming lanes and an unobstructed route to the surface.

Can I keep a planted betta tank without CO₂?

Yes — every plant recommended in this guide grows without injected CO₂. What you trade is speed. Low-tech tanks fill in over months rather than weeks, and demanding carpets and red stem plants generally won't thrive. Anubias, Java Fern, Hornwort, mosses and floaters are all genuinely low-tech plants.

Why are my new aquarium plants melting?

Most often it's normal transplant melt from emersed-grown stock converting to submerged growth — expected in cryptocorynes especially, and it resolves in two to four weeks. If melt continues past a month, check planting depth first (buried rhizome or crown), then nutrients, then light duration.

Will a betta eat or damage live plants?

No. Bettas are carnivores and ignore plants as food. They will rest on leaves, push through dense growth and occasionally dislodge a freshly planted stem — which is another reason to weight new stems and let them root properly before adding the fish.

What light do I need for a low-tech planted betta tank?

A basic LED rated for planted tanks, run six to eight hours a day on a timer, is enough for everything in this guide. Longer photoperiods and higher intensity mostly benefit algae in a tank without CO₂. If algae appears, cut the duration before you change anything else.

Should I add plants before or after the betta?

Before, ideally by two to four weeks. Plants get to root undisturbed, the tank finishes cycling, and any melt or ammonia leach from new substrate happens before a fish is exposed to it. The betta then arrives to a settled, established tank.

The Short Version

Buy an Anubias, glue it to a rock two-thirds up the tank, and pen a handful of Salvinia into one corner at the surface. That's a planted betta tank, and it will outlive most elaborate aquascapes because there's nothing in it to get wrong.

Everything else in this guide — the swords, the stems, the carpets, the fertilizer schedule — is what you add once you've watched your betta claim that first leaf and decided you want more of it. Get the depth right, give it three patient weeks, and the tank does the rest on its own.

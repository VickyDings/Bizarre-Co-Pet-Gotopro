Parakeets are sold as a starter bird, and that framing is responsible for most of what goes wrong with them. A budgie is a flock animal with a ten-year lifespan, a flight muscle that needs using, and a digestive system that a seed-only diet quietly destroys. Get the setup right and you have a decade of company. Get it wrong and you have a bird that dies at four of a preventable liver condition.

This guide covers the cage, the diet, the household hazards that kill birds fastest, and the first fortnight with a new parakeet. Every number here is one you can check against your own setup tonight.

🎉 Fun fact #1

Every budgie you have ever seen in a cage descends from a green and yellow bird. That is the only colour wild budgerigars come in — the blues, whites, greys, violets and albinos in the pet trade are all mutations selectively bred since the 1870s. The name itself is thought to come from the Gamilaraay word gidjirrigaa.

The cage: wide beats tall, and one number is non-negotiable

Budgies fly horizontally. In the wild they cross open scrub in fast, level bursts — they do not hover or climb the way a finch or a lovebird does. A tall narrow cage gives a parakeet almost nothing it can use. Floor plan matters more than height.

Bar spacing is a safety number, not a preference

Bars must be ½ inch (12 mm) apart or less. Wider than that and a budgie can push its head through and fail to pull it back out — a fatal entrapment that happens in minutes and is one of the most common preventable deaths in pet birds. Check the spacing with a ruler before you buy. Cages sold for cockatiels and conures frequently have ¾-inch bars and are not safe for a parakeet, whatever the shelf label says.

Two more things to check before you pay: the cage should be wider than it is tall, and the bars on at least two sides should run horizontally so the bird can climb. Round cages are a genuinely bad design — they offer no corner for a bird to retreat to, and a parakeet with nowhere to feel cornered-but-safe stays low-level stressed.

Perches: throw away the ones that came in the box

Nearly every cage ships with two identical smooth wooden dowels. A budgie standing on the same diameter, in the same grip, for sixteen hours a day develops pressure points on the same spot of the foot every time. Over months that becomes pododermatitis — bumblefoot: swelling, then ulceration, then a genuinely difficult infection to clear.

The fix costs very little. Replace the dowels with natural wood branches of varying diameter, roughly ⅜ to ¾ inch, so the foot closes differently on each one. Safe untreated woods include apple, pear, willow, elm, ash and manzanita. Avoid cherry, plum, apricot, peach, oak, yew and anything treated, painted or from a roadside.

Never use sandpaper perch covers

Sold as nail trimmers, they abrade the sole of the foot rather than the nail, and they are a direct route to the ulceration described above. Concrete or mineral perches are acceptable as one perch among several — never as the highest one, which is where the bird sleeps.

Place the highest perch away from the cage wall so the tail doesn't drag, and never directly above a food or water bowl. Two or three perches at different heights is plenty; a cage crowded with perches has no flight space left.

🎉 Fun fact #2

Budgies have zygodactyl feet — two toes pointing forward and two back, like a pair of pliers. It is a superb climbing grip and the reason a parakeet can hang sideways off cage bars without thinking about it. It is also exactly why perch diameter matters: that four-toed clamp closes to a fixed shape, and on an identical dowel it closes to the same fixed shape every time.

Diet: the seed myth is the biggest killer

Ask most people what a parakeet eats and they'll say seed. Seed is what parakeets choose, in the same way a child chooses chips — and an all-seed diet is the leading cause of premature death in pet budgies. Seed is high in fat, low in calcium, and deficient in vitamin A and iodine. The results are predictable and well documented: obesity, hepatic lipidosis (fatty liver disease), and thyroid goitre from iodine deficiency, which shows up as a clicking or wheezing sound as an enlarged thyroid presses on the airway.

Good vegetables: kale, romaine, chard, broccoli florets, grated carrot, bell pepper (including the seeds), peas, cilantro, dandelion greens, cooked sweet potato. Chop finely — a budgie has a small beak and ignores anything that looks like work.

Never feed: avocado, chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, onion, garlic, rhubarb, apple seeds, salty or fried human food. Avocado is the one people are most surprised by, and it is genuinely lethal to birds.

Converting a seed addict takes weeks, not days

A budgie will starve rather than eat something it doesn't recognise as food, so never switch cold turkey. Mix a small proportion of pellets into the seed and increase it over three to four weeks. Weigh your bird on a gram scale twice a week during the transition — a drop of more than 10% of body weight means stop and call an avian vet. Eating alongside your bird helps: budgies are flock feeders and copy what they see being eaten.

🚫 Myth buster

Parakeets do not need grit. They hull their seeds before swallowing, so there is no husk in the crop for grit to grind. Birds offered grit sometimes gorge on it, and crop or gizzard impaction follows. This advice persists from poultry keeping, where it is correct — and pigeons and doves do need it, because they swallow seed whole.

The household hazards that kill fastest

A bird's respiratory system is startlingly efficient — air passes through the lungs on both inhalation and exhalation. That efficiency is why canaries went down mines, and it is why airborne toxins that a human wouldn't notice can kill a parakeet in the next room within minutes.

PTFE fumes: the number one avian household killer

Non-stick coatings (PTFE, sold as Teflon and many other brand names) release fumes when overheated that cause fatal pulmonary haemorrhage in birds. There is no treatment and death is usually within 30 minutes. The risk is not limited to frying pans: air fryers, self-cleaning oven cycles, some space heaters, hair dryers, irons, drip trays, waffle makers and heat lamps can all carry PTFE coatings. If you keep birds, move to stainless steel or cast iron in the kitchen and check the specification of every heated appliance. Never keep a bird in or near the kitchen.

Also airborne and also serious: aerosol sprays, scented candles, incense, plug-in air fresheners, oven cleaner, nail polish and remover, cigarette smoke, and essential oil diffusers. Ventilate well and take the bird out of the room before you clean.

The physical hazards are more ordinary and no less lethal: ceiling fans, open windows and doors, uncovered water (including a toilet or a full sink), hot hobs, other pets, and being stepped on. A flighted budgie out of its cage needs the room prepared and someone paying attention.

One bird or two?

This is the question people get wrong in both directions, and the honest answer depends entirely on your time.

A single budgie will bond harder to you and tame faster — but you are now its flock. That means several hours of company a day, every day, for a decade. A single bird left alone during work hours is a lonely animal, and loneliness in parrots shows up as feather damaging behaviour, screaming and repetitive habits.

A pair is the kinder default for most households. They will be somewhat less interested in you and slower to tame, but they preen each other, chatter, and are never alone. Two males generally coexist most easily. A male and female will breed given any cavity that resembles a nest, so no nest boxes, no coconut huts, and no dark enclosed spaces.

Skip the mirror

A mirror is not company. Single budgies frequently treat their reflection as a mate — courting it, regurgitating for it, and becoming hormonally frustrated by a bird that never responds. In females this can trigger chronic egg laying, which strips calcium and is genuinely dangerous. If your bird is lonely enough to need a mirror, it needs a companion or more of your time.

🎉 Fun fact #3

Budgies see in ultraviolet. Their crown and cheek feathers fluoresce under UV light, and studies of mate choice show they use that glow when picking a partner. Under normal room lighting you are seeing a substantially duller bird than another budgie sees — which is one argument for giving them some natural daylight.

Sleep, light and temperature

Budgies need 10 to 12 hours of dark, quiet sleep. A bird whose cage sits in a living room with the television on until midnight is chronically sleep-deprived, and sleep deprivation in parrots looks like irritability, biting and a weakened immune system. A breathable cage cover, or moving the cage to a quiet room at night, solves it.

Keep the room between 65 and 80°F. Budgies tolerate a range but not sudden swings, and draughts are worse than cold. Avoid placing the cage in direct sun with no shaded side — a bird that cannot move out of the sun will overheat.

Birds are prey animals and hide illness until they cannot. By the time a budgie looks obviously unwell it has usually been ill for a while, so the useful skill is noticing small changes early.

The one piece of advice every bird keeper repeats

A gram scale is the cheapest diagnostic tool you will ever own. Weigh your budgie weekly, at the same time of day, and write it down. Weight loss precedes visible symptoms by days or weeks, and a trend on paper is far more useful to a vet than "he's seemed a bit quiet."

🎉 Fun fact #4

The record for the largest vocabulary of any bird belongs to a budgie. Puck, an American budgerigar, was credited with 1,728 words. Budgies don't have vocal cords — they shape sound with the syrinx and tongue, which is why their speech has that fast, gravelly, slightly compressed quality.

Your first two weeks with a new parakeet

  1. Days 1–3: do nothing. Set the cage up before the bird arrives, then leave it alone beyond food, water and quiet presence in the room. A new budgie is terrified. Talking softly nearby while you read or work is the entire task.
  2. Days 4–7: become furniture. Sit beside the cage daily. Rest a hand on the outside without reaching in. The bird should stop reacting to your presence before you ask anything of it.
  3. Week 2: introduce millet. Hold a spray of millet through the bars. When the bird takes it there, move to holding it just inside, then in an open palm. Millet spray is the most effective training currency a budgie has.
  4. Week 2 onwards: the step-up. Press a finger gently against the chest just above the legs — a budgie's balance reflex does most of the work. Never grab. Never chase around the cage; that undoes a fortnight in one session.
  5. Ongoing: open the door. Once the bird steps up reliably, let it come out on its own terms in a bird-proofed room. Don't lift a bird out — let it choose, and it will come back.

Quarantine a second bird for 30–45 days

If you already keep a bird, a new arrival goes in a separate cage in a separate room — ideally on separate airspace — for at least a month. Psittacosis, avian bornavirus and air-sac mites all incubate silently, and a shop or rescue bird can look perfect on day one and take out your whole flock by day twenty.

More fun facts to impress people with

🎉 Fun fact #5

Wild budgie flocks in inland Australia can run to tens of thousands of birds, moving nomadically to follow rainfall rather than migrating on a fixed route. That flock instinct is precisely why a solitary budgie in an empty house struggles — it is wired to be surrounded.

🎉 Fun fact #6

The cere is a working hormone gauge. It is blue in mature males and tan or crusty brown in females, but it shifts with breeding condition and with illness — a male's blue cere turning brown can indicate a testicular tumour. That is why "his beak looks different" is a sentence avian vets take seriously.

🎉 Fun fact #7

Budgies are one of a very small number of species shown to synchronise movement to a musical beat. Beat perception appears to travel with vocal learning, which is why parrots can dance and your dog genuinely cannot.

🎉 Fun fact #8

The budgerigar is widely reckoned the third most popular pet in the world, after dogs and cats — and it has been kept in captivity since the 1850s. For an animal that common, it is remarkable how much of the standard advice about it is still wrong.

Frequently asked questions

How big should a parakeet cage be?

For one budgie, 18 × 18 × 18 inches is the absolute floor and 30 inches wide is what we'd actually buy. Prioritise width over height — budgies fly horizontally. Bar spacing must be ½ inch (12 mm) or less regardless of cage size, because wider bars risk fatal head entrapment.

What should I feed my parakeet?

Roughly 60–70% formulated pellets, 20–30% fresh vegetables and leafy greens, and no more than 10% seed as a treat. An all-seed diet is the leading cause of early death in pet budgies, through obesity, fatty liver disease and iodine deficiency. Keep a cuttlebone available permanently, and never feed avocado, chocolate, caffeine, onion or apple seeds.

Do parakeets need grit?

No. Budgies hull their seeds before swallowing, so grit has nothing to grind and can cause crop or gizzard impaction if the bird gorges on it. The advice comes from poultry and from pigeons and doves, which swallow seed whole and genuinely do need it.

Should I get one parakeet or two?

Two, unless you can give a single bird several hours of daily company for the next decade. A single budgie bonds harder and tames faster, but you become its entire flock. A pair is the kinder default. Avoid a male–female pair unless you want eggs, and never provide a nest box or any dark cavity.

How long do parakeets live?

Five to ten years typically, with twelve or more achievable on a good diet. Budgies on all-seed diets frequently die at four to six. Lifespan in this species is unusually responsive to husbandry — most of it is in your hands.

Why is non-stick cookware dangerous for birds?

Overheated PTFE coatings release fumes that cause fatal lung haemorrhage in birds, often within half an hour and with no effective treatment. It applies well beyond frying pans — air fryers, self-cleaning oven cycles, some space heaters, hair dryers and irons can carry the same coating. Never house a bird in or near the kitchen.

How do I tell if my parakeet is male or female?

Look at the cere, the fleshy band above the beak. In mature adults it is usually blue in males and tan, beige or crusty brown in females. It is unreliable in young birds under about four months, and in some colour mutations such as albinos and lutinos it never becomes definitive.

Should I clip my parakeet's wings?

Generally no. Flight is a budgie's primary exercise and a clipped bird loses cardiovascular conditioning and the ability to escape a fall or a hazard. Bird-proof the room instead. Where a vet advises clipping for a specific medical or safety reason, have the vet do it — a badly cut clip causes crash landings and keel injuries.

The five-minute check that catches most problems

Once a week: weigh the bird, look at the cere and feet, check the droppings on fresh paper, watch it breathe at rest for thirty seconds, and note whether the seed on the floor is hulled or whole. That routine catches the large majority of budgie health problems while they are still cheap and easy to fix.

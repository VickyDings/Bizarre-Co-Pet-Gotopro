An axolotl is one of the easiest exotic pets to keep alive — right up until the water gets warm.

Nearly every axolotl that dies young dies of one of three things: water above 74°F, a tank that was never cycled, or swallowed gravel. None of them are hard to avoid, and all three are baked into the way axolotls are usually sold.

The trouble is that axolotls get shelved next to tropical fish, and almost everything that applies to tropical fish is wrong for them. They need cold water, not heated. Still water, not filtered hard. And the medications sitting on the same shop shelf can kill them. This guide gives you the numbers, corrects the two pieces of advice that cause the most harm, and covers the kit worth having in the cupboard before you need it.

🌡️ Temperature: The Thing That Actually Kills Them

If you remember one thing from this guide, make it this: axolotls are cold-water animals, and most homes are already too warm for them.

They come from cool highland water. Sustained warmth suppresses the immune system and appetite, and fungal infection follows within days. There is no scenario in which an axolotl tank needs a heater — if a shop sells you a "starter kit" with one in it, take it out.

TemperatureStatusWhat you'll see
54–60°F / 12–16°CCoolSafe. Slower metabolism, reduced appetite. Used deliberately when treating illness.
60–68°F / 16–20°CIdealGood appetite, full forward gills, active at dusk
68–72°F / 20–22°CUpper limitSurvivable short-term. Start cooling now, don't wait.
72–76°F / 22–24°CStress zoneAppetite drops, gills shrink, floating, heavy slime. Immune system failing.
Above 76°F / 24°CEmergencyFungal and bacterial infection, organ stress, death within days

How to cool a tank, cheapest first

Work down this list. Most keepers never need to reach the bottom of it.

  • Put the tank in the coolest room — a basement or north-facing room away from windows and radiators. Free, and often enough on its own.
  • Lift the lid and run a clip-on fan across the surface. Evaporative cooling reliably buys 2–4°F. Top up with conditioned water as it evaporates.
  • Air-condition the room. Cooling the air is far more efficient than cooling the water.
  • Fit an aquarium chiller. The only method that holds an exact temperature through a heatwave. Expensive, but not optional in a hot climate.
  • Frozen water bottles are an emergency measure only — they cause swings, and swings are their own stressor.
A thermometer with an alarm set at 70°F is the cheapest insurance you can buy.

💧 Water Quality & Cycling

Axolotl skin is permeable. Water chemistry isn't just their surroundings — it passes straight into them, which is why water quality matters more here than it does for a fish.

The nitrogen cycle, briefly

Your axolotl produces ammonia, which is toxic. In a mature tank two groups of bacteria live in the filter: one converts ammonia to nitrite (also toxic), the other converts nitrite to nitrate (much less so). You export nitrate with weekly water changes. Growing those colonies before the animal arrives is called cycling, and it takes 4–8 weeks.

⚠️ Cycle the tank before you buy the animal

This is the most common beginner mistake and it usually shows up as chemical burns on the gills within two weeks. Set the tank up, dose ammonia to 2–4 ppm, and wait until a 2 ppm dose reads 0 ammonia and 0 nitrite 24 hours later. Only then buy your axolotl. If you already have one in an uncycled tank, move it to a tub with 100% daily water changes until the tank finishes.

💚 Use a liquid test kit, not paper strips

Strips are notoriously inaccurate for ammonia and nitrite — the two readings that actually matter. A liquid master kit costs about the same as two packs of strips and lasts hundreds of tests.

🪨 The Tank & What Goes In It

A single adult needs a 20-gallon long as an absolute minimum; a 29 or 40-gallon breeder is meaningfully better and barely costs more. What matters is floor area, because axolotls spend almost all their time walking on the bottom. A tall narrow tank is a worse home than a shallow wide one of the same volume.

Substrate — the decision that kills the most axolotls

Axolotls feed by suction: they lunge and vacuum in whatever is nearby. That reflex is why substrate is a life-or-death choice.

Fine sand, two hides, low-light plants — a finished adult setup.

✅ Safe

  • Bare glass — safest. Zero impaction risk, trivial to clean. Some axolotls slip on it; a few slate tiles or a sand patch fixes that.
  • Fine aquarium sand, under 1 mm — recommended for adults over about 5 inches. Natural, lets them grip, passes through the gut. Stir the top layer weekly.
  • Large smooth stones — only if every piece is clearly bigger than the axolotl's head, with no sharp edges.

🚫 Never

  • Gravel, pebbles, glass beads — swallowed during feeding strikes, causing blockage, refusal to eat and death. This is the most common avoidable killer in the hobby.

Filtration, flow and furniture

  • Gentle flow above all. Axolotls are ambush predators from still canal water. Constant current exhausts them and curls their gills permanently forward. A sponge filter is close to ideal; a canister needs its return baffled or spray-barred along the back glass.
  • Two hides per animal, each wide enough to turn around in. Terracotta pots on their side are cheap and perfect.
  • Low light or none. They have no eyelids and dislike bright light — ambient room light is plenty. Add floating plants for shade.
  • Low-light plants — Anubias, Java fern, Java moss, Marimo, Elodea. Tie rhizome plants to wood rather than burying them.
  • A secure lid. Axolotls occasionally launch themselves at the surface.

⚠️ No tankmates. None.

Fish nip at those feathery gills and carry parasites. Small fish, shrimp and snails get inhaled and cause impaction. Other axolotls bite each other's limbs and gills, and juveniles are outright cannibalistic. A solitary axolotl is not lonely — in the wild they meet mainly to breed.

🪱 Diet: Worms, Basically

Axolotls are obligate carnivores, and one food covers almost everything they need. Earthworms — Canadian or European nightcrawlers — are the closest thing to a complete axolotl diet that exists. A quality sinking pellet is the backup. Everything else is variety.

Adult9 in and upEvery 2–3 days1–3 nightcrawlers, or the same volume of pellets

✅ Good food

  • Earthworms (nightcrawlers) — the staple. High protein, cheap, near-complete nutrition. Rinse before feeding and cut to size for smaller animals.
  • Soft sinking axolotl pellets — an excellent backup and a fine everyday option when you can't get worms.
  • Live blackworms — ideal for juveniles and for tempting a fussy eater.
  • Frozen bloodworms & daphnia — good for young axolotls, but mostly water, so not an adult staple.

⚠️ Never feed these

Feeder fish (goldfish, minnows) — carry parasites and disease, and many contain thiaminase which destroys vitamin B1 over time.

Mealworms, superworms, crickets — hard chitin exoskeletons are poorly digested and cause impaction.

Snails and shelled shrimp — shell fragments are an impaction risk.

Beef heart, raw meat, cat food — fatty, foul the water badly, nutritionally wrong for an amphibian.

Wild-caught worms or insects — pesticide residue and parasites. Buy from a bait or worm supplier.

💚 Axolotls need no vitamin supplements

This surprises people coming from reptiles. Axolotls need no UVB lighting and no calcium or D3 dusting — a varied diet built on whole earthworms supplies what they need. The supplements that matter for an axolotl go into the water: a dechlorinator, beneficial bacteria while cycling, a mineral booster if your tap water is soft, and non-iodised salt kept ready for treatment baths.

🔍 Two Myths Worth Correcting

Two pieces of advice turn up constantly in axolotl care sheets, and both cause real harm.

💚 Myth 1: "Axolotls need shallow water"

This one is everywhere and it's backwards. Axolotls are fully aquatic and happily use the whole water column — anything from about 8 inches upwards is fine, and deeper water is actually easier to keep cool and chemically stable, because a larger volume changes temperature more slowly. Since heat is the number one killer, that stability works in your favour. The only genuine requirement is that flow stays low. Fill the tank properly and baffle the filter.

💚 Myth 2: "It's a fish tank, so fish medication is fine"

It absolutely is not, and this is the mistake most likely to kill an otherwise healthy animal. Copper-based treatments, used in many ich and parasite remedies, are highly toxic to amphibians. Tea-tree and melaleuca products — the popular "fix"-type remedies — are widely reported to harm amphibian skin. Salt is a short supervised bath, never a permanent tank additive. If your axolotl is unwell, move it to a separate container and get advice from an exotics vet rather than dosing the display tank.

🩺 Reading Your Axolotl

Axolotls tell you how they feel with their gills and their tail. Both are easy to read once you know what you're looking at.

✓ Healthy — full, feathery gills held out, straight tail
✕ Stressed — gills curled forward, hooked tail tip
  • Gills curled sharply forward over the head — stress from flow, heat or ammonia. An early warning, and it reverses once the cause is gone.
  • Gill filaments shrinking or going bald — poor water quality or sustained warmth.
  • Tail tip curled to one side — the same stress signal, and often the first one you'll notice.
  • Red, inflamed gill stalks or skin — ammonia or nitrite burn. Test immediately.
  • White cottony tufts — fungal infection, usually starting on the gills. Fix temperature and water first.
  • Floating and unable to sink — overfeeding, trapped gas or heat. Persistent floating with a firm belly suggests impaction.
  • Refusing food for several days — check temperature, then water, then impaction, in that order.
  • Constant surfacing for air — occasional gulps are normal; constant trips are not.

🚑 Three treatments worth knowing before you need them

Read these now, while nothing is wrong. All three are things you may have to do at 9pm on a Sunday.

1. Tubbing

The universal first response.

Move the axolotl to a clean lidded plastic tub with enough dechlorinated, temperature-matched water to cover it comfortably, and change 100% of the water daily. Tubbing gives perfect water quality, lets you watch appetite and waste closely, and removes the animal from whatever caused the problem.

It's the right answer to an uncycled tank, a parameter spike, an injury, or "something looks off and I'm not sure why".

2. Salt baths

For fungus.

Dissolve 2–3 teaspoons (roughly 10–15 g) of non-iodised aquarium or rock salt per litre of dechlorinated, tank-temperature water. Bathe for 10–15 minutes, once or twice daily for up to three days, returning the animal to clean water each time.

Stay and watch the whole time — if it thrashes or goes rigid, end the bath early. Never salt the main tank.

3. Fridging

For impaction and stubborn illness.

Slowing the metabolism right down can buy time and help pass a blockage. Put the tub of dechlorinated water in a fridge held at 41–46°F (5–8°C), cool it gradually over several hours rather than in one step, change 100% of the water daily, and don't feed.

Two weeks is a normal maximum, and warming back up must be equally gradual. This is a real medical intervention — do it with an exotics vet's input, not as a first guess.

⚠️ Find your exotics vet on day one

Very few general practices treat amphibians. Search for an exotic-animal or aquatic vet in your area, call ahead, and confirm they see axolotls before you have a sick animal and a weekend to get through. Save the number next to your tank readings. Quarantine any new arrival for 30 days.

🎉 Fun Fact

Axolotls never grow up. Almost every other amphibian hatches as an aquatic larva then metamorphoses — loses its gills, grows lungs, walks onto land. Axolotls skip that step entirely and keep their larval body for life: feathery external gills, a tail fin, lidless eyes. Biologists call it neoteny. They also regrow lost limbs, tails, gills and parts of the heart and spinal cord, scar-free — which is why they're one of the most-studied animals in regenerative medicine.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do axolotls need a heater?

No — never. Axolotls are cool-water animals needing 60–68°F (16–20°C). Most homes are already at or above the top of that range, so the equipment you may need is a chiller or a clip-on fan, not a heater. If a starter kit arrives with a heater in it, set the heater aside.

Do axolotls need shallow water?

No, and this myth is everywhere. Anything from about 8 inches upwards is fine, and deeper water is actually easier to keep cool and chemically stable — which helps, because heat is the main killer. The only real requirement is that flow stays low. Fill the tank properly and baffle the filter.

Can axolotls live with fish?

Realistically no. Fish nip at those feathery gills and can introduce parasites; small fish, shrimp and snails get inhaled and become an impaction risk. Even other axolotls bite each other's limbs, and juveniles are cannibalistic. Keep them singly — a solitary axolotl is not lonely.

What substrate is safe for an axolotl?

Bare glass or fine aquarium sand under 1 mm. Axolotls feed by suction and will inhale whatever is near the food, so gravel, pebbles and glass beads cause fatal impaction — this is the most common avoidable killer. Wait until the animal is past about 5 inches before adding sand.

How often should I feed my axolotl?

Adults over about 9 inches eat every 2–3 days. Sub-adults eat every other day, juveniles daily, and hatchlings twice a day on live food. Offer as much as the animal takes in one sitting — roughly the volume of its own head — and remove anything uneaten within half an hour.

Why won't my axolotl eat?

Work through three things in order: temperature (is it above 70°F?), water quality (test ammonia and nitrite), and impaction (is the belly firm, has it passed waste?). Those three explain the large majority of hunger strikes. Only once all three check out is it worth trying a different food.

Can I use normal fish medication on an axolotl?

No, and this is the mistake most likely to kill a healthy animal. Copper-based treatments, common in ich and parasite remedies, are highly toxic to amphibians, and tea-tree "fix"-type products are widely reported to harm amphibian skin. Treat in a separate container with advice from an exotics vet rather than dosing the display tank.

Do axolotls need calcium or vitamin supplements?

No. Unlike reptiles, axolotls need no calcium dusting, no D3 and no UVB. A diet built on whole earthworms with a quality sinking pellet as backup covers their needs. The supplements that matter go into the water: a dechlorinator every water change, beneficial bacteria while cycling, a GH booster on soft water, and non-iodised salt kept ready for treatment baths.

Are axolotls legal where I live?

Quite possibly not. In the US they're banned outright in California and Washington D.C., and banned in practice in Maine and New Jersey where the permits aren't issued for pets. Hawaii and New Mexico allow them with an import permit. In Canada they're illegal in British Columbia, New Brunswick and PEI. The UK needs no licence. Check with your own wildlife agency before buying — see the table above.

How long do axolotls live?

Ten to fifteen years in captivity, and well-kept animals occasionally reach twenty. Cool, clean, stable water is the single biggest factor in reaching the top of that range. Treat it as a decade-long commitment rather than a starter pet.

The Bottom Line

Axolotl care comes down to four things: water between 60 and 68°F with a cooling plan ready before summer, a tank cycled to 0 ammonia and 0 nitrite before the animal arrives, bare glass or fine sand instead of gravel, and earthworms every two to three days. Get those right and you have a decade-long pet that asks very little of you.

Then do the thing most owners skip: buy the thermometer with the alarm, and put a plastic tub and a bag of non-iodised salt in the cupboard. The thermometer catches the problem, and the tub and salt are what you'll reach for at 9pm on a Sunday when you can't get to a vet until Monday.

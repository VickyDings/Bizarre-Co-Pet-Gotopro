A cat arrives with almost nothing. A carrier, a bag of whatever food the shelter was using, and a leaflet. Compare that to a puppy, which arrives with a crate, a bed, a harness and a list, and you start to see why so many first-time cat owners furnish the whole house for the human and buy the cat a plastic tray and a fluffy donut bed. The cat then spends a year telling them what is missing, in the only language it has: scratching the sofa arm, peeing on the bath mat, yowling at 4am, and sitting on top of the refrigerator because it is the only high place in the house. Almost none of that is a behavior problem. It is a furniture problem.

This guide is the setup we would give a friend who has just said yes to a cat and has two weeks to get ready. We will cover how much vertical space a cat actually needs and how to buy it without spending $300, why the standard litter box is roughly half the size it should be and what the n+1 rule really means, where boxes go and the three placements that cause most accidents, how to set up food and water so a cat drinks enough, how to redirect scratching instead of fighting it, and what a normal day, week and year of care looks like. We will finish on the health signs that are genuinely urgent — because a male cat straining in the litter box with nothing coming out is a same-night emergency, and knowing that one fact is worth more than every product on this page.

Two cats, two levels, one window. Cats resolve most of their social tension vertically rather than by fighting — which is why a second perch is often cheaper than a second vet visit.

Before you bring one home: the fifteen-year version of the decision

A cat is not a low-commitment pet. It is a low-maintenance pet, which is a different thing, and the two get confused constantly. The daily labor is genuinely small — ten minutes of scooping and feeding. The commitment is fifteen to twenty years, across house moves, job changes, new partners, new babies and at least one period where money is tight. Most cats surrendered to shelters are between one and three years old, handed in by people who meant it when they adopted and whose circumstances changed. Think about your next five years honestly before the next five minutes.

Where you get the cat matters less than people think, with one exception. Shelters and rescues are the default and the right one: adult cats are already spayed, vaccinated, tested and known. A rescue can tell you whether a cat is good with dogs, children or other cats, because they have watched it. That information is worth more than a pedigree. The exception is the person selling kittens online with no vet records, no mother on show and a willingness to let them go at six weeks. Kittens separated before eight weeks, and ideally twelve, have measurably more behavior problems later — biting, litter box trouble, poor impulse control. Walk away from a six-week kitten even when it is the one you want most. Especially then.

Two cats are usually easier than one, which surprises people. A single cat left alone nine hours a day with no company and nothing to climb is the classic profile for destructive behavior and overgrooming. Two cats who get along entertain each other. The caveat is that "who get along" is doing heavy lifting in that sentence — bonded pairs from a shelter are a safe bet, two random adults thrown together are not. If you are adopting two, adopt two who already live together.

Vertical space: the thing almost everyone under-buys

The old advice is that a cat needs a bed, a bowl and a box. That advice produced a generation of cats sitting on kitchen counters and on top of wardrobes, and owners who assumed their cat was being naughty. A cat on the counter is not defying you. It is solving a problem you gave it. Cats evaluate a space vertically: height is where they sleep safely, watch from, and get away from each other and from you. A floor-level home with no legitimate high places is a studio apartment to them, however many square feet it has.

The rule of thumb: every cat in the house needs at least one dedicated perch above four feet, and in a multi-cat home those perches need to be in different rooms. This is the cheapest behavioral intervention available. A $45 cat tree placed well does more for a cat's quality of life than $200 of toys.

Three rules that decide the cat tree

Get these right and everything else is easy.

Stability beats height every time

A cat tests a new structure by pushing on it. If the top platform sways when a cat lands, it is recorded as unsafe and never used again — and you cannot undo that judgment later. Look for a base wider than 16 in, a low center of mass, and a wall strap on anything over 5 ft.

The cheap tall tower is the classic mistake: 60 in of height on a 12 in base, which wobbles, gets abandoned in a week, and teaches owners that cats do not like cat trees.

Platform depth matters more than platform count

Cats sleep curled or stretched, and a platform shallower than about 14 in supports neither. Six shallow shelves lose to two deep ones. Measure your cat lying stretched out and buy for that, not for the picture on the box.

Sisal, not carpet, on the posts. Carpet shreds into loops that catch claws, and it teaches a cat that carpet is for scratching — which your stairs will eventually pay for.

It has to be somewhere the cat already wants to be

A tree in the spare room is furniture. A tree by the window in the room where the family sits is a bed, a lookout and a retreat at once. Cats want to be near their people and above them, not away from them.

A route beats a destination

The best setups let a cat move around a room without touching the floor: tree to shelf to windowsill to cabinet. This matters enormously in a multi-cat house, because it lets a cat leave a room without walking past another cat.

Where two cats fight, look at the floor plan before you look at the cats. Most tension is a geography problem.

Where the cat tree goes

Put it against a window, in an occupied room. A window gives a cat moving things to watch, which is most of the enrichment an indoor cat gets for free, and the room choice tells the cat it is allowed to be part of the household. South or east-facing is a bonus — cats thermoregulate by seeking sun and will move around the house all day chasing it.

Avoid putting a tree directly beside a door that slams, a washing machine on a spin cycle, or a forced-air vent. Cats will tolerate noise they can predict and avoid places where they cannot. And keep the top platform at least 18 in clear of the ceiling, or a cat that jumps up will hit its head once and never go back.

⚠️ Never declaw a cat to protect furniture

Declawing is not a nail trim. It is amputation of the last bone of each toe — the equivalent of removing a human finger at the final joint. It is banned across much of Europe, in several US states and in a growing list of cities. Declawed cats show measurably higher rates of chronic pain, back pain from altered gait, litter box avoidance because digging hurts, and biting, because the cat has lost its first line of defense. Every scratching problem in this guide has a furniture-based solution. None of them require surgery.

Litter boxes: how many, how big, and where they go

Litter box avoidance is the leading behavioral reason cats are surrendered to shelters. It is also, more often than not, a solvable setup problem rather than a behavior problem — and the three variables that fix it are number, size and placement, in that order.

The n+1 rule: one box per cat, plus one spare. One cat, two boxes. Three cats, four boxes. The extra is not generosity, it is insurance — against one box being dirty when a cat needs it, against another cat guarding a doorway, and against a cat developing a preference you did not predict. People resist this rule on aesthetic grounds and then spend months cleaning urine off a rug. Two boxes for one cat is the single highest-return decision in this entire guide.

Size is where almost every commercial box fails. A litter box should be about one and a half times the length of your cat from nose to the base of the tail. For a typical 10 lb cat that is roughly 22–24 in of interior length. The standard box sold in pet stores is 18 in. Cats need room to turn, dig, position and cover, and a cat that cannot do all four in the box will stand with its front feet in and its back end out, which is exactly the situation that ends with a wet floor and an owner convinced the cat is spiteful. Storage totes make excellent, cheap, correctly-sized litter boxes and a good number of experienced cat owners use nothing else.

Placement is the third variable and the most frequently botched. Boxes go in quiet, low-traffic places that still have two exits — a cat will not use a box it can be cornered in. They do not go next to the washing machine, inside a cupboard with a swinging door, or in a basement a cat has to cross the whole house to reach. Critically, in a multi-floor home there should be a box on every floor, and in a multi-cat home the boxes must not all be in one room, because a single doorway is trivially easy for one cat to guard.

Finally, keep boxes well away from food and water. Cats will not eliminate near where they eat, and if the only box is beside the bowls, something has to give. Usually it is your carpet.

  1. Measure the cat, then buy the box. Nose to tail base, multiply by 1.5. Buy for the adult size if you have a kitten — you will not want to buy twice.
  2. Place boxes before the cat arrives. Moving a box later is harder than placing it right. Count the floors, count the cats, add one.
  3. Fill to 2–3 in of unscented fine clumping litter. No liner, no deodorizer, no baking soda. Cats smell all of it.
  4. Show the cat each box on day one. Place the cat in it gently, let it step out on its own. Do not hold it there or dig its paws in the litter — that teaches avoidance.
  5. Scoop twice a day, every day. Non-negotiable, and the most common failure. A cat's standard of clean is considerably higher than yours.
  6. Wash the box monthly with unscented soap and hot water. No bleach, no pine or citrus cleaners — cats find both aversive.
  7. Replace plastic boxes yearly. Claw scratches hold odor permanently, and eventually the box smells wrong no matter how clean it is.

💚 A cat that suddenly stops using the box is sick until a vet proves otherwise

Sudden-onset house soiling in a previously reliable cat is a medical sign far more often than a behavioral one. Urinary tract inflammation, crystals, kidney disease, diabetes and arthritis all present this way — the cat associates the box with pain and stops going near it. Book the vet first and work on behavior second. Reversing that order wastes weeks and lets something treatable progress.

A chair leg is chosen for the same reasons a post is: it is rigid, it is tall enough for a full stretch, and it does not move. Match those three and the cat will switch.

Scratching: redirect it, do not fight it

Scratching is not optional behavior and it is not destructive intent. Cats scratch to shed the dead outer sheath from their claws, to stretch the whole length of the spine and shoulders, and to mark territory — there are scent glands in the paw pads, so a scratched surface is both a visual and a chemical signpost. A cat that is prevented from scratching does not stop wanting to. It finds somewhere you like less.

The whole strategy is to make the right surface more attractive than the wrong one, and the attractiveness is mostly physics. A scratching post must be tall enough for a full stretch — at least 32 in for an average adult — and rigid enough not to move. Cats scratch with real force; a post that tips is rejected instantly. The surface should be vertical sisal rope or sisal fabric for most cats, though a minority are horizontal scratchers and want a flat cardboard pad instead. Watch what your cat already ruins: if it is the carpet, buy horizontal; if it is the sofa arm, buy vertical.

Placement follows the same logic as everything else: put the post where the cat already scratches, not where it looks tidy. Cats scratch on waking, so a post beside the sleeping spot gets used immediately. They also scratch at territorial boundaries, which is why the sofa by the front door takes the damage — it is the doorway into the home, and marking it is the point. Put a post there and the sofa is spared.

For furniture already in use, cover the spot temporarily with double-sided tape or a smooth throw, and put the new post directly in front of it. Remove the deterrent after about a month, once the habit has moved. Do not use water sprays or loud noises. They do not teach a cat to stop scratching; they teach a cat to scratch when you are not in the room, and they damage the relationship you need for everything else.

The first week: how to introduce a cat to a house

Almost every new-cat problem traces back to too much space too soon. A cat that is given the run of a house on day one has nowhere it understands, and the usual result is three weeks under a bed. One room, then expansion, is faster in the end even though it feels slower.

  1. Set up one room completely before the cat arrives. Box at one end, food and water at the other, a hiding place at floor level and a perch above it. A spare bedroom or a quiet office is ideal; a bathroom works.
  2. Open the carrier and walk away. Do not lift the cat out. Sit on the floor across the room, or leave entirely. Most cats come out within an hour of being left alone.
  3. Expect hiding for two to five days. This is normal and not a sign of a problem. Keep feeding on schedule, keep scooping, and resist the urge to pull the cat out to reassure it.
  4. Sit in the room twice a day and ignore the cat. Read aloud, work on a laptop, let the cat approach on its own schedule. Direct eye contact and reaching both slow this down. A slow blink is the one social gesture worth offering.
  5. Start wand play as soon as the cat will eat in front of you. Play is how a nervous cat stops thinking about you and starts thinking about the toy, and it is usually the moment things turn.
  6. Open the rest of the house once the cat is eating normally and greeting you. One room at a time, with the safe room still available. Keep the original box in place for several weeks after.
  7. If another cat already lives there, do not skip the scent stage. Swap bedding between the rooms for several days, then feed both cats on either side of a closed door, then allow brief visual contact through a cracked door or pen. Only then let them share space. Rushed introductions can take months to undo.

Food and water: the hydration problem nobody mentions

Cats evolved in arid environments eating prey that was roughly 70% water, and they inherited a weak thirst drive as a result. A cat on dry food alone takes in far less total water than one eating wet food, and does not reliably make up the difference at the bowl. Over years, that chronic mild dehydration is implicated in the two conditions that define feline old age: chronic kidney disease and lower urinary tract disease. This is the single most consequential thing on this page after the litter box count.

Feed wet food as the base of the diet, every day, not as a treat. Canned, pouched or raw — the format matters less than the moisture. If dry food suits your schedule, use it as a topper or in a puzzle feeder rather than as the whole ration. A cat eating two wet meals a day is getting most of its water with its food, which is exactly how the species is built to work.

Cats are obligate carnivores in the strict sense. They cannot synthesize taurine, arachidonic acid, or vitamin A from plant precursors, and a taurine-deficient diet causes dilated cardiomyopathy and irreversible retinal degeneration. There is no safe vegetarian diet for a cat. Any food you use should state that it meets AAFCO nutrient profiles for the cat's life stage — kitten, adult or all life stages — on the label.

On the water side, position beats product. Cats dislike drinking where they eat, dislike whiskers touching bowl sides, and strongly prefer moving water. Use wide shallow bowls rather than deep narrow ones, place water in several rooms away from the food, and consider a fountain. Fountains are not a gimmick — the moving water genuinely increases intake in most cats, and for any cat with a history of urinary problems that is a clinical benefit rather than a convenience.

⚠️ Foods and plants that are genuinely dangerous

Lilies — every part of a true lily, including pollen and vase water, causes acute kidney failure in cats. A cat brushing past one and grooming the pollen off its coat is enough. Do not have them in the house. Onion, garlic, chives and leeks damage red blood cells and are common in human leftovers and baby food. Grapes and raisins cause kidney injury. Chocolate, xylitol, alcohol and raw dough are all toxic. Cow's milk is not toxic but most adult cats are lactose intolerant and it causes diarrhea. Paracetamol/acetaminophen is lethal to cats at a single tablet — never give a cat human pain relief under any circumstances.

What every feline vet wishes owners knew

Health: what to watch, and what is an emergency

Cats are both predator and prey, and prey animals that advertise weakness do not survive. The instinct to conceal illness is intact in a house cat with no predators, which is why owners so often report that a cat "went downhill overnight" when the disease had been progressing for months. Your defenses are a monthly weight, hands-on contact every day, and knowing which signs cannot wait. Find a vet you trust before you need one, and ask now whether they have out-of-hours cover or refer to an emergency hospital — at 11pm on a Sunday is the wrong time to find out.

💚 A healthy cat

  • Stable weight month to month, with a waist visible from above and ribs you can feel but not see
  • Clear eyes and nose, no discharge, no squinting
  • Coat smooth and evenly groomed, no mats, no bald patches
  • Eats with enthusiasm and finishes meals in a normal time
  • Urinates two to four times a day in firm, well-covered clumps
  • Passes formed stool roughly once daily
  • Jumps to its usual heights without hesitating or gathering itself first
  • Plays, hunts the wand toy, and sleeps in several places through the day
  • Breathes 20–30 times a minute at rest, with no effort and no open mouth

⚠️ Call a vet

  • Straining in the box with little or no urine, especially a male cat — urinary blockage, fatal within 24–48 hours. Go tonight.
  • Open-mouth breathing, panting, or belly-heaving to breathe — never normal in a cat at rest. Emergency.
  • Not eating for 24 hours — cats develop hepatic lipidosis from short fasts. This is urgent, not a wait-and-see.
  • Any suspected lily, antifreeze, paracetamol or rodenticide exposure — go immediately, do not wait for symptoms.
  • A sudden inability or refusal to use the back legs — possible aortic thromboembolism, extremely painful, emergency.
  • Repeated vomiting, or vomiting with lethargy
  • Weight loss over about 5% of body mass, even with a normal appetite
  • Drinking or urinating noticeably more than usual
  • Hiding, or a sudden change in temperament — cats in pain withdraw
  • Bad breath, drooling, or dropping food while eating — dental pain
  • Limping, or no longer jumping to a spot it always used

⚠️ Products to leave on the shelf

Scented or deodorizing litter — the perfume is for you and is a leading cause of box rejection. Plastic liners — claws tear them and urine pools underneath. Plastic food and water bowls — they scratch, harbor bacteria and are associated with chin acne. Declawing, and the "humane" tendon-cutting alternative — both are amputations or permanent disabling of normal function. Vegetarian or vegan cat food — cats cannot make taurine from plants, and deficiency causes heart failure and blindness. Milk marketed for cats — unnecessary calories for a species that is mostly lactose intolerant as an adult. Shock or spray collars for scratching — they suppress the behavior only while you are watching, and reliably make the cat more anxious. Retractable leashes for a harness-trained cat — no control at the moment you need it.

More fun facts to win an argument with

Fun fact

A cat's nose print is as individual as a human fingerprint — the pattern of ridges and bumps on the leather is unique to each cat and stays the same for life. It has been proposed as an identification method, though microchips proved considerably easier to read.

Fun fact

Cats cannot taste sweetness. The gene that codes for one half of the mammalian sweet-taste receptor, Tas1r2, is permanently disabled in all cats from house cats to tigers. It is a side effect of being a strict carnivore for millions of years: nothing in the diet needed detecting as sweet.

Fun fact

The purr is not only contentment. Cats also purr when injured, in labor and while dying, and the dominant frequency sits around 25 to 150 hertz — a range associated in other contexts with bone and tissue healing. The leading theory is that purring began as self-soothing and self-repair, and only later became social.

Fun fact

Adult cats do not meow at each other. Meowing is almost entirely reserved for humans — kittens meow to their mothers and stop as they mature, but house cats keep it up for life, having worked out that it works on us. Feral cats living away from people are notably quieter.

Fun fact

The righting reflex that lets a falling cat rotate feet-first develops at around three to four weeks old and is reliable by about seven weeks. It is also why vets see "high-rise syndrome" — cats survive falls that should kill them, then arrive with the chest and jaw injuries of a feet-first landing. Window screens are not optional above the ground floor.

Frequently asked questions

How many litter boxes do I need for one cat?

Two. The rule is one box per cat plus one spare, so a single cat needs two and two cats need three. The spare covers the times when one box is dirty, when another cat is blocking the way, or when your cat simply develops a preference. Owners resist this more than any other recommendation here, and it prevents more problems than anything else on the list.

How tall should a cat tree be?

At least one perch above 4 ft (1.2 m) per cat, and 5–6 ft is better for an adult. Height matters less than stability and platform depth though — a 5 ft tree with a wide base and 16 in platforms beats a 6 ft tree that wobbles. In a small apartment, a floor-to-ceiling tension-mounted tree gives you the most height per square foot of floor.

Why is my cat peeing outside the litter box?

See a vet first, especially if this is new. Urinary inflammation, crystals, kidney disease, diabetes and arthritis all cause house soiling because the cat associates the box with pain. Once medical causes are ruled out, work through number, size and placement in that order — too few boxes, too small a box, and a box in a cornered or noisy spot cause most of the rest.

Is dry food bad for cats?

Not bad, but insufficient on its own. Cats have a weak thirst drive and evolved getting most of their water from prey, so a dry-only diet leaves them chronically under-hydrated in a way that is linked to kidney and urinary disease over a lifetime. Use wet food as the base of the diet and dry as a topper or in a puzzle feeder.

Should my cat go outdoors?

Indoor cats live 12–18 years; outdoor cats average 2–5. The gap is traffic, disease, fights, poisoning and predation. If you want your cat to have outdoor air, a catio, a secured balcony or harness training gives most of the benefit with almost none of the risk. This is the single largest lifespan decision you will make for your cat.

How do I stop my cat scratching the sofa?

Put a tall, rigid scratching post directly in front of the spot being scratched, and cover that spot temporarily with double-sided tape or a smooth throw. The post needs to be at least 32 in so the cat can stretch fully, and sisal rather than carpet. Remove the deterrent after about a month. Never use a spray bottle — it teaches the cat to scratch when you are out of the room.

Do cats need a companion?

Many do, especially young, active cats left alone all day. Two cats who get along entertain each other in ways a human working full time cannot. The caveat is important though: adopt a bonded pair who already live together, rather than introducing two unrelated adults and hoping. A badly matched pair is worse than a single cat.

What temperature should I keep the house for a cat?

Cats are comfortable in the range most people keep a house at, but their preferred ambient temperature is around 86°F (30°C) — noticeably warmer than ours. That is why they chase sunbeams and sit on radiators. Give them a sunny perch and a warm elevated bed and they will self-regulate. Below about 60°F (15°C) an older or thin-coated cat will need somewhere warm.

How often should I take a cat to the vet?

Once a year for a healthy adult, and twice a year with bloodwork and blood pressure once the cat passes ten. Cats hide illness extremely well, and kidney disease, hyperthyroidism and hypertension are all common in older cats, all treatable, and all silent until they are advanced. The annual visit is screening, not a formality.

What is the first week with a new cat supposed to look like?

Confine the cat to one room with its box, food, water, a hiding place and a perch, and let it come out on its own schedule. Most cats hide for two to five days, and that is normal rather than a bad sign. Sit in the room reading aloud and ignore the cat — approaching it directly slows everything down. Open the rest of the house only once it is eating normally and coming to you.

Crested geckos are sold as the low-maintenance reptile — no heat lamp, no live food, happy at room temperature — and most of that is true. Then people build a bioactive enclosure for one, because a planted vivarium looks incredible and promises to clean itself, and the whole thing turns into a mold farm inside a month. The gecko is not the problem, and neither is bioactive. The problem is that nearly all the advice online was written for dart frogs, where wetter is always better, and a crested gecko is not a frog.

This guide is the setup we would give a friend. The five layers, bottom to top, with the depths that actually matter. The cleanup crew that does the cleaning, and why it starves if you get one thing wrong. Which plants survive a lizard walking on them. The humidity cycle that gives clean, one-piece sheds instead of stuck toes. The temperature ceiling that kills faster than anything else in this guide. And the health signs that mean call a vet today.

Those eyelash-like crests give the species its name. The toe pads gripping the leaf work by molecular attraction, not suction — which is why a crestie can walk up bare glass.

Before you build one: what bioactive actually commits you to

A bioactive enclosure is not a decorated tank. It is a tank with a working decomposer population living in the soil, and that population is a second animal you are keeping. Done right, you never scoop a dropping again and the substrate never needs replacing. Done wrong, you have a sealed box of wet organic matter in your living room.

The build takes an afternoon and then a month of waiting. Plants need to root and the microfauna need to establish before the gecko moves in. If you want a gecko home this weekend, set it up on paper towel and build the bioactive enclosure alongside it.

Buy captive-bred. Crested geckos are Vulnerable in the wild, protected in New Caledonia, and cannot legally be exported — so every healthy crestie in the trade is captive-bred, and any seller vague about origin is worth walking away from. Ask when it last ate, what it eats, and whether it has ever dropped its tail.

Enclosure size: height is the number that matters

Crested geckos are arboreal. They live in the top third of the space and barely touch the floor, so floor area matters far less than vertical climbing room — the opposite of almost every other beginner reptile. The old advice of a small tank for a hatchling is fine but wasteful: a big enclosure only makes a gecko insecure if it is empty. Clutter it heavily and a hatchling will cross it happily.

The test that beats every number above: your gecko should be able to get from the floor to the ceiling without a long jump, sleep somewhere it feels covered at three different heights, and find both a damp corner and a dry one. If you can see the whole animal from across the room at all times, the enclosure needs more clutter, not more glass.

Three rules that decide the enclosure

Get these right and everything else is easy.

Cross-ventilation, not just a mesh lid

A bioactive enclosure has to dry out between mistings, and a single vent in the roof will not do it. Look for ventilation along the top and the front. This one detail is the difference between a vivarium that cycles properly and one that grows mold on the glass.

Front-opening doors, and a deep base

A hand coming down from above looks like a predator to a nocturnal animal, and reaching in over the top knocks plants about. Front doors solve both.

Check the base depth before you buy: you need room for 1.5–2 in of drainage plus 3–4 in of substrate without burying the doors.

Where the enclosure goes

Keep it cool. This is the whole ballgame for a crested gecko. Away from radiators, south-facing windows and anywhere that catches afternoon sun — a glass tank in a sunbeam can pass 85°F in under an hour. A cooler interior room is better than a warm bright one.

Keep it steady. Not next to a door that opens onto cold air, and not above a heat source. Bioactive setups forgive slow changes and punish fast ones.

Keep it quiet by day. Cresties sleep through daylight and come out at dusk. A home office or spare room usually works better than a hallway.

Temperature: the one mistake with no second chance

If you take one number from this guide, take 85°F. Crested geckos come from cool, shaded forest in New Caledonia, and they are among the very few reptiles a normal room already suits. Most homes need no heating equipment at all.

This matters more in a bioactive build than a bare one, because bioactive tempts you into adding light. Plants want light, light makes heat, and heat kills crested geckos. Every time you change a bulb, a fixture or its height, check the temperature an hour later at the height the gecko actually sleeps — not at the floor.

TemperatureStatusWhat happens

⚠️ No basking lamp, no heat rock, no colored night bulb

There is no scenario where a crested gecko needs a basking lamp. Heat rocks cause contact burns and have no safe version. Red and blue "night" bulbs disturb sleep in a nocturnal animal and add heat you do not want. A low-wattage heat mat on the side or back of the enclosure, on a thermostat, is occasionally used in genuinely cold rooms — never as a default and never under the substrate, where it will cook the soil and your cleanup crew. If your room reliably sits above 80°F in summer, the answer is a cooler room or air conditioning, not equipment inside the tank.

Humidity: a cycle, not a setting

This is the single biggest difference between a bioactive vivarium that thrives and one that goes fuzzy. Crested geckos want humidity that rises and falls daily: a heavy misting in the evening taking it to 60–80%, then a drop back to 50–60% before you mist again.

Held permanently at 80% you get mold, fungus gnats and a nocturnal lizard in constantly damp air. And counterintuitively, chronic damp causes stuck shed rather than preventing it, because the animal never gets the drying phase that helps old skin release. The drying phase is not a gap in your care. It is part of it.

💚 The wrung-out sponge test

Push a finger two inches into the substrate and squeeze a pinch. Damp all the way through with no water running out is correct. If water beads or drips, stop misting for two or three days and increase airflow. If it is dust-dry at depth, your cleanup crew will die out long before you notice anything wrong with the gecko. If the glass never clears and the surface stays dark between mistings, the problem is almost always ventilation rather than water — and the fix is airflow, not less misting.

Lighting and UVB

Crested geckos can be kept without UVB, and for years they were, because a complete powdered diet already contains vitamin D3. But low-level UVB lets the animal make its own, supports natural behavior, and is increasingly the standard of care. We would fit it.

Crested geckos sit in Ferguson Zone 1 — a UV index of roughly 0.5–1.0, the dappled shade of a forest understory. Use a low-output 2–7% T5 tube (a ShadeDweller-grade fixture, for example), never a desert 10–12% bulb, mounted over a shaded branch with plenty of cover so the gecko can choose. Run it 10–14 hours on a timer and replace the tube every 12 months, writing the install date on the glass.

Plants need their own light. A low-wattage LED bar on the same timer keeps pothos and bromeliads growing without adding meaningful heat — which is the point, because a hot bulb over a crestie is dangerous.

Setting up the enclosure, layer by layer

Bottom to top. Build the whole thing, seed the cleanup crew, and leave it a month before the gecko moves in. Total depth at the bottom of the tank comes to roughly six inches before you add hardscape.

  1. Drainage — 1.5–2 inches. Expanded clay aggregate (LECA). Water drains through the soil, collects in the gaps between the clay balls and evaporates back up, which holds humidity steady while the soil above stays merely damp. Inert, reusable and light enough to lift.
  2. Mesh barrier. Fine synthetic mesh or plain window screen, cut to the footprint and laid straight on the clay. Its only job is stopping soil washing down into the reservoir. Skip it and the drainage layer silts up within a year.
  3. Substrate — 3–4 inches of ABG mix. Developed at the Atlanta Botanical Garden for orchids, and it moved into the vivarium hobby because it drains hard, holds humidity for weeks and refuses to compact. Mixing your own is cheaper by volume: 2 parts tree fern, 2 parts orchid bark, 1 part each sphagnum, peat and horticultural charcoal — measured by volume, not weight.
  4. Leaf litter — 1–2 inches. Magnolia and sea grape. This is the cleanup crew's food and cover, not decoration, and it is the difference between isopods that breed and isopods that quietly die out. Both break down slowly, over months rather than weeks.
  5. Hardscape and plants. Cork bark, branches and plants, wedged or siliconed so nothing can shift — a branch that moves under a gecko is how tails get dropped. Build the climbing structure across the upper two thirds, because that is where the animal lives.
  6. Seed the crew, then wait four weeks. Tip in the springtails and isopods, plant up, mist, and leave it alone with no gecko. You are growing a small ecosystem and it needs a head start on the animal that will be feeding it.

⚠️ Substrates to keep out of a crested gecko enclosure

Calci-sand and crushed walnut shell — sold as reptile substrate, cause fatal impaction, and useless in a bioactive build anyway.

Coco fiber on its own — compacts into an airless mat that rots plant roots and suffocates your cleanup crew.

Anything with fertilizer, perlite or wetting agents — including most bagged houseplant compost. Read the label.

A heat mat underneath the substrate — it dries the soil from below and kills the microfauna.

Dense planting is not just decoration. Cover at three different heights is what turns a nervous new gecko into one you actually see at dusk.

The cleanup crew: two invertebrates do all the work

Add them four weeks before the gecko and you will never scoop a dropping. They are a one-time purchase, because they breed.

Springtails — the mold police

Microscopic, harmless, and the reason a properly seeded vivarium does not go fuzzy. They eat mold and fungal growth before you ever see it, plus the fine organic particles isopods miss. Tip the whole culture in, charcoal and all, and leave it alone — expect a boom, a crash, then a steady population.

If you buy only one half of the crew, buy this half.

Isopods — the waste processors

The heavier shift: droppings, shed skin, dead leaves and old food. Dwarf whites are the standard pick for cresties — small, substrate-dwelling and no bother to the gecko. Powder blues and dairy cows work too and are easier to spot.

Start with 20–30 in a standard enclosure and give them four weeks with food and no predator.

💚 Feed the crew, not just the gecko

The most common bioactive failure after over-watering is a cleanup crew that starves. One gecko does not produce enough waste to sustain a large isopod colony. Keep the leaf litter topped up, and drop in a pinch of fish flake, a slice of cucumber or a piece of soft rotting wood every couple of weeks. A crew that is breeding is a crew that is working — and if your isopod numbers are visibly falling, the cause is almost always too little leaf litter or substrate that has dried out, not the gecko eating them.

Plants that survive a lizard walking on them

Plant choice is where most first bioactive builds go wrong. The enclosure is humid, dimly lit, and periodically walked over by something the weight of a large plum. Delicate species do not last.

💚 Reliable

  • Pothos — near-unkillable, climbs, tolerates low light. The default for a reason.
  • Bromeliads — hold water in the center and take gecko traffic well
  • Syngonium and philodendron — vigorous climbers, forgiving of humidity
  • Dracaena and schefflera — sturdy enough to be sat on
  • Begonias and ferns — ground cover for the damper corners
  • Sphagnum and cushion moss — surface interest, holds moisture

⚠️ Leave out

  • Succulents and cacti — they want the opposite climate and will rot
  • Anything spiny or sharp-edged — a falling gecko lands on it
  • Unwashed garden-center plants — pesticide residue on the leaves and through the root ball. Rinse thoroughly and repot into your own substrate first.
  • Fertilizer of any kind — the gecko's waste feeds the plants. That is the entire point.

💚 The pothos question, answered honestly

Pothos is the most recommended vivarium plant there is, and it is also listed as mildly toxic if eaten in quantity. Both are true. Crested geckos are frugivores and insectivores that do not graze on foliage, so in practice it is used in thousands of enclosures without incident. The sensible position: use it, and if you ever catch your gecko actually chewing leaves rather than walking on them, swap it for a bromeliad. Do not lose sleep over it.

Diet: one tub does nearly everything

Crested geckos are among the easiest reptiles to feed correctly, because the work has been done for you. A complete powdered diet — Pangea, Repashy or Clark's — carries balanced calcium, D3 and vitamins, and is the reason metabolic bone disease in cresties went from common to avoidable.

AgePowdered dietInsects
Hatchling (0–3 months)Fresh every other dayOptional, tiny, dusted
Juvenile (3–12 months)Every other dayOnce weekly, dusted
Adult (12 months+)3× weeklyOnce or twice a fortnight, dusted
  1. Mix to a thin ketchup consistency. Too thick and it skins over; too thin and it runs into the substrate.
  2. Offer it raised. A shallow ledge cup mounted up off the floor, where the animal actually lives. A dish on the substrate often goes untouched.
  3. Remove it after 24–36 hours. The isopods will deal with spills, but a cup of fermenting diet is still a mold source.
  4. Alternate two flavors. Fussy cresties are common, and a refused flavor is not a refused meal.
  5. Insects are enrichment, not the staple. Nothing wider than the space between the gecko's eyes, dusted with plain calcium, in a smooth-sided dish.

⚠️ Never feed these

Baby food, fruit puree or mashed banana as a staple — the old advice, and it causes metabolic bone disease. Sugar with no calcium behind it is not a diet.

Avocado, rhubarb, citrus, onion — toxic or far too acidic.

Wild-caught insects — pesticide residue and parasites.

Mealworms as a regular feeder — mostly indigestible shell, little nutrition.

Extra D3 on top of a complete diet — it is fat-soluble and accumulates. Dust insects with plain calcium, not a D3 blend, unless a vet says otherwise.

What every reptile vet wishes owners checked first

Handling: your first two weeks

Crested geckos tolerate handling well once settled, but they are fast, they jump, and a dropped tail is permanent. Patience in the first fortnight gives you a calmer animal for the next fifteen years.

  1. Days 1–7 — hands off. Mist, change water, check temperatures, and otherwise leave it completely alone. It needs to learn the enclosure is safe. Expect to barely see it.
  2. Days 7–10 — first meal. Fresh diet in the ledge cup every other day. A newly-homed crestie commonly refuses for a week or two; that is normal, and weight is the thing to watch rather than appetite.
  3. Days 10–12 — hand in the enclosure. Rest a flat hand on a branch in the evening and let the gecko walk onto it in its own time. Do not grab, and never grab the tail.
  4. Days 12–14 — short sessions, low down. Five minutes, sitting on the floor, hand-over-hand as it walks. They are jumpers, so stay low over a soft surface.
  5. Within the first month — vet check. A new-animal exam with a fecal test, especially for a gecko from a store or an expo.

Never handle a gecko that is in shed or has just eaten, and never restrain one that is trying to leave. Tail drop is a stress response — the animal chooses it. Give it the option not to.

Shedding: what normal looks like

Crested geckos shed every few weeks as juveniles and less often as adults. The skin goes dull and grayish beforehand, the gecko often refuses food, and then it comes off in pieces over a few hours — cresties eat most of it, so you may never see the shed at all.

What you must check is the extremities. Stuck shed forms a tight ring on toes and the tail tip, cuts off circulation, and the digit goes necrotic and drops off. Look at the feet after every shed. If skin is stuck, raise humidity toward the top of the range, give the gecko a damp moss hide, and let it work the skin off itself — a short session in a ventilated tub with damp paper towel helps. Never pull at it.

Health: what to watch, and what is an emergency

Cresties hide illness well, and most of what goes wrong is husbandry wearing a medical name. Find an exotic-animal vet who treats geckos before you need one, and quarantine any new arrival for 30 days away from other reptiles.

💚 A healthy crested gecko

  • Clear eyes, licked clean — they have no eyelids
  • Straight limbs and a firm, square-set jaw
  • Rounded tail base, not a sharp hip line
  • Grips firmly and moves confidently at dusk
  • Sheds cleanly, with nothing left on the toes
  • Steady or rising weight, week to week
  • Firm, well-formed droppings

⚠️ Call an exotic vet

  • Gaping or lethargy in a warm room — heat stress, the fastest killer here
  • Soft or lumpy lower jaw, bowed limbs, tremors, a tail that will not lift — metabolic bone disease
  • A tight ring of stuck shed on a toe or the tail tip — act within days, not weeks
  • Refusing food for more than a week with weight loss
  • Weight loss of more than about 10%
  • Runny droppings, or weight loss despite eating — parasites
  • Swelling, discharge, or a wound that is not closing

⚠️ A dropped tail never grows back

Unlike leopard geckos, crested geckos do not regenerate a dropped tail. What you get is a small rounded stump, and it is permanent. It is cosmetic rather than a health crisis — adults in the wild very commonly have no tail — but it is irreversible, and it is the single best reason to handle briefly, low down, and never by the tail. Floppy tail syndrome, where the tail hangs over the hips, is a separate problem linked to calcium deficiency and a lack of horizontal resting places.

When it goes wrong: mold and fungus gnats

Both are symptoms of the same thing — too much water, too little air — and both are fixable without tearing the tank down.

White fuzz on the substrate or wood

Common in the first few weeks and usually harmless: it is normally saprophytic fungus on new wood, and a healthy springtail population eats it.

Fix: mist less, increase ventilation, and check you actually seeded springtails. Remove thick patches by hand and break up dense clumps of sphagnum, which are reliable mold magnets.

Small flies rising from the soil

Fungus gnats. Their larvae live in permanently damp organic media, so their presence is a moisture diagnosis in itself.

Fix: let the surface dry properly between mistings and put a few small yellow sticky traps near the top vents. Never use an insecticide — it will kill your cleanup crew and can harm the gecko.

More fun facts to win an argument with

Fun fact

Crested geckos were thought extinct for most of the twentieth century. Described in 1866, then not reliably seen again until a small population turned up in New Caledonia after a tropical storm in 1994. Nearly every crested gecko in the pet trade descends from that rediscovery.

Fun fact

They are still listed as Vulnerable in the wild and are protected in New Caledonia, where export is prohibited. That is why the hobby is entirely captive-bred — and why a seller who cannot tell you where an animal came from is worth avoiding.

Fun fact

Those toe pads work by van der Waals forces — molecular attraction between the setae on the foot and the surface. Not suction, not glue. It is why a crestie can hang from bare glass, and why a dusty surface defeats it completely.

Fun fact

A crested gecko has no eyelids. It keeps its eyes clean with a long tongue, the same way a gecko drinks droplets off leaves after a misting — which is why most of their water intake never touches the water dish.

Fun fact

Wild adults very often have no tail at all. Dropping it is a deliberate escape response, and unlike leopard geckos they never regrow it. A tailless crestie is not a damaged animal — in the wild it is close to the norm.

Frequently asked questions

Is a bioactive enclosure harder than a normal one?

Harder to build, easier to run. The setup takes an afternoon plus four weeks of waiting for plants and microfauna to establish. After that you stop spot-cleaning droppings entirely, the substrate never needs replacing, and humidity self-regulates far better than it does on paper towel.

Do crested geckos need a heat lamp?

Almost never. They want 72–78°F, which is room temperature in most homes, and they suffer heat stress above 82°F and can die above 85°F. In a genuinely cold room, a low-wattage mat on the side or back of the enclosure on a thermostat is the answer — never a basking lamp, never a heat rock, and never a mat under the substrate.

Do crested geckos need UVB?

Not strictly, because a complete powdered diet contains D3 — but it is increasingly recommended and we would fit it. They are Ferguson Zone 1, so you want a low-output 2–7% T5 tube over a shaded branch on a 10–14 hour timer. Never a desert-grade 10–12% bulb. Replace the tube every 12 months.

What substrate should I use in a bioactive crested gecko enclosure?

Three to four inches of ABG mix over 1.5–2 inches of expanded clay, with mesh between them. Coco fiber on its own compacts into an airless mat that rots roots and suffocates the cleanup crew. To mix your own by volume: 2 parts tree fern, 2 parts orchid bark, 1 part each sphagnum, peat and horticultural charcoal.

Is loose substrate an impaction risk?

Much less than with a desert species. Crested geckos feed up in the branches rather than striking at prey on the ground, and ABG mix is coarse and fibrous rather than fine and sandy. Feed from a raised ledge cup and keep insects in a smooth-sided dish. Calci-sand and crushed walnut shell are a genuine hazard and should never be used with any species.

How big should the enclosure be?

18 × 18 × 24 inches is the accepted minimum for an adult, and taller is better because they are arboreal and live in the top third of the space. You can raise a hatchling in a full-size enclosure from day one as long as you clutter it heavily with branches and foliage.

Why won't my crested gecko eat the powdered diet?

Check the temperature first, then how long it has been settled — a fortnight of refusal after a move is normal. Then try a different flavor, because fussiness is extremely common. Make sure the cup is raised up where the animal actually lives and that the food is fresh; a day-old cup has usually been written off.

Will my gecko eat the cleanup crew?

Occasionally, and it does no harm — an isopod is a snack, not a meal, and a healthy colony breeds far faster than one gecko can eat it. If your crew is visibly dwindling, the cause is almost always too little leaf litter or substrate that has dried out.

Can two crested geckos live together?

No. They are solitary, and cohabiting causes injuries you may not see coming — nipped toes and tail tips, and a subordinate animal that quietly stops feeding. Two males will fight. A pair will breed continuously, which is hard on the female. Two females can sometimes share a much larger enclosure, but it needs close watching and a spare tank ready.

Does a dropped tail grow back?

No. Unlike leopard geckos, crested geckos never regrow a dropped tail — you get a small rounded stump and it is permanent. It is cosmetic rather than a health problem, and wild adults very commonly have no tail, but it is the best reason to keep handling brief, low down, and never by the tail.

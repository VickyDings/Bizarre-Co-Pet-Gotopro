A bioactive crested gecko enclosure is the easiest reptile habitat to keep alive — and the easiest one to drown.

Nearly every bioactive build that fails does it in one of three ways: substrate kept wet instead of damp, no airflow to dry it between mistings, or a light that quietly heats the tank past the point a crested gecko can survive.

The trouble is that most bioactive advice online came out of dart frog keeping, where wetter really is better. Crested geckos are not frogs. They need humidity that cycles — up after an evening misting, back down to the fifties before the next one — and they die at temperatures a tropical frog would find pleasant. This guide builds the enclosure layer by layer, gives you the numbers to check it against, and corrects the three pieces of advice that cause the most harm.

🌡️ Temperature: The Thing That Actually Kills Them

If you remember one number from this guide, make it 85°F. Crested geckos come from the cool, shaded forests of New Caledonia, and they are one of the very few reptiles that a normal room already suits. Most homes need no heating equipment at all.

This matters more in a bioactive build than a bare one, because bioactive tempts you into adding lights. Plants want light; light makes heat; heat kills crested geckos. Every time you change a bulb, a fixture or its height, check the temperature an hour later at the height the gecko actually sleeps.

⚠️ No heat mat, no basking lamp, no coloured night bulb

A heat mat on the side or back of the enclosure is occasionally used in genuinely cold rooms, on a thermostat, and never as a default. Basking lamps belong on desert species. Red and blue "night" bulbs disturb sleep in a nocturnal animal and add heat you do not want. If your room reliably sits above 80°F in summer, the answer is air conditioning or a cooler room — not equipment inside the tank.

💧 Humidity Is a Cycle, Not a Setting

This is the single biggest difference between a bioactive vivarium that thrives and one that goes mouldy. Crested geckos want humidity that rises and falls daily: a heavy misting in the evening taking it to 60–80%, then a drop back to 50–60% before you mist again.

A tank held permanently at 80% grows mould, breeds fungus gnats and puts a nocturnal lizard in permanently damp air. The drying phase is not a gap in your care — it is part of it.

The wrung-out sponge test

The one check that tells you whether your substrate moisture is right.

Push a finger two inches into the substrate and squeeze a pinch of it.

  • Damp throughout, no water running out — correct. This is what "like a wrung-out sponge" means.
  • Water beads or drips — too wet. Stop misting for two or three days and increase airflow.
  • Dust-dry at depth — too dry. Your cleanup crew will die out first, before you notice anything wrong with the gecko.

If the glass never clears and the surface stays dark between mistings, the problem is almost always ventilation rather than water. Cross-flow — vents along the top and the front — is what lets a planted tank dry.

Damp through, nothing dripping — the target.

🏗️ Building It, Layer by Layer

Bottom to top. Do the whole build, seed the cleanup crew, and leave it a month before the gecko moves in — the plants need to root and the microfauna need to establish.

The five layers

Total depth at the bottom of the tank: roughly 6 inches before you add hardscape.

1. Drainage — 1.5–2 in

Expanded clay aggregate (LECA). Water drains into it and evaporates back up, which keeps humidity steady while the soil above stays merely damp. Inert and reusable.

2. Mesh barrier

Fine synthetic mesh or plain window screen cut to the footprint, laid straight on the clay. Its only job is stopping soil washing down into the reservoir. Skip it and the drainage layer silts up within a year.

3. Substrate — 3–4 in

ABG mix. Drains hard, holds humidity for weeks and resists compaction, because the bark and tree fern hold air pockets open. Compacted airless soil is how roots rot and isopods suffocate.

4. Leaf litter — 1–2 in

Magnolia and sea grape. This is the cleanup crew's food and cover, not decoration. Without a real layer of it, isopods quietly die out and you are back to scooping droppings.

5. Hardscape and plants

Cork bark, branches and plants, wedged or siliconed so nothing can shift. Build the climbing structure across the upper two thirds — that is where the animal actually lives.

Then wait

Seed springtails and isopods, plant up, mist, and give it four weeks with no gecko. You are growing a small ecosystem, and it needs a head start on the animal that will be feeding it.

🌿 Plants That Actually Survive a Gecko

Plant choice is where most first bioactive builds go wrong. The enclosure is humid, dimly lit and periodically walked over by something the weight of a large plum. Delicate species do not last.

✅ Reliable

  • Pothos — near-unkillable, climbs, tolerates low light. The default for a reason. (See the caveat below.)
  • Bromeliads — hold water in the centre, take gecko traffic well, and look the part
  • Syngonium and Philodendron — vigorous climbers, forgiving of humidity
  • Dracaena and Schefflera — sturdy enough to be sat on
  • Begonia and ferns — good ground cover in the damper corners
  • Sphagnum and cushion moss — surface interest, holds moisture

🚫 Leave out

  • Succulents and cacti — they want the opposite climate and will rot
  • Anything spiny or sharp-edged — a falling gecko lands on it
  • Plants from a garden centre, unwashed — pesticide residue on the leaves and in the root ball. Rinse thoroughly and repot into your own substrate before it goes in.
  • Fertiliser of any kind in the enclosure. The gecko's own waste feeds the plants; that is the point of bioactive.

💚 The pothos question, answered honestly

Pothos is the most recommended vivarium plant there is, and it is also listed as mildly toxic if eaten in quantity. Both are true. Crested geckos are frugivores and insectivores that do not graze on foliage, so in practice it is used in thousands of enclosures without incident. The sensible position: use it, and if you ever catch your gecko actually chewing leaves rather than walking on them, swap it for a bromeliad. Do not lose sleep over it.

🦠 The Cleanup Crew Is the Whole Point

A bioactive enclosure is not a decorated tank — it is a tank with a functioning decomposer population. Two invertebrates do the work, and between them they mean you never spot-clean droppings again.

Springtails — the mould police

Microscopic, harmless, and the reason a properly seeded vivarium does not go fuzzy. They eat mould, fungal growth and the fine organic particles isopods miss. Tip the entire culture in, charcoal and all. Expect a population boom, a crash, then a steady state.

If you buy only one half of the crew, buy this half.

Isopods — the waste processors

Droppings, shed skin, dead leaves, old food. Dwarf whites are the standard choice for cresties: small, substrate-dwelling and no bother to the gecko. Powder blues and dairy cows also work and are easier to spot.

Start with 20–30 in a standard enclosure and give them four weeks before the gecko arrives.

💚 Feed the crew, not just the gecko

The commonest bioactive failure after over-watering is a cleanup crew that starves. A single gecko simply does not produce enough waste to sustain a large isopod colony. Keep the leaf litter topped up, and drop in a pinch of fish flake, a slice of cucumber or a piece of soft wood every couple of weeks. A crew that is breeding is a crew that is working.

🍑 Diet: One Tub Does Nearly Everything

Crested geckos are among the easiest reptiles to feed correctly, because the hard work has been done for you. A complete powdered diet — Pangea, Repashy or Clark's — contains balanced calcium, D3 and vitamins, and is the reason metabolic bone disease in cresties went from common to avoidable.

  • Mix to a thin ketchup consistency and offer in a shallow ledge cup, up off the floor where the animal actually lives.
  • Remove it after 24–36 hours. In a bioactive tank the isopods will deal with spills, but a cup of fermenting diet is still a mould source.
  • Buy two flavours and alternate. Fussy cresties are common, and a refused flavour is not a refused meal.
  • Insects are enrichment, not the staple. Nothing wider than the space between the gecko's eyes.
  • Fresh water in a shallow dish, changed daily, even though most of their drinking is done off misted leaves.

⚠️ Never feed these

Baby food, fruit purée or mashed banana as a staple — the old advice, and it causes metabolic bone disease. Sugar with no calcium behind it is not a diet.

Avocado, rhubarb, citrus, onion — toxic or far too acidic.

Wild-caught insects — pesticide residue and parasites.

Mealworms as a regular feeder — mostly indigestible shell, little nutrition.

Anything requiring live prey larger than the gap between the eyes — a choking and impaction risk.

🔍 Three Myths Worth Correcting

These three turn up in almost every beginner thread, and each one causes real harm.

💚 Myth 1: "Bioactive means you never clean it"

Bioactive means you stop scooping. It does not mean hands-off. You still wipe the glass, remove uneaten food, top up leaf litter, prune the plants, check the drainage layer is not silted or flooded, and watch the crew's numbers. What you gain is a tank that processes waste continuously instead of accumulating it between deep cleans — and a gecko living on living soil rather than kitchen roll. That is a genuinely better home, not a shortcut.

💚 Myth 2: "Higher humidity is always better"

This is the dart-frog assumption, and it is the most common way a bioactive crestie build goes wrong. Crested geckos need a daily cycle — up to 60–80% after an evening misting, back down to 50–60% before the next. Held permanently high, you get mould, fungus gnats and a nocturnal lizard in constantly damp air. Paradoxically, chronically wet conditions also cause stuck shed, because the animal never gets the drying phase that helps the old skin release. The fix is almost never more water; it is more airflow.

💚 Myth 3: "Two geckos will keep each other company"

They will not. Crested geckos are solitary, and cohabiting them causes injuries you may not see coming — nipped toes and tail tips, and a subordinate animal that quietly stops feeding. Two males will fight outright. A male and female will breed continuously, which is hard on the female. Two females can sometimes share a much larger enclosure (24 × 18 × 36 in and up) but it needs close watching and a spare tank ready. One gecko per enclosure is the answer that never goes wrong.

🩺 Reading Your Gecko

Cresties hide illness well, and most of what goes wrong is husbandry wearing a medical name.

  • Soft or lumpy lower jaw, bowed limbs, tremors, a tail that will not lift — metabolic bone disease. Vet urgently, then fix the diet. Floppy tail syndrome is linked to the same calcium deficiency, and to a lack of horizontal resting places.
  • Stuck shed as a tight ring on toes or tail tip — act fast. Constricted digits go necrotic and drop off. Usually a humidity-cycle problem, in either direction.
  • Gaping away from any warm spot, or lethargy in a warm room — check the thermometer first. Heat stress is the likeliest answer and the fastest to kill.
  • Refusing food for more than a week — temperature, then stress, then illness, in that order. A newly-homed gecko commonly refuses for a fortnight while it settles.
  • Weight loss — weigh weekly on a kitchen scale and write it down. It shows up in the numbers long before you can see it.
  • A dropped tail — crested geckos drop their tails readily and, unlike leopard geckos, never grow them back. It is cosmetic, not a health crisis, but it is permanent, and it is the main reason handling should be brief and low over a soft surface.

⚠️ Find an exotics vet before you need one

Few general practices treat reptiles. Search for an exotic-animal vet, call ahead and confirm they see geckos, and save the number somewhere you can find it at 9pm on a Sunday. Quarantine any new arrival for 30 days away from other reptiles, and take a fresh faecal sample in during the first month.

🐛 When It Goes Wrong: Mould and Gnats

Both are symptoms of the same thing — too much water, too little air — and both are fixable without tearing the tank down.

White fuzz on the substrate or wood

Common in the first few weeks and usually harmless — it is normally saprophytic fungus on new wood, and a healthy springtail population eats it.

Fix: mist less, increase ventilation, and check you actually seeded springtails. Remove any thick patch by hand. Break up dense clumps of sphagnum, which are reliable mould magnets.

Fungus gnats

Small flies drifting up from the soil. Their larvae live in permanently damp organic media, so their presence is a moisture diagnosis in itself.

Fix: let the surface dry properly between mistings and put a few small yellow sticky traps near the top vents — the adults are drawn to that yellow. Do not use any insecticide: it will kill your cleanup crew and can harm the gecko.

⚠️ Products to leave on the shelf

Basking lamps and heat rocks — there is no scenario where a crested gecko tank needs either.

Desert-grade 10–12% UVB — far too strong for a Zone 1 species.

Coloured "night-time" bulbs — they disturb sleep and add heat.

🎉 Fun Fact

Crested geckos were believed extinct for most of the twentieth century. Described in 1866, then not reliably seen again until a small population turned up after a tropical storm in New Caledonia in 1994. Nearly every crested gecko in the pet trade descends from that rediscovery — and they are still classed as Vulnerable in the wild, where they remain protected and cannot legally be exported. They also have no eyelids, so they clean their eyes with their tongues, and the toe pads that let them walk up glass work by van der Waals forces — molecular attraction, not suction or glue.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a bioactive enclosure harder than a normal one?

Harder to build, easier to run. The setup takes an afternoon and a month of waiting for the plants and microfauna to establish. After that you stop spot-cleaning droppings entirely, the substrate never needs replacing, and humidity self-regulates far better than it does on paper towel. The ongoing work is misting, feeding, wiping glass and the occasional prune.

Do crested geckos need a heat lamp?

Almost never. They want 72–78°F, which is normal room temperature in most homes, and they suffer heat stress above 82°F and can die above 85°F. If your room is genuinely cold, a heat mat on the side or back of the enclosure on a thermostat is the usual answer — never a basking lamp, and never a heat rock.

Do they need UVB?

Not strictly, if you feed a complete powdered diet containing D3 — but it is increasingly recommended, and we would fit it. Crested geckos are Ferguson Zone 1, meaning a UV index of roughly 0.5–1.0, so you want a low-output 2–7% linear tube over a shaded branch, on a 10–14 hour timer. Never a desert-grade 10–12% bulb. Replace the tube every 12 months.

Can I use just coco fibre instead of ABG mix?

For a non-bioactive tank, yes. For a bioactive one, no — coco fibre compacts into an airless mat, which rots plant roots and suffocates your cleanup crew. ABG mix stays open because of the bark and tree fern in it. If you want to save money, mix your own by volume: 2 parts tree fern, 2 parts orchid bark, 1 part each sphagnum, peat and horticultural charcoal.

Is loose substrate an impaction risk?

Much less than with a desert species, because crested geckos hunt and feed up in the branches rather than striking at prey on the ground, and ABG mix is coarse and fibrous rather than fine and sandy. Feed from a raised ledge cup, keep insects in a smooth-sided dish, and the risk is small. Calci-sand and crushed walnut shell are a genuine impaction hazard and should never be used with any species.

How big does the enclosure need to be?

18 × 18 × 24 in is the accepted minimum for an adult, and taller is better — they are arboreal and live in the top third of the space. You can raise a hatchling in a full-size adult enclosure from day one if you clutter it heavily with branches and foliage so it always feels covered, which saves buying twice.

Why won't my gecko eat the powdered diet?

Check the temperature first, then how long it has been settled in — a fortnight of refusal after a move is normal. After that, try a different flavour: fussiness is extremely common and a refused flavour is not a refused meal. Make sure the cup is raised up where the animal actually lives, and that it is fresh; a day-old cup has usually been written off.

Will my gecko eat the cleanup crew?

Occasionally, and it does no harm — an isopod is a snack, not a meal. A healthy colony reproduces far faster than a single gecko can eat it. If your crew is visibly dwindling, the cause is almost always too little leaf litter or substrate that has dried out, not predation.

Does a dropped tail grow back?

No. Unlike leopard geckos, crested geckos never regrow a dropped tail — what you get is a small rounded stump, and it is permanent. It is cosmetic rather than a health problem, and adults in the wild very commonly have no tail at all. It is still the best reason to keep handling brief, low down, and over a soft surface.

How long do they live?

Fifteen to twenty years with good care, which is longer than most people expect from a small lizard. Treat it as a long commitment rather than a starter pet.

The Bottom Line

A bioactive crested gecko enclosure comes down to five numbers: an 18 × 18 × 24 in enclosure or bigger, 72–78°F with a hard ceiling at 85°F, humidity cycling between 80% and 50% rather than sitting still, 3–4 inches of ABG mix over a 1.5–2 inch drainage layer, and a complete powdered diet three times a week.

Get those right and the enclosure largely runs itself for years — which is the real reward for the afternoon of building and the month of waiting.

Then do the thing most keepers skip: buy the digital thermometer and put the probe where the gecko sleeps. It costs less than a bag of substrate, and heat is the one mistake in crested gecko keeping that does not give you a second chance.

Pet costumes are sold the way novelty gifts are sold: by the picture on the bag. A pug in a hot dog bun, a cat glaring out of a tiny witch hat, and a size chart that says "Medium — fits most dogs 20–40 lbs". Then it arrives, the chest strap will not close on a barrel-chested beagle, the hood sits over one eye, and the dog spends the evening frozen in the hallway doing the slow-motion walk every owner recognizes. The photo gets taken anyway. The costume goes in a drawer.

This guide is the setup we would give a friend who wants the photo and does not want their dog miserable to get it. We will cover how to measure a pet properly — neck, chest girth and back length, never breed or weight — why the chest measurement is the one that decides everything, the two-week plan that turns a costume from an ambush into a non-event, the stress signals that owners read as "he's being good", why cats are a different proposition entirely, and what to do when the answer is a bandana instead. We will finish on Halloween night itself, because the costume is the small risk. The chocolate, the open front door and the glow sticks are the ones that fill emergency clinics on November 1.

A costume a pet has worn three times before the night is a costume it ignores. One pulled out of the bag an hour before guests arrive is something that happened to it.

Before you buy: does your pet actually want this

Start with an honest question, because the answer decides whether you buy a costume at all. Some animals genuinely do not mind clothes. Dogs that already wear a harness, a coat in winter or a cooling vest have learned that things going over their head are survivable, and a costume is a variation on a known theme. Dogs that have never worn anything, cats in general, and any animal that has had a bad experience with handling are a different case.

The honest test is simple. Drape a bath towel over your pet's back and let go. A pet that shakes it off and carries on is telling you something workable. A pet that freezes, drops into a crouch, or walks out of the room is telling you something too, and it is worth listening to the first time rather than the fourth.

Freezing is the signal owners get wrong most often. An animal that goes completely still in a costume is almost never relaxed — stillness is what prey animals do when they cannot work out how to escape. Owners read it as "she's being so good about it". A behaviorist reads it as shutdown. If your pet stops moving when the costume goes on, the costume comes off.

None of this means no costume. It means the bandana version is the right version for that animal, and there is no prize for the full outfit.

Sizing: the chest is the only measurement that matters

Costume size charts are written around weight because weight is the number owners know. It is also the number that tells you least. A 25 lb beagle and a 25 lb whippet are the same on the scale and nowhere near the same around the ribs, and the beagle is the one who cannot do up the strap. Measure the animal, with a soft tape, and buy to the chest girth.

Three measurements, taken with the pet standing:

  1. Chest girth — the one that decides it. Around the deepest part of the chest, just behind the front legs. This is where costumes fail. If the listing gives one measurement only, it should be this.
  2. Back length. From the base of the neck, where a collar sits, to the base of the tail. Not to the tip. A costume cut too long bunches over the hips and a dog will try to sit it off.
  3. Neck. Around where the collar sits, with two fingers of slack included in the number. Anything that closes at the throat needs this right.

Then size up when you are between sizes, every time. A loose costume is a photo with a slightly baggy costume in it. A tight one restricts breathing, rubs under the forelegs, and teaches the animal that costumes hurt.

Three rules that decide the costume

Get these right and everything else is a detail.

Nothing near the eyes, ears, nose or mouth

Hoods that slide, masks, and anything over the muzzle. A dog that cannot see properly cannot read another dog, and a dog that cannot read another dog gets into trouble on a busy street on the one night everyone is out.

Ears matter more than people think: covering them removes most of a dog's early warning and a lot of its ability to signal.

Full range of movement, including going to the toilet

Walk, sit, lie down, turn around, squat. Run through all five before you commit to an evening. The one owners forget is the last, and a dog that cannot squat comfortably will hold it.

Check under the forelegs for a seam that digs in. It is the commonest rub point and it is invisible from above.

Nothing small enough to swallow

Bells, buttons, googly eyes, sequins, feathers, plastic charms. If it can come off it will come off, and a pet that has been mildly irritated for an hour will chew the thing that irritated it.

It comes off in one movement

Before you buy, work out how fast you can get it off. If the answer involves unthreading legs or undoing four fastenings, it is the wrong costume — in a panic you need one pull.

Keep scissors to hand on the night if anything has elastic in it.

Where to try it first

Indoors, quiet, no audience. Not in a pet store aisle, not at the party. A first fitting at home with the family doing nothing special gives you a clean read on whether the animal minds. Everything else is noise on top of the signal you are trying to measure.

⚠️ Never leave a costumed pet unattended, even briefly

This is the rule with no exceptions and it is why costumes hurt animals. A loose strap catches on a crate door, a chair leg, a fence. A trailing cape goes under a paw on the stairs. An animal alone in a costume that has shifted will try to get out of it, and a pet that panics while tangled can strangle, dislocate a leg, or chew and swallow part of the garment. "Just while I answer the door" is exactly how it happens — and on Halloween you will be answering the door all evening. Costume on means you are in the room. Leaving the room means the costume comes off first.

The two weeks before: how to make the costume a non-event

The difference between a pet that wears a costume happily and one that endures it is almost entirely rehearsal. A costume worn for the first time on Halloween night — with the doorbell going, children shrieking and the house full of smells — is three stressors stacked on top of each other. Spread over two weeks, it is one small thing the pet has already decided is boring.

Ten minutes a day, and stop every session while the pet is still relaxed rather than when it has had enough. The order matters more than the total time.

  1. Days 1–2: the costume exists. Leave it on the floor where the pet sleeps. Put a treat on it. That is the entire exercise — you are making it furniture, not clothing.
  2. Days 3–4: it touches them. Drape it over the back for three seconds, treat, remove. Repeat five times. Three seconds, not thirty. You are aiming for the pet to look faintly disappointed when it comes off.
  3. Days 5–7: it fastens. Do up one fastening, treat, undo. Then two. Build to fully on for ten seconds. Do not progress on a day the pet freezes — repeat the previous step instead.
  4. Days 8–10: movement in it. Costume on, then walk a few steps for a treat. Sit. Lie down. This is where a bad fit shows up, so watch the gait: a shortened stride or a dog that will not sit means something is pulling.
  5. Days 11–12: duration. Five minutes, then ten, doing normal things. Feed a meal in it. A pet that eats comfortably in a costume has accepted it.
  6. Days 13–14: the dress rehearsal. Full costume, in the room you will be in, with the lights and decorations up. If anything is going to go wrong, this is when you want to find out.
  7. On the night: put it on late. Twenty minutes before the photo, not three hours before. The costume should be the last thing that happens, not the thing the pet has been wearing all evening.

A hat a cat will sit under for one photograph is the realistic ceiling for most cats. Anything with leg holes is a different conversation.

Cats are not small dogs about this

Cats have a much lower tolerance for clothing than dogs, and the reason is physical as well as temperamental. A cat's coat sits over loose skin that moves independently of the body, and that mobility is part of how a cat judges where it is — fabric pinning it down removes information the animal relies on. Add that cats were not domesticated as working animals wearing gear, and the result is an animal that experiences a costume as being held.

The behavior to watch for is the flop. A cat in a costume that lies down and stops moving is not accepting the outfit; it has concluded that movement is not working. It is the same response as the freeze, and it resolves the moment the costume comes off. Owners share these photos as "she loves it" and the cat in the photo is doing the opposite.

For the great majority of cats the right answer is something at the neck and nothing else: a themed collar, a bow tie, a small bandana. You get the photograph, the cat gets to keep moving normally, and nobody spends the evening watching for distress. A minority of cats — usually ones handled constantly from kittenhood, and often the flatter-faced pedigree breeds who are used to grooming — will tolerate more. You will know which you have within thirty seconds of the towel test.

💚 A bandana is not the consolation prize

It photographs better than a badly fitted costume, it costs a fraction as much, it carries no strangulation or ingestion risk, and the animal behaves normally in it so you get a picture of your actual pet rather than a startled statue. For cats, flat-faced dogs, puppies, seniors and anyone who failed the towel test, it is the correct choice rather than the compromise.

Small pets, birds and reptiles: the short answer is no

Costumes are sold for rabbits and guinea pigs, and occasionally for parrots. We would not put one on any of them, and the reasoning is not squeamishness — it is physiology.

Rabbits and guinea pigs are prey animals with a stress response that can kill them. Both species are prone to gastrointestinal stasis, where the gut slows and stops under stress, and it is a genuine emergency that develops over hours rather than days. Being restrained and dressed is precisely the kind of handling that triggers it. A rabbit also has a spine that fractures easily relative to the power of its own hind legs, and the thrashing that follows being held and wrapped is exactly the movement that does the damage.

Birds should never wear anything. A bird breathes using air sacs that run through its body and require the chest and abdomen to expand freely; anything snug around the torso interferes with breathing in a way it does not in a mammal. Birds also preen compulsively and will work at any fabric until they ingest some of it.

Reptiles cannot regulate their own temperature. A costume on a bearded dragon sits between the animal and its heat source, which is the one thing its body depends on. Tiny hats sold for them photograph well and do nothing good.

If you want a small pet in the Halloween photographs, decorate around them rather than on them. A pumpkin beside the enclosure, a themed hide, a safe seasonal forage mix in the dish. The animal behaves normally, which is what makes a photo worth keeping.

💚 Hay and safe forage beat a costume every time

For a rabbit or guinea pig, a scatter of herbs and a cardboard pumpkin stuffed with hay produces a far better photograph than anything you can dress them in, because the animal is doing something rather than enduring something. It also gets them eating, which for a species that must keep its gut moving is the opposite of a stress response.

Halloween night: the hazards that are not the costume

The outfit is the visible risk and the small one. Emergency clinics are busy on the night and busier on November 1, and the costume is rarely why. Four things do the damage, and all four are manageable if you decide about them before the doorbell starts.

Candy. Chocolate is the famous one — dark and baking chocolate are the worst, and a small dog needs surprisingly little. The bigger modern danger is xylitol, listed as birch sugar on a lot of packaging, which causes a crash in blood sugar and liver failure in dogs at doses found in a few pieces of sugar-free gum. Raisins and grapes cause kidney injury at unpredictable doses. Wrappers and lollipop sticks cause obstructions that need surgery. A bowl of candy at dog height is the single most dangerous object in the house that night.

The front door. It will be open more times in four hours than in the previous month, with a stranger behind it each time. This is the night pets get out. Put the animal in a closed room away from the door before the first knock, with the television on and something to chew. Check the microchip details are current now rather than on November 1, and make sure the collar tag is readable.

Decorations. Glow sticks are the classic Halloween call: the liquid tastes vile rather than being seriously toxic, and the drooling and frantic behavior that follow frighten owners badly. Real candles inside pumpkins and a wagging tail are a genuine fire risk. Fake cobweb is an ingestion hazard, and small animals and birds can become entangled in it. Electrical cords for lights are a chewing temptation at exactly the wrong time of year.

The noise and the costumes on people. A child in a mask does not read as a child to a dog. Even sociable, well-socialized animals misread people in costume, and a dog that has never growled at a child can be badly startled by one in a ghoul mask at face height. Do not take the dog trick-or-treating to test this.

⚠️ Call a vet tonight if any of this happens

Any amount of xylitol or birch sugar — this is an emergency before symptoms appear, not after. Chocolate, with the wrapper if you have it so the clinic can calculate the dose. Grapes or raisins, including anything baked into a treat. Repeated vomiting, tremors, collapse or seizures. Unproductive retching with a swollen, hard abdomen — that is possible bloat and it is minutes-matter urgent in deep-chested dogs. Any swallowed wrapper, stick or costume part. Program your clinic's out-of-hours number into your phone before the evening starts; looking it up at 10pm with a distressed animal is the worst possible time.

What every veterinary behaviorist wishes owners knew

Reading your pet: comfortable, or putting up with it

Animals are specific about this if you know what to look at, and the signals are easy to miss because several of them look like calm. Check thirty seconds after the costume goes on, and again after five minutes — a pet that was fine initially can tip over once the novelty wears off.

💚 Comfortable

  • Moves at a normal pace, normal stride length
  • Sits, lies down and turns around without hesitating
  • Loose body, tail carried as usual
  • Takes treats readily and eats normally
  • Initiates play or comes to you unprompted
  • Ears in their usual position, mouth soft
  • Ignores the costume rather than attending to it

⚠️ Take it off now

  • Freezing, or refusing to move at all — the most misread signal there is
  • Panting that is not from heat or exercise
  • Whale eye — the whites showing at the side
  • Ears flat back, tail tucked, body lowered
  • Lip licking, repeated yawning, turning the head away
  • Trying to back out of it, or chewing at a strap
  • Walking low and slow, or lifting paws oddly
  • Refusing a treat they would normally take
  • Hiding, or any attempt to leave the room

⚠️ Products to leave on the shelf

Anything with a mask or a hood that covers the eyes — a dog that cannot see cannot read other dogs, on the one night everyone is out. Costumes with bells, buttons or googly eyes — they come off and get swallowed, and that is surgery. Rubber bands or hair ties to hold anything in place — they disappear into fur and cut off circulation. Dyes and sprays sold as pet-safe fur color — human product on an animal that grooms itself. Anything with a drawstring at the neck. Glow sticks and glow necklaces as collars — the casing cracks, and a chewed one means a frantic, drooling dog at 9pm. Costumes for pets who failed the towel test — no amount of money fixes that one.

More fun facts to win an argument with

Fun fact

Americans spend around $700 million a year on pet Halloween costumes, and the pumpkin has been the most popular design for over a decade — ahead of the hot dog, the bat and, with grim inevitability, the spider.

Fun fact

Dogs cannot sweat through their skin the way we do. They have sweat glands only in their paw pads, and lose nearly all their heat by panting — which is why a costume that covers the chest and belly matters far more than it would on a person, and why twenty minutes is a sensible ceiling indoors.

Fun fact

The superstition about black cats and Halloween has a real modern consequence: many shelters report that black cats and black dogs wait longer for adoption than any other color, an effect known in rescue as "black dog syndrome". Some shelters once paused black cat adoptions in late October, though most have dropped the policy as the fear proved largely unfounded.

Fun fact

A dog's startle response to a person in a mask is not stupidity — it is face recognition working correctly. Dogs read human faces using the eyes and the mouth together, and a mask removes the mouth while leaving the eyes, producing something that scans as human-shaped and wrong at the same time.

Fun fact

Chocolate is toxic to dogs because of theobromine, which humans clear in a few hours and dogs take more than 17 hours to halve. It is the same compound that makes chocolate dangerous to cats, horses and parrots — we are the unusual ones for being able to eat it at all.

Frequently asked questions

How do I measure my dog for a costume?

Three measurements with a soft tape, dog standing: chest girth at the deepest point just behind the front legs, back length from the base of the neck to the base of the tail, and neck where the collar sits. Chest girth is the one that decides the size. Buy up when you are between sizes.

Is it cruel to put a costume on a dog?

Not inherently — it depends entirely on the dog and the fit. A dog that already wears a harness or coat, in a costume that does not restrict movement, breathing or vision, worn briefly and supervised, is fine. A dog that freezes, pants or tries to back out of it is telling you no, and the answer then is a bandana rather than persistence.

Why does my cat lie down and stop moving in a costume?

That is a shutdown response, not acceptance. Cats rely on freely moving skin to judge where their body is, and fabric pinning it removes that information — many cats respond by going still because movement is not solving the problem. Take it off. For most cats a themed collar or bandana is the realistic limit.

How long can a pet safely wear a costume?

Twenty minutes indoors is a sensible ceiling, and only while you are in the room. Dogs lose heat almost entirely by panting and a costume traps it against the chest and belly. Flat-faced breeds — pugs, bulldogs, Persians — should wear one for the photo and little more.

My dog's costume arrived too tight. Can I just leave it undone?

No — a costume that does not fasten shifts, and a shifting costume is what catches on things and frightens the animal. Return it and size up on chest girth. If exchanging is not possible in time, use a bandana this year. A badly fitted costume is how a pet learns to dread the sight of one.

What if my dog chews at the costume?

Take it off and check the fit before you do anything else. Chewing almost always means something is rubbing, usually a seam under the forelegs or a strap pulling at the throat. If the fit is genuinely fine and the dog still chews, the costume is simply not for that dog — and a swallowed strap or button is an obstruction, which is surgery.

Should I take my dog trick-or-treating?

For most dogs, no. Crowds, costumes on strangers, masks at face height, children running, dropped candy on every sidewalk and doorbells for hours is a lot to ask, and it is the night dogs most often bolt. If your dog is genuinely bombproof in crowds, keep them on a short fixed leash — never retractable — and leave the moment they start scanning for exits.

How much chocolate is actually dangerous?

It depends on the type and the dog's weight, and dark and baking chocolate are far worse than milk. Rather than calculating, call your vet with the wrapper to hand so they can work out the dose. Do not wait for symptoms, and be aware that xylitol — often labeled birch sugar — is a more urgent emergency than chocolate at much smaller amounts.

My pet ate a glow stick. Is that an emergency?

Usually not a poisoning, but it looks alarming. The liquid is bitter rather than seriously toxic, and the drooling, pawing at the mouth and frantic behavior are a reaction to the taste. Offer water or a little food to clear the mouth and call your vet for advice. The real risk is the plastic casing, which can cause an obstruction.

What is the best costume for a pet that hates costumes?

A themed bandana, a collar or a bow tie. It photographs better than a badly fitted outfit, costs a few dollars, carries no strangulation or ingestion risk, and the animal behaves normally — so the picture is of your pet rather than a startled statue. For cats, flat-faced breeds, puppies and seniors this is the right answer, not a compromise.

📏 Six Rules That Matter More Than the Chart

💚 Get these right and most compatibility problems never appear

1. Tank size fixes aggression. Most "incompatible" pairings are really space problems. The same two fish that fight in a 20 gallon often coexist in a 55 because there's room to avoid each other.

2. Buy schooling fish in proper groups. Six is the minimum, eight to ten is better. Under-schooled fish are stressed fish, and stressed fish nip, hide and get sick. A small group of tiger barbs is far nastier than a large one.

3. Match temperature ranges, not just temperament. Two peaceful species that want 68°F and 82°F will never both thrive. Check the overlap before you buy.

4. Match water hardness too. Mollies and African cichlids want hard, alkaline water; cardinal tetras, rams and most shrimp prefer soft and acidic. This quietly kills more fish than aggression does.

5. Fill the levels, don't crowd one. A good community uses the whole tank — top swimmers, mid-water schoolers, bottom dwellers. Six species that all occupy mid-water will feel far more crowded than the same number spread across all three.

6. If it fits in the mouth, it's food. Not aggression — just biology. Always compare adult sizes, never the sizes in the shop tank.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I keep a betta with other fish or not?

Often yes — in a 10 gallon or larger, heavily planted, with peaceful bottom dwellers like corydoras and kuhli loaches, or calm mid-water fish like harlequin rasbora and ember tetras. But temperament varies enormously between individual bettas, so always have a divider or spare tank ready. Never with guppies, gouramis or fin-nippers.

Why did my "peaceful" fish suddenly turn aggressive?

Usually one of four things: it matured and became territorial, it's breeding and defending a nest, the group is too small so the pecking order has nowhere to disperse, or the tank is too crowded for anyone to hold territory peacefully. Adding hiding places and breaking up sight lines with plants and decor fixes a surprising amount.

Will my fish eat my shrimp?

Adult shrimp survive alongside most peaceful community fish. Shrimplets get eaten by almost anything with a mouth. If you want a breeding colony, either keep shrimp alone or plant heavily with java moss so the babies have somewhere to hide. Never keep shrimp with cichlids, loaches, crayfish or large-mouthed fish.

How many fish can I actually put in my tank?

Ignore the inch-per-gallon rule — it's meaningless. Work from adult sizes, research each species' minimum tank, add stock slowly in small batches, and let your nitrate readings be the judge: if you can't hold nitrate under 40 ppm with a weekly 25% water change, you're overstocked regardless of what any rule says.

Can I add new fish to an established tank?

Yes, but slowly — a few at a time with two to three weeks between additions, so the bacteria colony can grow to match. Quarantine new arrivals in a separate tank for two to four weeks if you can; almost every disease outbreak in an established aquarium arrives with new fish. Rearranging the decor when you add fish also helps disrupt existing territories.

What's the most beginner-proof community you can build?

A 20 gallon long with harlequin rasbora, corydoras and a honey gourami. Everything in it is hardy, peaceful, wants the same temperature and water, and occupies a different level of the tank. Add a few amano shrimp and nerite snails and you have a genuinely low-drama aquarium.

The Bottom Line

Compatibility comes down to three questions, asked before you buy rather than after: will it fit in anyone's mouth when fully grown, does it want the same water and temperature, and does it have enough space and company of its own kind? Get those right and the chart above will rarely surprise you.

And when a shop assistant tells you a fish "stays small" or "grows to fit the tank" — check for yourself. That single habit will prevent most of the mistakes in this guide.

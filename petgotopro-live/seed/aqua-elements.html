🧬 Meet Each Element: What It Is and What It Does to Your Fish

Every one of these has a chemical name that looks intimidating on a test kit. Here's what each actually is, where it comes from, and — the part that matters — exactly what it does to a fish.

1. Ammonia (NH₃ and NH₄⁺) — the silent killer

What it is: nitrogen waste, produced constantly and unavoidably. Fish excrete most of it directly through their gills as they breathe; the rest comes from droppings, uneaten food, dying plant leaves and any creature that has died unnoticed behind the decor.

The bit almost nobody explains: ammonia exists in two forms that swap back and forth. NH₃ (free ammonia) is the genuinely dangerous one. NH₄⁺ (ammonium) is far gentler. Which one you have depends on your water: warm water and high pH push it towards deadly NH₃; cool, acidic water keeps it as harmless NH₄⁺. Your test kit shows both added together, which is why the same "0.5 ppm" reading can be mildly concerning in a pH 6.5 tank and an emergency in a pH 8.2 tank.

What it does to fish: it chemically burns gill tissue. Damaged gills can't absorb oxygen properly, so the fish slowly suffocates in perfectly oxygenated water. It also damages the skin, fins and internal organs, and suppresses the immune system, which is why ammonia problems so often turn into fin rot and fungal outbreaks a fortnight later.

⚠️ Signs of ammonia poisoning

Gasping at the surface · red, purple or inflamed gills · lying on the bottom · clamped fins · refusing food · darting and erratic swimming · red streaks in the fins · cloudy eyes.

Target: 0 ppm, always. There is no safe level — even 0.25 ppm causes damage over time. If you can detect it, act today.

2. Nitrite (NO₂⁻) — the invisible suffocation

What it is: what stage-one bacteria produce after eating ammonia. It's a sign your cycle has started — but it's every bit as lethal as what it replaced.

What it does to fish: nitrite crosses the gills into the bloodstream and binds to haemoglobin, converting it into methaemoglobin, which cannot carry oxygen at all. The blood literally turns brown — which is why fishkeepers call it brown blood disease. The fish is being asphyxiated from the inside, and adding an airstone won't help because the problem isn't the water's oxygen, it's the blood's ability to carry it.

⚠️ Signs of nitrite poisoning

Gasping at the surface even beside an airstone · rapid gill movement · brown or tan gills rather than healthy red · listlessness and hanging in one spot · loss of appetite.

Target: 0 ppm, always. Above 0.25 ppm is dangerous. Water changes are the fix; a small amount of aquarium salt can help block nitrite uptake at the gills in an emergency, but check it's safe for your species first — many catfish and loaches dislike salt.

3. Nitrate (NO₃⁻) — the slow one

What it is: the end product of a working cycle, made by stage-two bacteria eating nitrite. Finding nitrate in your tank is good news — it's the proof your bacteria are doing their job.

What it does to fish: far less than the other two. Fish tolerate moderate nitrate indefinitely. The problem is chronic rather than acute: sustained high levels cause stunted growth, faded colours, weakened immunity, poor breeding and general listlessness, and they feed algae blooms that turn the tank green. Some sensitive species — shrimp, discus, and fry of any species — are considerably fussier than hardy community fish.

Target: under 40 ppm, ideally under 20. Nothing in a normal tank removes it, so it climbs steadily until you change water. Live plants consume some, which is one of the best arguments for a planted tank.

4. The bacteria — your actual filter

What they are: two different groups of microscopic organisms that arrive on their own from the air, your hands, plants and fish, then multiply to match the food supply.

  • Nitrosomonas and friends — eat ammonia, produce nitrite. These establish first, usually within one to two weeks.
  • Nitrospira and friends — eat nitrite, produce nitrate. These are slower to establish, which is why the nitrite stage feels like it lasts forever.

Where they live: overwhelmingly inside your filter media, because that's where oxygen-rich water flows past them constantly. They also coat the substrate, glass and decor. This is why filter maintenance is the most dangerous routine job in the hobby — and why "the filter is the tank's biological engine, not just a water pump" is the single most useful thing a beginner can understand.

What kills them: chlorine and chloramine from untreated tap water, going without oxygenated flow for more than a few hours, most antibiotic medications, and simply running out of ammonia to eat.

🧴 What a Starter Treatment Kit Actually Does

Walk into any aquatics shop and you'll be sold a "starter kit" of bottles. They're genuinely useful — but only if you know what each one is for, because they do completely different jobs and none of them is magic.

The two bottles that matter

1. Water conditioner (dechlorinator). Tap water contains chlorine or chloramine to keep it safe for humans — and both are lethal to fish and to the bacteria you're trying to grow. Conditioner neutralises them instantly. This one is genuinely non-negotiable and you'll use it forever, at every water change. Better conditioners like Seachem Prime also temporarily bind ammonia and nitrite into a form fish can tolerate for around 24–48 hours — a real lifesaver during a fish-in cycle, because it buys your fish time without starving your bacteria.

2. Live nitrifying bacteria. A bottle of the actual living bacteria your filter needs. Without it, you're waiting for the right microbes to drift in from the air and multiply from almost nothing — which works, but slowly. Pouring in a concentrated culture skips the "waiting to be colonised" phase entirely.

How bottled bacteria genuinely help

Think of your filter as an empty field. Left alone, a few seeds blow in on the wind and eventually the field fills — that's four to eight weeks. A bottle of bacteria is a bag of seed sown directly into the soil: the field still needs time to grow, but you've skipped the waiting and started with far more.

In practice, a good starter culture cuts cycling from roughly 6–8 weeks down to 2–4 weeks, and it makes the process far more reliable. It also gives you a safety net: added after a filter clean, a medication course or a big new stocking, it tops the colony back up before ammonia has a chance to spike.

⚠️ Be realistic about "instant cycle" claims

Several bottles promise you can add fish the same day. Sometimes that works. Often it doesn't — because these are live cultures, and a bottle that has been sitting in a hot warehouse or on a sunlit shelf for a year may contain very little that's still alive. You have no way to check.

Our honest advice: use bottled bacteria to speed up a fishless cycle, not to skip it. If you do add fish early, add one or two hardy ones only, test every single day, and be ready to do water changes. Buy from a shop with good stock turnover, check the date, and refrigerate the ones that call for it.

What else turns up in starter kits

  • Ammonia source (better kits) — controlled ammonium chloride to feed the bacteria during a fishless cycle. Essential if you're doing it properly.
  • Slime coat protectant — aloe-based conditioners that help fish repair damaged skin and gills. Genuinely useful after an ammonia incident, optional otherwise.
  • Test strips — often included, and the weakest item in any kit. Replace them with a liquid test kit.
  • "Ammonia remover" pads or resins — avoid these during cycling. They strip out the ammonia your bacteria need to eat, so the colony never grows and the cycle stalls indefinitely.

A bearded dragon is the friendliest lizard you can keep — and the one most often kept wrong. 

Almost every sick beardie we see traces back to three things: a UVB bulb that isn't doing its job, a basking spot too cool to digest food, and an adult still being fed like a baby.

The frustrating part is that most of the bad advice comes pre-packaged. Starter kits routinely ship with a tank a quarter of the right size, a coil UVB bulb that produces almost nothing usable, and a heat mat that does nothing for an animal which basks with its back. This guide sticks to what the equipment actually needs to do, gives you the numbers to check it against, corrects the two myths that cause the most harm, and covers the kit worth having before you need it.

☀️ Lighting & Heat: The Part Everyone Gets Wrong

If you remember one thing from this guide, make it this: a bearded dragon needs a sun you have to buy, and it is the most expensive part of the setup. Everything else is comparatively forgiving.

Two separate jobs are happening. Heat lets them digest — a dragon below about 95°F cannot process the food in its gut, and undigested food is how impaction starts. UVB lets them synthesise vitamin D3, which lets them absorb calcium. Without it they develop metabolic bone disease, and advanced MBD is permanent.

The temperatures, and how to actually measure them

Surface temperature and air temperature are different numbers, and it is the surface that matters. Point an infrared temperature gun at the basking platform itself. Digital probes handle the ambient air at each end.

⚠️ Bin the stick-on dial thermometer

The analogue dials that come in starter kits are routinely wrong by 10–20 degrees, and they only read the air touching the glass they're stuck to. You need an infrared temp gun for the basking surface and digital probes for the air at each end. Together they cost about $30 and they are the difference between guessing and knowing.

Run the bulb through a dimming thermostat, probe secured at basking height. Without one, your basking temperature moves every time the room does.

  • Adjust by raising or lowering the platform rather than constantly swapping bulb wattages.
  • No colored night bulbs. Red and blue lamps disturb sleep — dragons need full darkness, and a drop to room temperature does them good.
  • No heat rocks, ever. They cause contact burns and there is no safe version.
  • Heat mats are not a primary heat source. Dragons absorb heat through their backs and don't reliably sense belly heat, which is exactly how mat burns happen.

📦 The Enclosure

REPTI ZOO Reptile Tempered Glass Extra-Long Terrarium 

The floor for an adult is 4 × 2 × 2 ft. That is a minimum, not a target — five or six feet of length is meaningfully better, because a longer enclosure makes a better thermal gradient, and the gradient is the entire point.

You can raise a hatchling in the adult enclosure from day one, which saves buying twice. Clutter it heavily with hides, branches and foliage so a small dragon can cross it feeling covered, and hand-feed at first rather than making it hunt across four feet of open ground.

Substrate

  • Slate or ceramic tile — the best all-round answer. Impossible to swallow, holds basking heat so the whole surface radiates, wipes clean, and files their nails as they walk. Most tile shops will cut it to size free.
  • Bioactive soil mix — roughly 60% topsoil, 30% play sand, 10% clay, with springtails and isopods. Lets them dig naturally. More work to establish and needs correct temperatures to stay dry.
  • Paper towel — ideal for quarantine, hatchlings and sick animals. Cheap, hygienic, dull.
  • Reptile carpet — avoid. The loops catch claws and cost toes.
  • Calci-sand and crushed walnut shell — never. Marketed as digestible; they are not, and they are a well-known cause of fatal impaction.

Furnishing it

  • Two hides — one warm, one cool, so they never choose between temperature and security.
  • Climbing branches, wedged securely. Bearded dragons are semi-arboreal and a flat enclosure wastes half the space. Falls break bones.
  • A basking platform wide enough for the whole animal and stable under half a kilo of lizard. It sets the distance to both lamps, so it sets your temperatures and your UVI.
  • A dig box of slightly damp sand and soil, especially for females.
  • Shallow food and water dishes, heavy enough not to tip.

⚠️ The starter kit problem

Bearded dragon "starter kits" almost universally include a 20–40 gallon tank, a coil UVB bulb and a heat mat. All three are wrong: the tank is a quarter of the space an adult needs, coil bulbs produce far too little usable UVB, and belly heat does nothing for an animal that basks with its back. Budget for the real setup, or wait until you can.

🍽️ Diet: The Ratio That Flips

Bearded dragons are omnivores, and the single most-missed fact in the hobby is that their diet inverts as they grow. Hatchlings are growing fast and eat mostly insects. Adults are not growing at all, and an adult still eating a juvenile's insect load becomes obese and develops fatty liver disease. By eighteen months the salad is the main meal.

💚 The eye-spacing rule

Never feed an insect wider than the space between your dragon's eyes. Anything larger is a choking and impaction risk, and in young dragons oversized prey has been linked to hind-leg paralysis from pressure on the spine.

✅ Insects worth feeding

  • Dubia roaches — the best staple there is. Excellent meat-to-shell ratio, soft-bodied, can't climb smooth surfaces, don't chirp or smell, and live for months. Check local rules — a few places restrict them.
  • Black soldier fly larvae (Calciworms) — naturally high in calcium with a favorable calcium-to-phosphorus ratio, so many keepers don't dust them at all.
  • Crickets — nutritious and available everywhere. Noisy, smelly, and they escape. Never leave uneaten crickets in overnight; they bite a sleeping dragon.
  • Silkworms — high protein, high moisture, soft. Genuinely excellent, just expensive.
  • Hornworms — about 85% water, so the go-to for a dehydrated or constipated dragon. Too low in protein to be a staple.

⚖️ Occasional only

  • Superworms — fatty with a tough exoskeleton. Adults only, as a treat.
  • Mealworms — mostly chitin, little nutrition, and a real impaction risk in small dragons. There is always a better option.
  • Waxworms and butterworms — very fatty and reportedly addictive. Dragons hooked on them refuse better food.

🥬 Greens: the adult staple

The salad should be roughly 80–90% leafy greens with the rest other vegetables. Chop small, serve fresh each morning, and rotate rather than repeat.

  • Daily staples: collard greens, mustard greens, turnip greens, dandelion greens, escarole, endive, watercress
  • Good regulars: butternut and acorn squash, bell pepper, green beans, snap peas, prickly pear pad
  • Small amounts, rotated: kale and broccoli — fine occasionally, not daily

⚠️ Never feed these

Lethal: fireflies and any glowing insect. They contain lucibufagins, cardiac toxins, and a single firefly can kill a bearded dragon.

Toxic: avocado, rhubarb, onion, garlic, chives.

Wrong for them: spinach and beet greens (oxalates bind the calcium you're working to provide), iceberg lettuce (water with no nutrition), citrus, and any insect caught in the garden — pesticide residue and parasites.

💊 Supplements: Calcium Is Not Optional

Unlike small herbivores, bearded dragons genuinely need dusting. Feeder insects are naturally low in calcium and high in phosphorus, which is exactly backwards for a lizard.

⚠️ More is not better with D3 and vitamin A

Both are fat-soluble and accumulate in the body, so over-supplementing causes real harm. With genuinely good UVB, dragons make their own D3. Follow a schedule rather than dusting everything with everything, and don't stack the D3 and the multivitamin on the same feed.

🔍 Two Myths Worth Correcting

Two pieces of bad advice cause more harm than everything else combined, and both are still sold in shops.

💚 Myth 1: "A compact coil UVB bulb is fine"

It is not, and this is the single most common cause of metabolic bone disease in pet bearded dragons. Coil bulbs produce a small amount of UVB over a tiny area — a dragon has to sit directly beneath one to get anything at all, and older designs were linked to eye irritation. A desert species needs a linear T5 HO tube running roughly two thirds of the enclosure, mounted inside, so there is a UV gradient to move through. If your kit came with a coil bulb, replace it before anything else.

💚 Myth 2: "Feed insects every day"

True for a hatchling, actively harmful for an adult. Care sheets that give one feeding schedule for the whole life of the animal are the reason so many pet bearded dragons are obese with fatty liver disease. Insects are roughly 70% of a juvenile's intake and around 20% of an adult's, offered only two to four times a week. Past eighteen months, the daily meal is the salad.

🩺 Warning Signs Worth Knowing

Bearded dragons hide illness well, and most of what goes wrong is husbandry wearing a medical name.

  • Swollen, soft or rubbery lower jaw; bowed legs; tremors — metabolic bone disease. Vet urgently, then fix the UVB.
  • No stool for over a week, straining, bloating, dragging hind legs — impaction. Check the basking temperature first; it is usually the cause.
  • Mucus or bubbles at the mouth and nose, open-mouth breathing away from the basking spot — respiratory infection. Needs a vet and antibiotics.
  • Runny foul stool, weight loss despite eating — parasites. A fecal test settles it.
  • Yellow or grey crusty patches under the scales — yellow fungus. Serious, contagious, vet immediately.
  • Stuck shed as a tight ring on toes or tail tip — humidity too low. Act quickly; constricted digits are lost.
  • A beard that stays black for hours or days, away from any obvious threat — check temperatures, then look for illness.

💚 Gaping is normal — mostly

A dragon sitting on its basking spot with its mouth open is thermoregulating, and it's completely normal. Gaping away from the heat, or with labored or clicking breathing, is not. That distinction saves a lot of unnecessary worry and catches the cases that matter.

⚠️ Book a fecal test in the first month

Parasites are extremely common in shop-bought dragons, cheap to treat early and expensive to ignore. Find an exotics vet before you need one, take a fresh stool sample in during month one, and quarantine any new dragon for 30 days away from other reptiles.

🔄 Brumation: Normal, But Check First

Somewhere between autumn and late winter, most adult bearded dragons slow right down — sleeping for days, hiding at the cool end, ignoring food. This is brumation, the reptile version of hibernation, and it is normal in animals over about a year old. It can last weeks or months.

  • Weigh weekly. Steady weight is brumation. Continued loss is illness.
  • Keep offering water and a small salad and keep the lights on a shortened cycle so they can warm up if they choose.
  • Rule out parasites, impaction and a cold basking spot first. A fecal test is the cheap way to be certain.
  • Don't force-feed or repeatedly wake a genuinely brumating dragon.
🎉 Fun Fact

That slow circular wave a bearded dragon does with one front leg isn't a greeting — it's a submission signal. It means "I see you, I'm not a threat," and it's most common in juveniles and females. Head-bobbing is the opposite: a dominance display, usually from males. They're having a conversation you can actually read, which is rare in a reptile.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size tank does a bearded dragon really need?

4 × 2 × 2 ft (about 120 gallons) is the minimum for an adult, and 5 or 6 feet of length is better. The length matters because it creates the thermal gradient — a dragon needs to move between a 100–105°F basking spot and a 75–85°F cool end to regulate its own temperature. You can raise a hatchling in the full-size enclosure from day one; just clutter it heavily so a small animal feels covered.

Is a coil UVB bulb really that bad?

Yes. It's the single most common cause of metabolic bone disease in pet bearded dragons. Coil bulbs produce a small amount of UVB over a tiny area, so a dragon has to sit directly beneath one to get anything. You need a linear T5 HO tube, 10.0 or 12% desert grade, running about two thirds of the enclosure at the warm end and mounted inside rather than over mesh.

How often do I replace the UVB tube?

Every 12 months, even though it still lights up perfectly. UVB output decays long before the tube stops emitting visible light, so a two-year-old bulb can look identical while producing almost nothing. Write the install date on the glass with a marker and set a calendar reminder.

Why has my bearded dragon stopped eating?

Work through it in order: basking temperature too low (they can't digest below about 95°F), impaction, parasites, then brumation. Season is a strong clue — a refusal in autumn or winter from an otherwise healthy adult is usually brumation. Weigh weekly: steady weight supports brumation, ongoing loss means see a vet. A newly-arrived dragon also commonly refuses food for a week or two while it settles.

Why is my bearded dragon's beard black?

A dark beard is a stress or threat display, and a brief one is completely normal — a new environment, a reflection, a bath or breeding season will all do it. Duration is what matters. A beard that stays black for hours or days, especially alongside hiding, appetite loss or open-mouth breathing, means something is wrong. Check your temperatures first, then look for illness.

Can two bearded dragons live together?

No. They're solitary and territorial. The dominant animal monopolises the basking spot and the subordinate slowly develops metabolic bone disease from missing out on UVB — frequently with no visible fighting to warn you. Two males will fight outright, and a mixed pair will breed constantly. One dragon per enclosure.

What substrate is safest?

Slate or ceramic tile. It's impossible to swallow, holds basking heat so the whole surface radiates warmth, wipes clean and files their nails as they walk — and most tile shops will cut it to your dimensions free. Bioactive soil is excellent but it's a project. Never use calci-sand or crushed walnut shell: both are sold as substrate and both cause fatal impaction.

Do bearded dragons carry Salmonella?

Commonly yes, usually with no sign of illness in the animal. Wash your hands thoroughly before and after every handling session, keep dragons and their equipment out of kitchens and away from food-preparation surfaces, don't bathe them in the kitchen sink, and supervise children closely. Households with infants, elderly or immunocompromised people should be especially careful.

The Bottom Line


Bearded dragon care comes down to four numbers: 
  • 4 × 2 × 2 ft enclosure
  • 100–105°F basking surface
  • Linear T5 HO UVB tube at UVI 4–6 replaced every 12 months
  • A diet that goes from mostly insects to mostly greens by 18 months. 
Get those right and almost nothing else can go badly wrong.

Then do the thing most owners skip: buy the infrared temp gun. It costs less than a bag of feeders, it takes five seconds to use, and it's the only way you'll ever know that the expensive lamp above your dragon is still doing its job.
